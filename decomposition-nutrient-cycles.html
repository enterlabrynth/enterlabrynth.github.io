MODULE 2.4 · PART 1

Matter cycles, and decomposition closes the loop

THE QUESTION Every living thing eventually dies. What turns a dead leaf back into something a plant can use, and what decides how fast that happens?

Seven steps: why matter cycles, what really moves when something is eaten, the law that makes it possible, what decomposition does, what sets its speed, why some dead matter lasts longer, and the animals that shred it.

STEP 1 · START HERE

Energy flows. Matter cycles.

Module 2.3 ended with energy leaving every food web as heat. Matter is different. The atoms an organism is made of are never used up. They are passed on, taken apart, and built into something new.

DIAGRAM · A LINE AND A LOOP

"Matter" means atoms: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the other elements living things are built from. They cycle between organisms and the abiotic environment: the soil, the water and the air.

THREE WORDS FOR NUTRIENTS

Essential, macro, limiting

ESSENTIAL NUTRIENTAn element required for growth, reproduction or metabolic function.
MACRONUTRIENTNeeded in large quantities. Macronutrients are the building blocks of proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates and phospholipids.
LIMITING NUTRIENTOne that limits the growth of primary producers when it is in short supply, which it often is. Nitrogen and phosphorus are limiting in some ecosystems.
COMMON MIX-UP · MACRO DOES NOT MEAN A BIG MOLECULE "Macro" is about how much an organism needs, not about the size of anything. Nitrogen is a macronutrient because living things need a lot of it. The word for a large molecule is macromolecule. The two are linked, which is why they get mixed up: macronutrients are what macromolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids are built from.

THE NAME FOR THE LOOP

What the word means

A biogeochemical cycle is the path an element takes as it moves from one pool to another within an ecosystem.

BIOSome of the pools are living things.
GEOSome are the non-living Earth: air, soil, water, rock.
CHEMICALThe element is built into different molecules as it moves.

This module follows two elements: nitrogen and phosphorus. Carbon is Module 2.5.

Check yourself: when nitrogen moves from the soil into a plant, is that the bio part, the geo part, or the chemical part?

All three at once. The atom leaves a geo pool (soil), enters a bio pool (the plant), and changes molecule on the way (from ammonium or nitrate into a protein). That is why it is one word.

The leaf contains protein. Does that same protein molecule end up in the giraffe? No. The molecule is taken apart. Its atoms are what move.

STEP 3 · THE LAW BEHIND IT

Atoms are never used up: conservation of mass

Why can matter cycle at all? Because of one law. Matter is not created or destroyed. In living things and in ecosystems, atoms are only rearranged into new molecules and passed on.

THE LAW OF CONSERVATION OF MASS

What it says, and what follows from it

THE LAWAtoms are not created and not destroyed by eating, digestion, growth, death or decomposition.
SOEvery atom in a living thing came from somewhere, and when the organism dies every one of them goes somewhere.
WHICH MEANSThe atoms on Earth are reused. That is what "matter cycles" means.
MATTER AND ENERGY ARE BOTH CONSERVED

So why does only matter cycle?

MATTERStays on Earth as the same atoms, so it can be used again and again: a loop.
ENERGYIs conserved too, but it ends up as heat, which organisms cannot reuse and which leaves for space. So it has to keep arriving from the sun: a one-way flow.
Check yourself: a fallen log slowly rots away until nothing is left. Were its atoms destroyed?

No. Decomposers turned its organic molecules into carbon dioxide and water, which went into the air, and into nutrients that went into the soil. Every atom still exists. It is just somewhere else, in a different molecule.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Conservation of mass

Matter is not created or destroyed. In an ecosystem, atoms are rearranged into new molecules and passed on, never used up.

STEP 4 · WHAT CLOSES THE LOOP

Decomposition: organic molecules in, inorganic nutrients out

Producers can only take up nutrients in simple inorganic forms. Everything that dies is made of complex organic molecules. Decomposition is the conversion of one back into the other. Hold on to this one sentence before meeting any decomposer by name.

Producers take up all three and build them back into organic molecules. The loop is closed.

ENERGY DOES NOT COME BACK Decomposers recycle the atoms in detritus. They do not recycle its energy. They use that energy to live, and it leaves as heat, just as it does at every other level of a food web.

Decomposers move nutrients from the detrital pool back to the abiotic environment, where producers can use them. The next three steps look at how fast this goes and who does it.

WHY IT MATTERS

Without decomposers, everything stops

Nutrients would stay locked inside dead bodies and waste. Producers would run out of nitrogen and phosphorus to take up, primary production would stop, and so would every food web built on it.

That is why decomposition is behind ecosystem services such as primary production. And it sets the pace: the rate of decomposition limits how fast nutrients move through an ecosystem.

Decomposers

Decomposition

The breakdown of detritus into inorganic forms that producers can use again.

Ecosystem services

Benefits that ecosystems provide, such as primary production. They depend on nutrients being recycled.

WHAT "QUALITY" MEANS HERE

Quality is from the decomposer's point of view

The quality of detritus is its chemistry: what it is made of, and so how easy it is for decomposers to break down and how much they get out of it.

HIGH-QUALITY DETRITUSRich in nitrogen, low in lignin. Soft tissue such as green leaves. Decomposes quickly, so its nutrients are back in circulation soon.
LOW-QUALITY DETRITUSPoor in nitrogen, high in lignin and other tough structural material. Wood, bark, needles. Decomposes slowly, so its nutrients stay locked up for longer.
THINK OF YOUR OWN FOOD Sugar is digested quickly. A bowl of whole grains, which is complex carbohydrate and fibre, takes much longer and keeps you going for longer. Detritus works the same way: the more complex and tough the molecules, the longer it lasts.
WATCH THE WORD In everyday talk the slow, complex food is the "healthy" one. In ecology the word runs the other way: high quality means it breaks down fast, and low quality means it breaks down slowly.
Check yourself: two forests are equally warm and wet. One drops soft leaves, the other drops tough needles and a lot of wood. In which do nutrients cycle faster?

The one with soft leaves. Its detritus is higher quality, so decomposition is faster, and the rate of decomposition is what limits how fast nutrients move through an ecosystem.

THE PROBLEM · A TRIPLE BONDWhy the biggest pool is out of reachIn N₂ gas the two nitrogen atoms share three bonds. A triple bond is extremely stable, so it takes a great deal of energy to pull the atoms apart. Plants, animals and fungi have no way of doing it. That is why air that is 78% nitrogen cannot feed a plant.
NITROGEN FIXATIONBreak the bond, add hydrogensNitrogen-fixing bacteria and archaea have an enzyme, nitrogenase, that can break the triple bond. As the atoms come apart, hydrogens are added to each one, giving two molecules of ammonia, NH₃.Where the hydrogen comes from: hydrogen ions (H⁺) that are always present in the watery inside of the cell, plus electrons (e⁻) that the bacterium takes from its food. An H⁺ and an electron together make one hydrogen atom. The reaction is costly: the cell spends a lot of ATP on it.
AMMONIA BECOMES AMMONIUMOne more hydrogen, from waterAmmonia dissolves in the water in soil, lakes and the sea, and picks up a hydrogen ion from the water. It is now ammonium, NH₄⁺. So NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are the same nitrogen, one hydrogen apart, and in wet places you mostly find ammonium.
NITRIFICATIONAmmonium is switched to nitrateNitrifying bacteria and archaea take the hydrogens off the nitrogen and put oxygens on instead: first nitrite (NO₂⁻), then nitrate (NO₃⁻). They do it to get energy, and they use oxygen gas to do it. Overall: NH₄⁺ + 2 O₂ → NO₃⁻ + H₂O + 2 H⁺.
DENITRIFICATIONThe reverse trip, back to N₂Where there is no oxygen gas, such as waterlogged soil, some bacteria use the oxygen in nitrate for their respiration instead. The oxygens are stripped off, the nitrogen atoms pair up again, and N₂ gas escapes to the air. Usable nitrogen has been lost from the soil.

HOW BIG IS THIS FLUX? Big. A hippo eats a great deal of grass every night and releases much of it into the water by day, and a river can hold thousands of hippos. Hippos alone supply a large share of the nitrogen and phosphorus that enter the river. One published estimate is that their dung and urine raise the river's nitrogen and phosphorus by roughly a quarter. It is a flux from the terrestrial pool to the freshwater pool, carried by an animal.
HOW DO YOU MEASURE THAT? Nobody can weigh what a wild hippo leaves in a river. So researchers measured hippos in a zoo pool: how much dung and urine they produce, and how much nitrogen and phosphorus is in it. A clean pool turned cloudy in less than a day. They then scaled those figures up to the number of hippos living along the river.
WHY THE SEASON MATTERS The same dung has opposite effects at different times. In the dry season the river is low and slow, so dung collects in the pools where hippos crowd together, and that is when oxygen can run out. When the rains return, the rising river flushes the pools out. That first rush can carry the oxygen-poor water downstream and kill fish there too, before the river recovers and the cycle starts again.

PUTTING THE MODULE TOGETHER

Why adding a nutrient can remove the oxygen

Every piece of this module is in that chain: a limiting nutrient is added, primary production jumps, the extra production becomes detritus, decomposers break it down, and their respiration uses up the oxygen.

Decomposition is essential, and usually invisible. A dead zone is what it looks like when there is suddenly far too much to decompose.
