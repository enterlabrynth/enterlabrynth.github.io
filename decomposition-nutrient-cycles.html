MODULE 2.4 · PART 1

Decomposition: how matter gets back

THE QUESTION Every living thing eventually dies. What turns a dead leaf, or a dead whale, back into something a plant can use?

Six steps: why matter cycles, what really moves when something is eaten, who the decomposers are, what sets their speed, how fungi work, and what happens on the sea floor.

STEP 1 · START HERE

Energy flows. Matter cycles.

Module 2.3 ended with energy leaving every food web as heat. Matter is different. The atoms an organism is made of are never used up. They are passed on, taken apart, and built into something new.

DIAGRAM · A LINE AND A LOOP

Two different journeys

ENERGY · passes through onceMATTER · goes aroundProducersConsumersheatsunProducersConsumersDetritusNutrients

"Matter" means atoms: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the other elements living things are built from. They cycle between organisms and the abiotic environment: the soil, the water and the air.

THREE WORDS FOR NUTRIENTS

Essential, macro, limiting

ESSENTIAL NUTRIENTAn element required for growth, reproduction or metabolic function.
MACRONUTRIENTNeeded in large quantities. Macronutrients are the building blocks of proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates and phospholipids.
LIMITING NUTRIENTOne that limits the growth of primary producers when it is in short supply, which it often is. Nitrogen and phosphorus are limiting in some ecosystems.
THE NAME FOR THE LOOP

A biogeochemical cycle

The path an element takes as it moves from one pool to another within an ecosystem. This module follows two elements: nitrogen and phosphorus. Carbon is Module 2.5.

STEP 2 · WHAT ACTUALLY MOVES

A giraffe eats a leaf. Where does the protein go?

The leaf contains protein. Does that same protein molecule end up in the giraffe? No. The molecule is taken apart. Its atoms are what move.

DIAGRAM · ONE PROTEIN, FOLLOWED THROUGH A MEAL

PROTEIN IN THE LEAFAMINO ACIDSPROTEIN IN THE GIRAFFEdigestion
chain taken apartbiosynthesis
a new chain built

Each bead is an amino acid, and each one carries nitrogen (N). The giraffe ends up with the leaf's nitrogen atoms, built into giraffe proteins. It is not the same protein that was in the leaf.

THE THREE THINGS THAT HAPPEN

Eat, take apart, rebuild

1Eaten
The leaf's protein enters the giraffe's gut as a large organic molecule.
2Digested
Enzymes break it into its building blocks, amino acids, which are small enough to absorb.
3Rebuilt
The giraffe's cells join those amino acids into its own proteins. Building new organic molecules is biosynthesis.
THE RULE FOR THIS WHOLE MODULE Molecules are built, broken and rebuilt. Atoms are passed on. When you trace a nutrient, trace the atom and name the molecule it is in at each stop.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Organic

Carbon-based molecules built by living things, such as proteins, sugars and nucleic acids.

Inorganic

Not organic. Ammonium, nitrate, phosphate, CO₂ and N₂ gas are inorganic.

Biosynthesis

Building new organic molecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids.

STEP 3 · WHO CLOSES THE LOOP

Decomposers turn dead matter back into nutrients

Producers can only take up nutrients in simple inorganic forms. Everything that dies is made of complex organic molecules. Something has to convert one back into the other.

Decomposition starts with organic molecules and ends with inorganic ones. Decomposers move nutrients from the detrital pool back to the abiotic environment, where producers can use them.

TWO JOBS, TWO NAMES

Detritivore vs. decomposer

DETRITIVORESAnimals that eat detritus, such as earthworms. They break it into smaller pieces, which gives decomposers more surface to work on.
DECOMPOSERSBacteria, archaea, fungi, and a few other eukaryotes. They break complex organic molecules down chemically into inorganic forms.
WHERE THEY SIT ON THE TREE OF LIFE

All three domains

BACTERIAMany decomposers
ARCHAEAMany decomposers
EUKARYAFungi, and a few others
WHY IT MATTERS

Without decomposers, everything stops

Nutrients would stay locked inside dead bodies and waste. Producers would run out of nitrogen and phosphorus to take up, primary production would stop, and so would every food web built on it.

That is why decomposition is behind ecosystem services such as primary production. And it sets the pace: the rate of decomposition limits how fast nutrients move through an ecosystem.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Detritus

Dead matter and waste. Detritus is a pool, not a flux.

Detritivore

An animal that eats detritus and breaks it into smaller pieces.

Decomposers

Bacteria, archaea, fungi and a few other eukaryotes that break complex organic molecules down into inorganic forms.

Decomposition

The breakdown of detritus into inorganic forms that producers can use again.

Ecosystem services

Benefits that ecosystems provide, such as primary production. They depend on nutrients being recycled.

TWO ROLES IN AN ECOSYSTEM

Lock it in · Part 1

  1. Energy flows through once. Matter cycles: the same atoms move between organisms and the abiotic environment.
  2. When something is eaten, its molecules are taken apart and rebuilt. The atoms are what get passed on.
  3. Decomposers (bacteria, archaea, fungi) turn organic molecules in detritus into inorganic forms producers can use. Detritivores are animals that eat and shred detritus.
  4. Decomposition is faster when it is warm, when moisture is moderate, and when the detritus is easy to break down. In water, temperature is the key driver.
  5. Fungi are eukaryotes and chemoheterotrophs. They digest outside the body and absorb, and their cell walls are chitin.
  6. A whale fall carries matter from the surface to the deep sea and feeds a whole community.

Nitrogen and phosphorus: following the atoms

THE QUESTION Air is mostly nitrogen. So why is nitrogen so often the thing plants run short of, and what happens when we give them too much?

Nine steps: what the two nutrients are for, how they move through a food web, pools and fluxes, the nitrogen cycle, who fixes nitrogen, a practice trace, phosphorus, hippos, and dead zones.

STEP 1 · WHY THESE TWO ELEMENTS

What nitrogen and phosphorus are for

Part 1 was about how nutrients get recycled. This part follows two of them. Start with why every organism needs them.

NNEEDED TO BUILDNitrogen

Every amino acid has nitrogen in it, so every protein does. So do DNA and RNA.

PNEEDED TO BUILDPhosphorus

Phospholipids are what cell membranes are built from. Phosphorus is essential for all life forms.

Taking up an inorganic nutrient and building its atoms into organic molecules. Producers assimilate ammonium and nitrate.

STEP 2 · UP THE FOOD CHAIN AND BACK

How N and P move through a food web

The same four moves carry nitrogen and phosphorus around every ecosystem. This is the giraffe and the leaf from Part 1, drawn as a loop.

DIAGRAM · ONE LOOP, FOUR MOVES

Taken up, eaten, shed, released

ProducersConsumersDetritusInorganic nutrientsuptake +
assimilationeatendeath
and wastedecompositionORGANIC · N and P are inside molecules made by living thingsINORGANIC

Follow the arrows clockwise from the bottom left. The atom goes inorganic → organic once, at the producer, and organic → inorganic once, at the decomposers. In between it is only passed along.

THE FOUR MOVES

What happens at each arrow

1Uptake and assimilation
A producer takes up ammonium, nitrate or phosphate and builds the atoms into its own proteins, nucleic acids, ATP and phospholipids.
2Eaten
A consumer eats the producer, digests those molecules, and rebuilds the atoms into its own. The same again at each step up the chain.
3Death and waste
Everything that dies, and everything that is excreted, joins the detritus. The N and P are still in organic molecules.
4Decomposition
Decomposers break those molecules down and release the N and P in inorganic form. Ready for step 1 again.
WHAT "RECYCLED" MEANS Decomposers are the recyclers. To say a nutrient is recycled is to say the same atoms are released from dead matter and taken up by producers again, over and over.
STEP 3 · THE VOCABULARY OF A CYCLE

Pools and fluxes

Every biogeochemical cycle is described with two words. One is for where the element is. The other is for the element moving.

TWO WORDS

A place, and a movement

POOL · A NOUN, A PLACEWhere the element is held: the atmosphere, the soil, the water, living things, detritus. Reservoir and compartment mean the same thing.
FLUX · A VERB, A MOVEMENTThe element moving from one pool to another. Fluxes are the arrows in a cycle diagram, and they are rates: an amount per year.

producer's moleculesconsumer's molecules

detritusammonia (NH₃)
Who does it: microbes: the decomposers.Dead producers, dead consumers and their waste all become detritus. Decomposers break the organic molecules down and return the nitrogen as ammonia. This is where nitrogen goes from organic to inorganic.

STEP 8 · ANIMALS MOVE NUTRIENTS TOO

Hippos: a conveyor belt from land to water

Nutrients do not only cycle on the spot. Animals can carry them from one ecosystem into another. Hippos in the Mara River in Kenya are the classic case.

SCENE · ONE HIPPO, ONE DAY

Eat on land, release in the river

NIGHT · grazes on landDAY · rests in the riverC, N and Pdung and urine
fertilize the watergrass is eaten here

Hippos graze on grassland at night and spend the day in the water, where they release dung and urine. Every day they move carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus from the terrestrial system into the freshwater one. Rainfall, runoff and gravity usually do this job. Here an animal does.

IN THE RIVER · THE DRY SEASON

How many hippos changes the answer

A FEW HIPPOSTheir nutrients act as fertilizer. Aquatic primary producers get more nitrogen and phosphorus, so NPP goes up.
A LOT OF HIPPOSToo much dung collects in slow, shallow water. The water turns murky, decomposers working on the dung use up the oxygen, fish die, and NPP goes down.

A hippo pool can go from clear to opaque in less than a day.

ON THE GRASSLAND

They change the land as well

Where hippos graze, the grass becomes stubbier and leafier, which makes it more nutritious for other grazers.

THE IDEA TO KEEP Ecosystems are linked. A nutrient flux can cross from land to water inside an animal, and whether it helps or harms depends on how much arrives.

3 OF 6 · THE BLOOM DIESPhytoplankton diePhytoplankton are short-lived. A huge bloom means a huge number of dead cells, and they sink.
4 OF 6 · DEAD MATTER PILES UPA huge detrital poolAll that dead algae is detritus: a mountain of organic matter on the bottom, far more than usual.
5 OF 6 · DECOMPOSERS BOOMDecomposers increaseMore food for decomposers means more decomposers. Their population grows to match the detritus.
6 OF 6 · OXYGEN RUNS OUTOxygen declines: a dead zoneDecomposers carry out cellular respiration, which uses oxygen. So many of them use it faster than it is replaced. Oxygen in the water falls so low that fish and other animals leave or die.The process that removes the oxygen is cellular respiration by decomposers. Not the nutrients, and not the algae while they are alive.
THE CHAIN IN ONE LINE

Six links, in order

Nutrients inBloomAlgae dieDetritus piles upDecomposers increaseOxygen declines

A dead zone is the end of eutrophication: water with so little oxygen that most animals cannot live in it. The best-known one forms in the Gulf of Mexico, fed by runoff from the Mississippi River.

PUTTING THE MODULE TOGETHER

Why adding a nutrient can remove the oxygen

Every piece of this module is in that chain: a limiting nutrient is added, primary production jumps, the extra production becomes detritus, decomposers break it down, and their respiration uses up the oxygen.

Decomposition is essential, and usually invisible. A dead zone is what it looks like when there is suddenly far too much to decompose.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Eutrophication

Excessive growth of algae and aquatic plants after extra nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, reach the water.

Dead zone

A stretch of water with so little oxygen that most animals leave or die.

Lock it in · Part 2

  1. Nitrogen builds proteins and nucleic acids. Phosphorus builds ATP, nucleic acids and phospholipids.
  2. Producers take up ammonium, nitrate and phosphate and assimilate them. Heterotrophs get N and P from food.
  3. A pool (reservoir) is where an element is. A flux is the element moving. Detritus is a pool.
  4. The largest nitrogen pool is the atmosphere, and most producers cannot use it directly.
  5. Nitrogen fixation (N₂ → ammonia) is done only by some bacteria and archaea, such as Rhizobium in legume nodules. Denitrification sends nitrogen back to the air.
  6. Decomposition returns nitrogen and phosphorus from detritus to inorganic forms.
  7. Phosphate cycles quickly through organisms but very slowly through soil and ocean.
  8. Extra nutrients → bloom → algae die → detritus → decomposers → oxygen declines: a dead zone.

Vocab · 25 terms
Essential nutrient An element that is required for growth, reproduction or metabolic function.
Macronutrient A nutrient needed in large quantities. Macronutrients are the building blocks of proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates and phospholipids.
Limiting nutrient A nutrient that limits the growth of primary producers when it is in short supply. Nitrogen and phosphorus are limiting in some ecosystems.
Biogeochemical cycle The path an element takes as it moves from one pool to another within an ecosystem.
Pool A place where an element is held: the atmosphere, soil, water, or living things. Also called a reservoir or a compartment.
Reservoir Another word for a pool.
Flux The movement of an element from one pool to another.
Organic Carbon-based molecules built by living things, such as proteins, sugars and nucleic acids.
Inorganic Not organic. Ammonium, nitrate, phosphate, CO₂ and N₂ gas are inorganic.
Detritus Dead matter and waste. Detritus is a pool, not a flux.
Detritivore An animal that eats detritus and breaks it into smaller pieces.
Decomposers Bacteria, archaea, fungi and a few other eukaryotes that break complex organic molecules down into inorganic forms.
Decomposition The breakdown of detritus into inorganic forms that producers can use again.
Fungi Eukaryotes that are chemoheterotrophs. They digest food outside their bodies and absorb it, and their cell walls are made of chitin.
Chitin The tough material in fungal cell walls. It is the same material as in the exoskeleton of arthropods.
Hyphae The thin threads that make up the body of a fungus. One thread is a hypha.
Mycelium The whole network of hyphae. The plural is mycelia.
Mycorrhizal fungi Fungi that live in and on plant roots. They help the plant take up phosphate and get sugars in return.
Ecosystem services Benefits that ecosystems provide, such as primary production. They depend on nutrients being recycled.
Assimilation Taking up an inorganic nutrient and building its atoms into organic molecules. Producers assimilate ammonium and nitrate.
Biosynthesis Building new organic molecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids.
Nitrogen fixation Turning N₂ gas into ammonia, a form living things can use. Only some bacteria and archaea can do it.
Rhizobium bacteria Nitrogen-fixing bacteria that live in nodules on the roots of legumes.
Eutrophication Excessive growth of algae and aquatic plants after extra nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, reach the water.
Dead zone A stretch of water with so little oxygen that most animals leave or die.
