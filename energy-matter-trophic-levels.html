HOW IT IS WRITTEN

A rate is an amount per unit of time. Productivity is also measured for a set area.

grams of carbonper m²per year

Written g C/m²/year. It can also be given as energy: kJ per unit area per unit time. Biomass works as a stand-in for energy because organic molecules hold chemical energy.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT

The total amount of chemical energy producers make in an ecosystem, as a rate: per unit area, per unit time.

Net primary productivity (NPP)

GPP minus the producers' own respiration. It is the new producer biomass available to consumers.

Cellular respiration

ATP

The form of chemical energy that cells use to carry out all their activities.

THE LIMIT

Why there are so few trophic levels

The energy that producers capture is a fundamental limit on an ecosystem. Nothing above them can use more than they fixed.

With about 90% gone at each step, there is soon too little left to support another level.

And it never comes back. When a top predator dies, decomposers use the energy in its body, and it ends as heat. Producers get its atoms back. They do not get its energy. That is why energy flows and does not cycle.

MODULE 2.3 · PART 2

Energy flows. Matter cycles.

THE QUESTION Why is there always less to eat at the top of a food chain, and why does an ecosystem need new energy every day but never new atoms?

Part 1 ended with energy leaving the producers. These six steps follow it the rest of the way: the big picture, trophic levels, the two laws energy follows, trophic efficiency, the 10% rule, and how matter loops back.

STEP 1 · START HERE

Every time one organism eats another, two things are passed along: energy and matter (the atoms themselves). They follow very different routes.

FOLLOW THE ENERGY

Energy flow is one-way

INFrom outside the ecosystem, almost always as sunlight.
ALONGFrom producers to consumers, and from both to the decomposers that feed on what dies.
OUTAt every one of them, some is used and becomes heat. Decomposers use up the last of it. The heat radiates away, out to space.

The atoms are a different story. Decomposers release them back into the soil, air and water, the abiotic environment, and producers take them up again.

STEP 3 · THE RULES ENERGY FOLLOWS

Two laws of thermodynamics

Two physical laws explain everything in the rest of this page.

FIRST LAW

Energy is never created or destroyed

A producer does not create energy. It converts sunlight into chemical energy stored in sugars.

When energy moves up a level, the total is all still there. It is stored in the eater's body, left in its waste, or released as heat.

SunlightChemical energy in a leafBody + waste + heat
SECOND LAW

No energy transfer is 100% efficient

Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, part of it becomes heat.

Why? Every organism spends energy to stay alive. That is respiration. Once the energy has been used, it ends up as heat.

So each trophic level holds less energy than the one below it.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
First law of thermodynamics

Energy is never created or destroyed. It is only converted from one form to another or passed on.

Second law of thermodynamics

No energy transfer is 100% efficient. Some energy always ends up as heat.

STEP 4 · WHY SO LITTLE IS PASSED ON

Trophic efficiency

Follow 1,000 units of energy from the producers (level 1) into one primary consumer (level 2). It shrinks in three stages.

DIAGRAM · EXAMPLE NUMBERS, BAR LENGTH = ENERGY

1,000 units in, 100 units kept

1,000 available in the producers
500 eaten · consumption
The other 500 is never eaten.
250 absorbed · assimilation
The other 250 leaves as waste.
100 becomes new body mass · production
The other 150 is used to stay alive and ends as heat.

Only the last bar, the new body mass, is there for level 3 to eat.

THE MATH

Three efficiencies, multiplied

CE · CONSUMPTION EFFICIENCYeaten ÷ available = 500 ÷ 1,000 = 50%
AE · ASSIMILATION EFFICIENCYabsorbed ÷ eaten = 250 ÷ 500 = 50%
PE · PRODUCTION EFFICIENCYnew body mass ÷ absorbed = 100 ÷ 250 = 40%
TROPHIC EFFICIENCY = CE × AE × PE0.5 × 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.10, so 10% is passed on
Try one: producers make 5,000 units. CE = 20%, AE = 40%, PE = 25%.

0.20 × 0.40 × 0.25 = 0.02, a trophic efficiency of 2%. Production at level 2 = 5,000 × 0.02 = 100 units.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Consumption efficiency (CE)

The share of the energy available at one level that the next level actually eats.

Assimilation efficiency (AE)

The share of what is eaten that is absorbed. The rest leaves as waste.

Production efficiency (PE)

The share of what is absorbed that becomes new body mass. The rest is used to stay alive and ends as heat.

Trophic efficiency

The share of one level's energy that becomes new body mass at the next level. CE × AE × PE, about 10% as a rule of thumb.

The exact number changes from one ecosystem to another, but about 10% of the energy in one trophic level reaches the next. The other 90% is not eaten, is lost as waste, or is used to stay alive.

WHAT IT MEANS

Why food chains are short

By level 4, only about a thousandth of the energy the producers captured is left: 10 out of 10,000, or 0.1%.

There is not enough energy to support many more levels. That is why food chains rarely go past four or five steps, and why top predators are always rare.

WHERE IT ALL GOES

Why energy can't cycle

Sooner or later, all of that energy ends up as heat. Even waste and dead matter: decomposers use it, and it becomes heat too.

Heat can't be turned back into food energy. It is a one-way exit. So energy has to keep arriving from outside. It is transferred in from the sun, not created.

THE BIG IDEA

Energy comes into an ecosystem from outside, almost always as sunlight. Only primary producers can capture it. They use part of it themselves, and what is left (NPP) feeds everything else. About 10% moves up at each step and the rest ends as heat, so energy flows and matter cycles.

SECTION 1

Ecosystems, and why primary production matters

  • Energy flow links four components of every ecosystem: primary producers, consumers, decomposers, and the abiotic environment.
  • The energy comes from an external source: usually the sun, sometimes chemical energy. Primary producers capture it.
  • Primary production is an ecosystem service. Food, wood and fibre all come from it, and every heterotroph depends on it.
  • The diversity of species an ecosystem can support depends on its primary production.
  • Abiotic factors (light, temperature, water, nutrients) set how much producers can make. That sets how much energy the whole community has.

GPP, respiration and NPP

GPP · GROSS PRIMARY PRODUCTIVITYThe total chemical energy producers make in an ecosystem, by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis.
R · RESPIRATIONThe part producers use themselves to make ATP. It ends as heat.
NPP · NET PRIMARY PRODUCTIVITYWhat remains. New producer biomass, available to consumers.
NPP = GPP − R   ·   GPP = NPP + R   ·   R = GPP − NPP
  • Both are rates: an amount per unit area per unit time. Most common units: g C/m²/year. Also given as energy (kJ per area per time).
  • Biomass can stand in for energy because organic molecules hold chemical potential energy.
  • The energy a producer captures is either used in its respiration or stored in new biomass.
  • The carbon it fixes is either released again as CO₂ by respiration or built into new cells and tissues.
  • Globally, NPP is about 170 billion tons of organic carbon a year. Humans use about 25% of it.
COMMON MIX-UPConsumers eat NPP, not GPP. The energy a plant has already respired is gone before anything can eat it.

SECTION 7

Global patterns and what limits NPP

  • On land, NPP is highest in the wet tropics and lowest in deserts and near the poles.
  • In the ocean, NPP is highest along coasts and low across most of the open ocean. A productive patch far from a coast is where nutrient-rich deep water rises into the sunlit surface.
  • Largest total: the open ocean. Its rate per m² is low, but its area is enormous. Total = rate × area.

SECTION 8

Energy flow: transfer, transformation and the 10% rule

TRANSFORMED · CHANGES FORMPhotosynthesis: solar → chemical. Cellular respiration: chemical → heat, or used for work.
TRANSFERRED · CHANGES OWNERFeeding: chemical energy moves from producer to primary consumer, and on to secondary consumer.
  • First law of thermodynamics: energy is never created or destroyed, only converted or passed on.
  • Second law: no transfer is 100% efficient. Some energy always ends as heat.
  • On average only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next. Every level loses energy as heat, producers included.
  • A trophic (energy) pyramid shows the energy at each level. Example: 1,000 kg of grasses, 100 kg of lemmings, 10 kg of fox, 1 kg of eagle.

SECTION 9

Why energy flows and matter cycles

  • All energy that enters a food web eventually leaves as heat, which radiates away to space, and organisms cannot capture heat again. So new energy has to keep arriving. Energy flow is unidirectional.
  • When a top predator dies, decomposers use the energy in its body and it ends as heat. Producers get its atoms back. They do not get its energy.
  • Matter is never used up. The same atoms pass from producers to consumers to detritus and back.

QUESTION 1 OF 34Which four parts of an ecosystem are linked by energy flow?

Correct. Not quite. Auto means self. Auto and hetero describe the carbon source. Photo and chemo describe the energy source.

QUESTION 5 OF 34A chemoheterotroph, such as an animal or a fungus, gets its energy and its carbon from:

Correct. Not quite. A chemoheterotroph takes in organic molecules made by others and gets both its energy and its carbon atoms from them.

QUESTION 6 OF 34What goes into photosynthesis, and what comes out?

Correct. Not quite. Sunlight + H₂O + CO₂ give organic molecules such as glucose, plus O₂. Solar energy becomes chemical potential energy.

QUESTION 7 OF 34Can all autotrophs carry out photosynthesis?

Correct. Not quite. Only photoautotrophs photosynthesize. Chemoautotrophs are autotrophs too, and they use chemical energy instead.

QUESTION 8 OF 34Which domains of life include photosynthetic organisms?

Correct. Not quite. Photosynthesis is found in Bacteria (such as cyanobacteria) and in Eukarya (algae and land plants). No photoautotrophs have been discovered in Archaea.

QUESTION 9 OF 34Which statement about phytoplankton is true?

Correct. Not quite. Phytoplankton are microscopic photoautotrophs in water, from both Bacteria and Eukarya. Tiny biomass, almost half of Earth's photosynthesis.

QUESTION 10 OF 34Sunlight does not reach deep-sea vents. Who are the primary producers there?

Correct. Not quite. Chemoautotrophic bacteria and archaea use the chemical energy in hydrogen sulfide and other molecules, with CO₂ as their carbon source.

QUESTION 11 OF 34Who are the main primary producers on land, and in water?

Correct. Not quite. Land plants dominate terrestrial habitats. In aquatic habitats the main producers are phytoplankton and other algae.

QUESTION 12 OF 34Which trait is shared by all land plants?

Correct. Not quite. All land plants have a multicellular haploid stage followed by a multicellular diploid stage. The other three traits belong to smaller groups.

QUESTION 13 OF 34What is the defining trait of vascular plants, and what does it do?

Correct. Not quite. Vascular plants have a transport system made of xylem and phloem.

QUESTION 14 OF 34Gymnosperms and angiosperms are both seed plants. Which trait do only angiosperms have?

Correct. Not quite. Seed plants share pollen and seeds. Angiosperms add flowers and fruit.

QUESTION 15 OF 34Gross primary productivity (GPP) is:

Correct. Not quite. GPP is the total. It is a rate, commonly given in g C/m²/year.

QUESTION 16 OF 34Do primary producers carry out cellular respiration?

Correct. Not quite. Every cell respires to make ATP, producers included. That is why NPP is smaller than GPP.

QUESTION 17 OF 34In cellular respiration, is all the energy in the organic molecules stored in ATP?

Correct. Not quite. Organic molecules + O₂ give ATP, H₂O and CO₂. Some of the energy is always released as heat.

QUESTION 18 OF 34A meadow has a GPP of 900 g C/m²/year. Its plants respire 350 g C/m²/year. What is NPP?

Correct. Not quite. NPP = GPP − R = 900 − 350 = 550 g C/m²/year.

QUESTION 19 OF 34Which one is available to consumers when they eat producer biomass?

Correct. Not quite. NPP is what remains after the producers' own respiration. It is stored as new biomass that consumers can eat.

Correct. Not quite. Without light there is no photosynthesis. Respiration uses O₂ and releases CO₂.

QUESTION 23 OF 34Besides weighing biomass and tracking gases, how else is primary productivity measured?

Correct. Not quite. Remote sensing uses satellite-based sensors to measure chlorophyll on land and in the surface ocean.

QUESTION 24 OF 34Which biome accounts for the largest amount of global NPP, and why?

Correct. Not quite. Total = rate × area. The open ocean has a low rate per square metre but an enormous area.

QUESTION 25 OF 34Where is NPP on land highest?

Correct. Not quite. On a global scale, terrestrial NPP follows temperature and water. Warm, wet places are the most productive.

QUESTION 26 OF 34A nutrient is added to a plot and NPP rises. That nutrient was:

Correct. Not quite. A limiting nutrient is the one in shortest supply compared with need. Adding more of it raises NPP.

QUESTION 27 OF 34In the open-ocean study where one nutrient was added at a time, which nutrients limited productivity?

Correct. Not quite. Adding nitrogen or iron raised growth. Phosphorus did nothing and silica's effect was not clearly different from zero.

QUESTION 28 OF 34What is eutrophication?

Correct. Not quite. Extra nitrogen and phosphorus from farming, industry and sewage drive algal blooms, tainted drinking water and very low oxygen.

Correct. Not quite. Human appropriation of net primary productivity is about 25%.

QUESTION 30 OF 34Which of these is an energy transformation, not a transfer?

Correct. Not quite. Transformed means the energy changes form. Feeding only transfers chemical energy from one organism to another.

Q1  Why should we care about energy flow and primary production? Where do we want more of it, and where is there too much?

Every food web runs on the energy that primary producers capture. Our food, wood and fibre are primary production, which makes it an ecosystem service.

Want more: crops, pasture, forests, fisheries.

Too much: eutrophication, where extra nutrients cause algal blooms in lakes and coastal water.

Q2  What four components of an ecosystem are linked by energy flow? Where does the energy usually come from, and who captures it?

Primary producers, consumers, decomposers and the abiotic environment.

The energy usually comes from the sun (sometimes from chemicals). Primary producers capture it.

Q3  What do food web arrows show? Which end does the arrow point to? How can you identify a primary producer?

An arrow shows energy moving from one organism to another. It points to the eater.

A primary producer has no arrows pointing to it. It eats nothing.

Q4  What happens to the energy in a top predator when it dies? Do primary producers use that energy?

Decomposers and detritivores feed on the body. They use its energy, and it ends as heat.

No. Producers take up the nutrients that are released, not the energy.

Q5  What do "auto", "hetero", "organic", "chemo" and "photo" mean?

Auto: self. Builds its own organic molecules from CO₂.

Hetero: other. Gets carbon from organic molecules made by others.

Organic: carbon-based molecules built by living things, such as sugars.

Chemo: energy from the chemical bonds of molecules.

Photo: energy from light.

Q6  Who can photosynthesize? Can all autotrophs? Where does the energy come from? What are the inputs and products? What is the sun's energy converted into, and who uses it?

Photoautotrophs: plants, algae, and cyanobacteria and some other bacteria. Not all autotrophs: chemoautotrophs do not.

The energy comes from the sun. Inputs: sunlight, H₂O, CO₂. Products: organic molecules ("CH₂O", such as glucose) and O₂.

Solar energy becomes chemical potential energy in organic molecules. The producer uses some. Whatever eats the producer uses the rest.

Q7  Which domains have photosynthetic organisms? Are land plants the main photosynthesizers by amount of photosynthesis? By number of groups across the tree of life?

Bacteria and Eukarya. None have been discovered in Archaea.

By amount, no. Phytoplankton are under 1% of photosynthetic biomass but do more than 45% of the planet's photosynthesis.

By number of groups, no. Many separate branches of bacteria and eukaryotes photosynthesize. Land plants are one branch.

Q8  What is a key characteristic of land plants? What does "alternation of generations" mean?

Alternation of generations: a multicellular haploid stage followed by a multicellular diploid stage.

Q9  What is a key characteristic of vascular plants? What does it allow them to do?

Vascular tissue, made of xylem and phloem. It lets them transport water (xylem) and nutrients (phloem) through the plant.

Q10  What trait is shared by gymnosperms and angiosperms? What trait is characteristic of angiosperms?

Both are seed plants: they make pollen and seeds.

Angiosperms alone make flowers and fruit.

Q11  What does "phytoplankton" mean? Which domains have phytoplankton?

Microscopic photoautotrophs that live in water. More than 5,000 species.

Bacteria and Eukarya. Not Archaea.

Q12  Sunlight does not reach deep-sea ecosystems. Where does their energy come from? Who are the primary producers? How diverse are the communities?

From chemical energy in hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and other molecules released at hydrothermal vents.

The producers are chemoautotrophs: bacteria and archaea. They use CO₂ as their carbon source.

Across most of the deep sea floor animals are uncommon, because so little food sinks that far. Around vents it is the reverse: animal populations are remarkably dense, all living on what the chemoautotrophs make.

Q13  Who are the main primary producers in aquatic environments? In terrestrial environments?

Aquatic: phytoplankton and other algae.

Terrestrial: land plants.

Q14  What is the term for all the chemical energy produced by photosynthesis in an ecosystem? What is a rate, and what units might it have?

Gross primary productivity (GPP).

A rate is an amount per unit of time. GPP is given per unit area per unit time, most often g C/m²/year, or as energy in kJ per area per time.

Q15  What happens to the energy produced by photosynthesis? To the carbon? Do primary producers carry out cellular respiration?

Energy: used in the producer's own respiration, or stored in new biomass.

Carbon: released again as CO₂ by respiration, or built into new cells and tissues.

Yes. All cells respire.

Q16  What are the ingredients and products of cellular respiration? Where is chemical potential energy stored? Is all the energy kept?

Ingredients: organic molecules ("CH₂O") and O₂. Products: ATP, H₂O and CO₂.

Chemical potential energy is stored in organic molecules.

No. Only part ends up in ATP. The rest is released as heat.

Q17  What is the difference between GPP and NPP? Which is available to consumers? How do you calculate the third from two?

GPP is the total made. NPP is what remains after the producers' own respiration.

NPP is available to consumers.

NPP = GPP − R. GPP = NPP + R. R = GPP − NPP.

Q18  How do humans use net primary productivity? A lot of it or a tiny fraction? Who depends on primary production?

For food, grazing, wood and fibre. About 25% of global NPP: a lot for one species.

All heterotrophs depend on primary production.

Q19  Which biome accounts for the largest amount of global NPP? Is that because of a very high rate per unit area?

The open ocean.

No. Its rate per square metre is low. It covers so much area that the total is the largest.

Q20  What are different ways to measure primary productivity?

Harvest and weigh dry plant biomass (this gives NPP).

Remote sensing: satellite sensors measure chlorophyll.

Gas exchange: track O₂ or CO₂, as in light and dark bottles.

Q21  What are the global patterns in NPP on land? In the oceans? Why is there sometimes high productivity in the middle of the ocean?

Land: highest in the wet tropics, lowest in deserts and near the poles.

Oceans: highest along coasts, low across most of the open ocean.

Mid-ocean patches: upwelling brings nutrient-rich deep water up into the sunlit surface.

Q22  What is a limiting nutrient? What happens to NPP when you add more of it?

The nutrient in shortest supply compared with what producers need.

NPP goes up.

Q23  What limits NPP on land at a global scale and at a local scale? In aquatic systems? Do oceans and fresh waters differ?

Land, global: temperature and water.

Land, local: nutrients, often nitrogen.

Aquatic: nutrients, and light near the surface. Oceans: nitrogen and iron in open-ocean data, and phosphorus in some regions. Fresh water: usually phosphorus.

Q24  What limits the number of trophic levels in a food web? What places a fundamental limit on energy flow through ecosystems?

Energy transfer between levels is inefficient: about 10% moves up each time, so little is left after a few steps.

The fundamental limit is the energy primary producers capture. Nothing above them can use more than that.

Q25  What is detritus? What is a detritivore? How does it differ from a decomposer?

Detritus: dead matter and waste.

Detritivore: an animal, such as a worm, that eats detritus and breaks it into smaller bits.

Decomposer: bacteria, archaea and fungi that break it down chemically and release its nutrients.

Q26  Why are energy transfers inefficient? Why doesn't energy cycle? Which trophic levels lose energy as heat?

Not everything at a level gets eaten, and every organism uses energy for cellular respiration, which releases heat.

Heat cannot be captured again by organisms, so energy passes through once.

All trophic levels lose heat, producers included.

Q27  What does it mean to say energy was transferred? Transformed?

Transferred: moved from one organism to another without changing form.

Transformed: changed from one form of energy to another.

Q28  Which process transforms solar energy into chemical energy? Chemical energy into heat or work? Which transfers chemical energy from producers to primary consumers, and from primary to secondary consumers?

Solar to chemical: photosynthesis.

Chemical to heat or work: cellular respiration.

Producer to primary consumer, and primary to secondary consumer: feeding (one eats the other).

Q29  Why does it take more corn to support a person who eats a cow than a person who eats corn?

The cow is an extra trophic level. Only about 10% of the energy in the corn becomes cow, and only about 10% of that reaches the person.

So eating beef takes roughly ten times more corn than eating the corn directly.
