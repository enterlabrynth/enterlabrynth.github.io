MODULE 2.3 · PART 1

Primary productivity: how energy gets in

THE QUESTION Where does the energy in a food web come from, who captures it, and what decides how much there is?

Eight steps: why it matters, how to read the names, who the producers are, the land plants, GPP and NPP, how they are measured, what limits them, and how the energy moves on and where it ends.

STEP 1 · START HERE

Energy has to get in somewhere

Every food web runs on energy that had to get in from outside. This part asks where it comes from, who captures it, and how much there is to go around. Part 2 follows it once it is inside.

WHERE THE ENERGY COMES FROMUsually sunlight. Sunlight is light energy (solar energy), not chemical energy. Photosynthesis transforms it into chemical potential energy, stored in organic molecules. The other, rarer source really is chemical: at deep-sea vents, chemoautotrophs get their energy from chemicals such as hydrogen sulfide.
THREE LIVING PARTSPrimary producers, consumers and decomposers. Energy also passes between consumers and decomposers, in both directions: decomposers feed on dead consumers, and some consumers eat decomposers.
THE FOURTH PART IS NOT ALIVEThe abiotic environment. Every living part exchanges with it, both ways. That is why it sits in the middle.
DECOMPOSER OR DETRITIVORE?Detritus is dead material and waste. A detritivore is an animal that eats detritus, such as an earthworm. Decomposers are the bacteria, archaea and fungi that break it down.

WHAT A FOOD WEB IS FOR

It connects the trophic levels

A food web connects the trophic levels in an ecosystem and describes how energy moves among them. Everything in it runs on what the producer at the bottom captured, which is why this part is about producers. Part 2 comes back to the web, level by level.

READ THE WEB · BOTTOM, TOP, AND WHERE THE ENERGY ENDS

FIND THE PRODUCERNo arrow points to it. Photosynthesis is what brings the sun's energy into its biomass.
FIND THE TOPArrows point in and none point out.
AFTER THE TOPThe top predator's body still holds chemical energy. Decomposers use it. This arrow is not drawn in the food chain, but it is always there.
THEN IT LEAVESOnce decomposers have used that energy it has left the ecosystem as heat, out to space. It is not destroyed, it is just no longer here to use. It does not go back to the plants. What goes back is matter: the atoms.
THE RULE Energy flow is unidirectional. It comes in as sunlight, moves up the web once, and leaves as heat. Matter is what cycles.

STEP 5 · THE BIG IDEA

GPP, respiration and NPP

A producer does not keep everything it makes. It has to run its own cells first. What is left over is what is available to the rest of the food web.

THE ENERGY DOES NOTIt comes in as sunlight, is held for a while as chemical potential energy, and leaves as heat. Not all of it ends up in ATP. This is why energy flows and matter cycles.

HOW IT IS WRITTEN

GPP and NPP are rates

A rate is an amount per unit of time. Productivity is also measured for a set area.

grams of carbonper m²per year

Written g C/m²/year. It can also be given as energy: kJ per unit area per unit time. Biomass works as a stand-in for energy because organic molecules hold chemical energy.

FOLLOW THE ENERGY, THEN THE CARBON

What happens to what the producers fix

chemical potential energy in glucose→stays in new biomass = NPP
THE CARBON · TWO PLACES IT CAN GO

THE EXACT WORDSGPP is the total amount of chemical potential energy produced by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis in an ecosystem. It is a rate.
WHERE THE CHEMICAL POTENTIAL ENERGY ISBefore respiration: in the bonds of organic molecules such as glucose. After: some of it is in ATP, the form of chemical energy cells use for all their activities.
NOT ALL OF ITThe rest is transformed into other forms of energy and released as heat. Heat is lost in every one of these transformations.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Gross primary productivity (GPP)

The total amount of chemical energy producers make in an ecosystem, as a rate: per unit area, per unit time.

Net primary productivity (NPP)

GPP minus the producers' own respiration. It is the new producer biomass available to consumers.

Cellular respiration

Organic molecules + oxygen make ATP, water and CO₂. Every cell does it, producers included.

ATP

The form of chemical energy that cells use to carry out all their activities.

STEP 6 · PUT A NUMBER ON IT

How productivity is measured

Nobody can count sugar molecules across a forest. Ecologists measure something easier that tracks it: plant mass, a gas, or the color of the planet from space.

METHOD 1 · HARVEST AND WEIGH

Dry plant biomass gives NPP

Cut the plants from a marked plot, dry them, and weigh them. The gain in biomass over time is NPP, not GPP: the energy the plants used in respiration has already left as heat, so it is not in the biomass you weigh.

Before: 150 g C/m²
After: 200 g C/m²
Nothing grazed the plot. The plants respired 50 g C/m² along the way.
NPP200 − 150 = 50 g C/m² · what a grazer could eat
GPPNPP + R = 50 + 50 = 100 g C/m²

Read the oxygen

YOUR QUESTION · WHO EVER GETS GPP?

Nobody eats GPP

GPP: all fixedrespired: left as heatNPP: what remainsthis is what gets eaten
WHY WEIGHING GIVES NPPBy the time you cut and dry a plant, respiration has already happened. The energy the plant used in respiration has already left as heat, so it is not on the scale. What you weigh is what remained: NPP.
WHAT A CONSUMER GETSThe same thing. A herbivore eats the plant's body, not the energy the plant spent staying alive. Whatever eats an organism gets its biomass, never what it already respired.
SO WHEN IS GPP USED?When the question is how much energy or carbon the producers captured in total: how much CO₂ photosynthesis took up, or how much the producers respired, since R = GPP − NPP. It is calculated or measured as gas exchange. It cannot be harvested.

The light and dark bottle method in this step goes back to Gaarder and Gran (1927), Rapports et Procès-Verbaux, Conseil International pour l'Exploration de la Mer 42: 1-48.

THE LIMIT

Why there are so few trophic levels

The energy that producers capture is a fundamental limit on an ecosystem. Nothing above them can use more than they fixed.

Only about 10% reaches the next level at each step, so there is soon too little left to support another level.

And it never comes back. When a top predator dies, decomposers use the energy in its body, and it ends as heat. Producers get its atoms back. They do not get its energy. That is why energy flows and does not cycle.

STEP 3 · THE RULES ENERGY FOLLOWS

Two laws of thermodynamics

Two physical laws explain everything in the rest of this page.

FIRST LAW

Energy is never created or destroyed

A producer does not create energy. It converts sunlight into chemical energy stored in sugars.

When energy moves up a level, the total is all still there. It is stored in the eater's body, left in its waste, or released as heat.

SunlightChemical energy in a leafBody + waste + heat
SECOND LAW

No energy transfer is 100% efficient

Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, part of it becomes heat.

Why? Every organism spends energy to stay alive. That is respiration. Once the energy has been used, it ends up as heat.

So each trophic level holds less energy than the one below it.

First law of thermodynamics

Energy is never created or destroyed. It is only converted from one form to another or passed on.

Second law of thermodynamics

No energy transfer is 100% efficient. Some energy always ends up as heat.

STEP 5 · THE RULE OF THUMB

About 10% moves up

The exact number changes from one ecosystem to another, but about 10% of the energy in one trophic level reaches the next. The other 90% does not reach the next level: some is not eaten, some leaves in waste (both go to the decomposers), and some is used in respiration and leaves as heat.

WHAT IT MEANS

Why food chains are short

By level 4, only about a thousandth of the energy the producers captured is left: 10 out of 10,000, or 0.1%.

There is not enough energy to support many more levels. That is why food chains rarely go past four or five steps, and why top predators are always rare.

WHERE IT ALL GOES

Why energy can't cycle

Sooner or later, all of that energy ends up as heat. Even waste and dead matter: decomposers use it, and it becomes heat too.

Heat can't be turned back into food energy. It is a one-way exit. So energy has to keep arriving from outside. It is transferred in from the sun, not created.

THE BIG IDEA

Energy comes into an ecosystem from outside, almost always as sunlight. Only primary producers can capture it. They use part of it themselves, and what is left (NPP) feeds everything else. About 10% moves up at each step and the rest ends as heat, so energy flows and matter cycles.

SECTION 1

Ecosystems, and why primary production matters

  • Energy flow links four components of every ecosystem: primary producers, consumers, decomposers, and the abiotic environment.
  • The energy comes from an external source, usually sunlight. Sunlight is light energy (solar energy), not chemical energy: photosynthesis transforms it into chemical potential energy. The rarer source really is chemical: at deep-sea vents, chemoautotrophs get energy from chemicals such as H₂S. Primary producers capture it.
  • Primary production is an ecosystem service. Food, wood and fiber all come from it, and every heterotroph depends on it.
  • The diversity of species an ecosystem can support depends on its primary production.
  • Abiotic factors (light, temperature, water, nutrients) set how much producers can make. That sets how much energy the whole community has.

SECTION 5THE CORE OF THE MODULE

GPP, respiration and NPP

GPP · GROSS PRIMARY PRODUCTIVITYThe total chemical energy producers make in an ecosystem, by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis.
R · RESPIRATIONThe part producers use themselves to make ATP. It ends as heat.
NPP · NET PRIMARY PRODUCTIVITYWhat remains. New producer biomass, available to consumers.
NPP = GPP − R   ·   GPP = NPP + R   ·   R = GPP − NPP
  • Both are rates: an amount per unit area per unit time. Most common units: g C/m²/year. Also given as energy (kJ per area per time).
  • Biomass can stand in for energy because organic molecules hold chemical potential energy.
  • The energy a producer captures is either used in its respiration or stored in new biomass.
  • The carbon it fixes is either released again as CO₂ by respiration or built into new cells and tissues.
  • Globally, NPP is about 170 billion tons of organic carbon a year. In 2005, people appropriated about 25% of the NPP of potential vegetation: what the land's vegetation would produce without us (Krausmann et al., 2013).

SECTION 7

Global patterns and what limits NPP

  • On land, NPP is highest in the wet tropics and lowest in deserts and near the poles.
  • In the ocean, NPP is highest along coasts and low across most of the open ocean. A productive patch far from a coast is where nutrient-rich deep water rises into the sunlit surface.
  • Largest total: the open ocean. Its rate per m² is low, but its area is enormous. Total = rate × area.
HABITATWHAT LIMITS NPP
Land · global scaleTemperature and water
Land · local scaleNutrients, often nitrogen
OceanNitrogen and iron in open-ocean data. Phosphorus in some regions. Light, away from the surface.
Fresh waterUsually phosphorus
  • A limiting nutrient is the one in shortest supply compared with what producers need. Add it and NPP goes up. Add a nutrient that is not limiting and nothing changes.
  • Eutrophication: excessive growth of algae and aquatic plants after nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus reach fresh or coastal water. Results: blooms of cyanobacteria, tainted drinking water, and hypoxia (very low oxygen).
  • It happens naturally over centuries as lakes age. Farming, industry and sewage have sped it up.
  • Why the oxygen falls: the bloom dies, and decomposers respiring on all that detritus take the oxygen out of the water.

Why nitrogen is limiting when the air is 78% nitrogen

  • N₂ is held by a triple bond that plants cannot break. Plants take up only ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻).
  • The only way in: nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Rhizobium turn N₂ into ammonia, which becomes ammonium. Different bacteria then do nitrification, ammonium to nitrate.
  • Only certain bacteria and archaea can fix nitrogen, it is slow and costly, and the usable forms are taken up as fast as they appear. Supply runs behind demand: that is what limiting means.

In water, light comes first

  • Sunlight is absorbed on the way down, so photosynthesis happens only in a thin sunlit layer near the surface. Nutrients are often deeper.
  • Upwelling matters because it brings deep nutrients up into the light.

SECTION 8

Energy flow: transfer, transformation and the 10% rule

TRANSFORMED · CHANGES FORMPhotosynthesis: solar → chemical. Cellular respiration: chemical → heat, or used for work.
TRANSFERRED · CHANGES OWNERFeeding: chemical energy moves from producer to primary consumer, and on to secondary consumer.
  • First law of thermodynamics: energy is never created or destroyed, only converted or passed on.
  • Second law: no transfer is 100% efficient. Some energy always ends as heat.
  • On average only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next. Every level loses energy as heat, producers included.
  • A trophic (energy) pyramid shows the energy at each level. Example: 1,000 kg of grasses, 100 kg of lemmings, 10 kg of fox, 1 kg of eagle.

SECTION 9

Why energy flows and matter cycles

  • All energy that enters a food web eventually leaves as heat, which radiates away to space, and organisms cannot capture heat again. So new energy has to keep arriving. Energy flow is unidirectional.
  • When a top predator dies, decomposers use the energy in its body and it ends as heat. Producers get its atoms back. They do not get its energy.
  • Matter is never used up. The same atoms pass from producers to consumers to detritus and back.

DETRITIVOREAn animal, such as a worm, that eats detritus and breaks it into smaller bits.
DECOMPOSERBacteria, archaea and fungi that break it down chemically and release its nutrients.

Quiz

46 questions on producers, primary productivity and energy flow. Pick an answer to see whether it is right and why.

QUESTION 1 OF 46Which four parts of an ecosystem are linked by energy flow?

Correct. Not quite. Every ecosystem has primary producers, consumers, decomposers and an abiotic environment. Energy flow links all four.

QUESTION 2 OF 46In a food web, an arrow runs from a berry bush to a mouse. What does it mean?

Correct. Not quite. Arrows point from the food to the eater. That is the direction the energy moves.

QUESTION 3 OF 46In a food web diagram, how can you spot a primary producer?

Correct. Not quite. A producer makes its own food, so no feeding arrow points to it. Arrows only lead away from it.

QUESTION 4 OF 46What does the prefix "auto" in autotroph tell you?

Correct. Not quite. Auto means self. Auto and hetero describe the carbon source. Photo and chemo describe the energy source.

QUESTION 5 OF 46A chemoheterotroph, such as an animal or a fungus, gets its energy and its carbon from:

Correct. Not quite. A chemoheterotroph takes in organic molecules made by others and gets both its energy and its carbon atoms from them.

QUESTION 6 OF 46What goes into photosynthesis, and what comes out?

Correct. Not quite. Sunlight + H₂O + CO₂ give organic molecules such as glucose, plus O₂. Solar energy becomes chemical potential energy.

QUESTION 7 OF 46Can all autotrophs carry out photosynthesis?

Correct. Not quite. Only photoautotrophs photosynthesize. Chemoautotrophs are autotrophs too, and they use chemical energy instead.

QUESTION 8 OF 46Which domains of life include photosynthetic organisms?

Correct. Not quite. Photosynthesis is found in Bacteria (such as cyanobacteria) and in Eukarya (algae and land plants). No photoautotrophs have been discovered in Archaea.

QUESTION 9 OF 46Which statement about phytoplankton is true?

Correct. Not quite. Phytoplankton are microscopic photoautotrophs in water, from both Bacteria and Eukarya. Tiny biomass, almost half of Earth's photosynthesis.

QUESTION 10 OF 46Sunlight does not reach deep-sea vents. Who are the primary producers there?

Correct. Not quite. Chemoautotrophic bacteria and archaea use the chemical energy in hydrogen sulfide and other molecules, with CO₂ as their carbon source.

QUESTION 11 OF 46Who are the main primary producers on land, and in water?

Correct. Not quite. Land plants dominate terrestrial habitats. In aquatic habitats the main producers are phytoplankton and other algae.

QUESTION 12 OF 46Which trait is shared by all land plants?

Correct. Not quite. All land plants have a multicellular haploid stage followed by a multicellular diploid stage. The other three traits belong to smaller groups.

QUESTION 13 OF 46What is the defining trait of vascular plants, and what does it do?

Correct. Not quite. Vascular plants have a transport system made of xylem and phloem.

QUESTION 14 OF 46Gymnosperms and angiosperms are both seed plants. Which trait do only angiosperms have?

Correct. Not quite. Seed plants share pollen and seeds. Angiosperms add flowers and fruit.

QUESTION 15 OF 46Gross primary productivity (GPP) is:

Correct. Not quite. GPP is the total. It is a rate, commonly given in g C/m²/year.

QUESTION 16 OF 46Do primary producers carry out cellular respiration?

Correct. Not quite. Every cell respires to make ATP, producers included. That is why NPP is smaller than GPP.

QUESTION 17 OF 46In cellular respiration, is all the energy in the organic molecules stored in ATP?

Correct. Not quite. Organic molecules + O₂ give ATP, H₂O and CO₂. Some of the energy is always released as heat.

QUESTION 18 OF 46A meadow has a GPP of 900 g C/m²/year. Its plants respire 350 g C/m²/year. What is NPP?

Correct. Not quite. NPP = GPP − R = 900 − 350 = 550 g C/m²/year.

QUESTION 19 OF 46Which one is available to consumers when they eat producer biomass?

Correct. Not quite. NPP is what remains after the producers' own respiration. It is stored as new biomass that consumers can eat.

QUESTION 20 OF 46Plant biomass on a plot goes from 150 to 200 g C/m². Nothing grazed it. The plants respired 50 g C/m². How much could herbivores have eaten?

Correct. Not quite. Harvesting and weighing dry biomass measures NPP: 200 − 150 = 50 g C/m². GPP was NPP + R = 100 g C/m².

QUESTION 21 OF 46Two bottles of pond water start at 8 mg O₂/L. The light bottle ends at 11. The dark bottle ends at 6. What is GPP?

Correct. Not quite. Light bottle change = NPP = +3. Dark bottle loss = R = 2. GPP = NPP + R = 5 mg O₂/L.

QUESTION 22 OF 46In a sealed dark bottle, oxygen falls and CO₂ rises. Which process is running?

Correct. Not quite. Without light there is no photosynthesis. Respiration uses O₂ and releases CO₂.

QUESTION 23 OF 46Besides weighing biomass and tracking gases, how else is primary productivity measured?

Correct. Not quite. Remote sensing uses satellite-based sensors to measure chlorophyll on land and in the surface ocean.

QUESTION 24 OF 46Which biome accounts for the largest amount of global NPP, and why?

Correct. Not quite. Total = rate × area. The open ocean has a low rate per square meter but an enormous area.

QUESTION 25 OF 46Where is NPP on land highest?

Correct. Not quite. On a global scale, terrestrial NPP follows temperature and water. Warm, wet places are the most productive.

QUESTION 26 OF 46A nutrient is added to a plot and NPP rises. That nutrient was:

Correct. Not quite. A limiting nutrient is the one in shortest supply compared with need. Adding more of it raises NPP.

QUESTION 27 OF 46In the open-ocean study where one nutrient was added at a time, which nutrients limited productivity?

Correct. Not quite. Adding nitrogen or iron raised growth. Phosphorus did nothing and silica's effect was not clearly different from zero.

QUESTION 28 OF 46What is eutrophication?

Correct. Not quite. Extra nitrogen and phosphorus from farming, industry and sewage drive algal blooms, tainted drinking water and very low oxygen.

QUESTION 29 OF 46In 2005, human appropriation of net primary productivity was about what share of the NPP of potential vegetation?

Correct. Not quite. About 25% of the NPP that the land's vegetation would have without human land use (Krausmann et al., 2013).

QUESTION 30 OF 46Which of these is an energy transformation, not a transfer?

Correct. Not quite. Transformed means the energy changes form. Feeding only transfers chemical energy from one organism to another.

QUESTION 31 OF 46On average, how much of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next?

Correct. Not quite. About 10%. This inefficiency is what limits the number of trophic levels.

QUESTION 32 OF 46Why does it take more corn to feed a person who eats beef than one who eats the corn?

Correct. Not quite. Corn to cow to person is two transfers at about 10% each. Corn to person is one.

QUESTION 33 OF 46When a top predator dies, what happens to the energy in its body?

Correct. Not quite. Decomposers get the energy, and it leaves as heat. Producers can reuse the atoms, but not the energy. That is why energy flows and does not cycle.

QUESTION 34 OF 46Which trophic levels lose energy as heat?

Correct. Not quite. Every organism carries out cellular respiration, producers included, so every level loses heat.

QUESTION 35 OF 46How does the diversity of species in an ecosystem relate to its primary production?

Correct. Not quite. Primary production is the energy supply for everything else in the ecosystem.

QUESTION 36 OF 46Where does the energy that enters a food web finally end up?

Correct. Not quite. Energy flow is one-way: in as sunlight, along the food web, and out as heat. Decomposers release the last of it as heat.

QUESTION 37 OF 46"Energy is never created or destroyed, only converted from one form to another." This is the:

Correct. Not quite. The first law. The second law says no transfer is 100% efficient: some energy always ends up as heat.

QUESTION 38 OF 46Which law explains why each trophic level holds less energy than the one below it?

Correct. Not quite. No energy transfer is 100% efficient. At every step some becomes heat.

QUESTION 39 OF 46Consumption efficiency (CE) is:

Correct. Not quite. CE = eaten ÷ available. AE = absorbed ÷ eaten. PE = new body mass ÷ absorbed.

QUESTION 40 OF 46An animal absorbs 250 units of energy and builds 100 units of new body mass. Its production efficiency (PE) is:

Correct. Not quite. PE = new body mass ÷ absorbed = 100 ÷ 250 = 40%. The rest was respired and left as heat.

QUESTION 41 OF 46CE = 50%, AE = 50%, PE = 40%. What is the trophic efficiency?

Correct. Not quite. Trophic efficiency = CE × AE × PE = 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.10, so 10% is passed on.

QUESTION 42 OF 46Producers make 5,000 units. CE = 20%, AE = 40%, PE = 25%. How much production is there at the next level?

Correct. Not quite. 0.20 × 0.40 × 0.25 = 0.02, a trophic efficiency of 2%. 5,000 × 0.02 = 100 units.

QUESTION 43 OF 46Sunlight is what kind of energy?

Correct. Not quite. Only the rarer source, at deep-sea vents, is chemical: chemoautotrophs use molecules such as H₂S.

QUESTION 44 OF 46Who does the most photosynthesis on Earth?

Correct. Not quite. Phytoplankton are under 1% of photosynthetic biomass but do more than 45% of the photosynthesis, most of it out in the ocean.

QUESTION 45 OF 46Air is 78% nitrogen. Why is nitrogen still often limiting on land?

Correct. Not quite. Plants take up only ammonium and nitrate. Only some bacteria and archaea can fix N₂, and usable forms are taken up as fast as they appear.

QUESTION 46 OF 46Why is photosynthesis in a lake or ocean limited to a thin layer near the surface?

Correct. Not quite. Nutrients are often deeper, which is why upwelling, bringing them up into the light, makes a patch so productive.

Q1  What are some reasons why we should care about energy flow and primary production? What are some cases where we want to promote primary production? What are some cases where there is too much primary production?

Every food web runs on the energy that primary producers capture. Our food, wood and fiber are primary production, which makes it an ecosystem service.

Want more: crops, pasture, forests, fisheries.

Too much: eutrophication, where extra nutrients cause algal blooms in lakes and coastal water.

Q2  What are the four components of an ecosystem that are linked by energy flow? Where does the energy that flows through a food web usually come from? Which organisms “capture” that energy when it enters a food web?

Primary producers, consumers, decomposers and the abiotic environment.

The energy usually comes from the sun (sometimes from chemicals). Primary producers capture it.

Q3  What do the food web arrows indicate? Which end does the arrow point to? How can you identify something as a primary producer?

An arrow shows energy moving from one organism to another. It points to the eater.

A primary producer has no arrows pointing to it. It eats nothing.

Q4  What happens to the energy in a top predator when it dies? Do primary producers use that energy?

Decomposers and detritivores feed on the body. They use its energy, and it ends as heat.

No. Producers take up the nutrients that are released, not the energy.

Q5  What does the “auto” prefix on a word refer to? What about “hetero”? What does “organic” mean? What does the “chemo” prefix refer to? What about “photo”?

Auto: self. Builds its own organic molecules from CO₂.

Hetero: other. Gets carbon from organic molecules made by others.

Organic: carbon-containing molecules such as carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.

Chemo: energy from the chemical bonds of molecules.

Photo: energy from light.

Q6  What organisms can carry out photosynthesis? Can all autotrophs carry out photosynthesis? Where does the energy for photosynthesis come from? What are the inputs to the photosynthesis reaction? What are the products of it? What is the energy from the sun converted into? Who uses that energy?

Photoautotrophs: plants, algae, and cyanobacteria and some other bacteria. Not all autotrophs: chemoautotrophs do not.

The energy comes from the sun. Inputs: sunlight, H₂O, CO₂. Products: organic molecules ("CH₂O", such as glucose) and O₂.

Solar energy becomes chemical potential energy in organic molecules. The producer uses some. Whatever eats the producer uses the rest.

Are land plants the main photosynthetic organisms in the world, when considering the amount of photosynthesis that occurs? Consider the number of taxa across the tree of life that are photosynthetic: are land plants the main photosynthetic organisms in the world?

Bacteria and Eukarya. None have been discovered in Archaea.

By amount, no. Phytoplankton are under 1% of photosynthetic biomass but do more than 45% of the planet's photosynthesis.

By number of groups, no. Many separate branches of bacteria and eukaryotes photosynthesize. Land plants are one branch.

Q8  What is a key characteristic of land plants? What does “alternation of generations” mean?

Alternation of generations: a multicellular haploid stage followed by a multicellular diploid stage.

Q9  What is a key characteristic of vascular plants? What does this trait allow them to do?

Vascular tissue, made of xylem and phloem. It lets them transport water (xylem) and nutrients (phloem) through the plant.

Q10  What trait is shared by gymnosperms & angiosperms? What trait is characteristic of angiosperms?

Both are seed plants: they make pollen and seeds.

Angiosperms alone make flowers and fruit.

Q11  What does the term “phytoplankton” mean? Which of the following domains has/have organisms that are considered phytoplankton: Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya?

Microscopic photoautotrophs that live in water. More than 5,000 species.

Bacteria and Eukarya. Not Archaea.

Q12  Sunlight does not reach deep sea ecosystems – where does the energy in these ecosystems come from? Who are the primary producers in deep sea ecosystems? How diverse are the communities in the deep sea?

From chemical energy in hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and other molecules released at hydrothermal vents.

The producers are chemoautotrophs: bacteria and archaea. They use CO₂ as their carbon source.

Across most of the deep sea floor animals are uncommon, because so little food sinks that far. Around vents it is the reverse: animal populations are remarkably dense, all living on what the chemoautotrophs make.

Q13  Who are the main primary producers in aquatic environments? Who are the main primary producers in terrestrial environments?

Aquatic: phytoplankton and other algae.

Terrestrial: land plants.

Q14  What is the term used for all of the chemical energy produced by photosynthesis in an ecosystem? This is measured as a rate – what is a rate? And what sorts of units might it have?

Gross primary productivity (GPP).

A rate is an amount per unit of time. GPP is given per unit area per unit time, most often g C/m²/year, or as energy in kJ per area per time.

Q15  What happens to the energy that is produced by photosynthesis? What happens to the carbon? Do primary producers carry out cellular respiration?

Energy: used in the producer's own respiration, or stored in new biomass.

Carbon: released again as CO₂ by respiration, or built into new cells and tissues.

Yes. All cells respire.

Q16  What are the ingredients in the cellular respiration reaction? What are the products? Where is chemical potential energy stored? Is all the energy from the reactants stored as chemical potential energy in the products of cellular respiration? If not, what happens to that energy?

Ingredients: organic molecules ("CH₂O") and O₂. Products: ATP, H₂O and CO₂.

Chemical potential energy is stored in organic molecules.

No. Only part ends up in ATP. The rest is released as heat.

Q17  What is the difference between Gross Primary Production and Net Primary Production? Which of these is available to consumers when they eat autotroph biomass? If you know two of the three of GPP, NPP, and respiration, how would you calculate the third?

GPP is the total made. NPP is what remains after the producers' own respiration.

NPP is available to consumers.

NPP = GPP − R. GPP = NPP + R. R = GPP − NPP.

Q18  How do humans use net primary productivity? Do they use a lot of global NPP or just a tiny fraction of it? Who depends on primary production?

For food, grazing, wood and fiber. A lot: in 2005, about 25% of the NPP of potential vegetation, meaning what the land's vegetation would produce without us.

All heterotrophs depend on primary production.

Q19  Which biome accounts for the largest amount of global NPP? Is this because this biome has a very high rate per unit area?

The open ocean.

No. Its rate per square meter is low. It covers so much area that the total is the largest.

Q20  What are different ways we can measure primary productivity?

Harvest and weigh dry plant biomass (this gives NPP).

Remote sensing: satellite sensors measure chlorophyll.

Gas exchange: track O₂ or CO₂, as in light and dark bottles.

Q21  What are the general patterns in NPP across the globe when considering terrestrial habitats? What are the general global patterns for oceans? Why is there sometimes high primary productivity in the middle of the ocean, far away from a coast?

Land: highest in the wet tropics, lowest in deserts and near the poles.

Oceans: highest along coasts, low across most of the open ocean.

Mid-ocean patches: upwelling brings nutrient-rich deep water up into the sunlit surface.

Q22  What is a limiting nutrient? What happens to NPP when you add more of a limiting nutrient – does NPP go up or down?

The nutrient in shortest supply compared with what producers need.

NPP goes up.

Land, global: temperature and water.

Land, local: nutrients, often nitrogen.

Aquatic: nutrients, and light near the surface. Oceans: nitrogen and iron in open-ocean data, and phosphorus in some regions. Fresh water: usually phosphorus.

Q29  Why does it take more corn to support a person if the person eats a cow, as compared to if the person eats corn?

The cow is an extra trophic level. Only about 10% of the energy in the corn becomes cow, and only about 10% of that reaches the person.

So eating beef takes roughly ten times more corn than eating the corn directly.
