MODULE 3.4 · PART 1

The spectrum of dominance

THE QUESTION Mendel's peas always had one allele hiding the other. What else can two alleles of one gene do?

In this part: complete, incomplete and codominance; why the heterozygote looks the way it does (allele → protein → trait); four o'clocks; why pink isn't blending; sickle cell; and a two-gene practice problem.

ONE GENE, TWO ALLELES

The spectrum of dominance: look at the heterozygote

Dominance is about how two alleles of the same gene behave together in one individual. The homozygotes always look like their own allele. The heterozygote is the only one that shows how the two alleles interact, so it decides which kind of dominance a gene has.1,2

INTERACTIVE · THREE KINDS

Red homozygote · heterozygote · white homozygote

Tap a button to explore
COMPLETE DOMINANCEThe heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygoteOne copy of the dominant allele is enough. Mendel's peas: Aa is yellow, just like AA.
INCOMPLETE DOMINANCEThe heterozygote is in betweenRed × white gives pink. The heterozygote is intermediate to the two homozygotes.
CODOMINANCEBoth alleles show in the heterozygoteNeither is hidden and nothing is in between: both alleles' products are visible side by side, like blood type AB.
TAP ONEWhich kind of dominance?All three start the same: one red allele and one white allele in the heterozygote. Tap a kind to see what that heterozygote looks like.
Check yourself: Why do we use the heterozygote's phenotype to decide between complete dominance, codominance and incomplete dominance?

Because the homozygotes look the same in all three cases (red is red, white is white). Only the heterozygote, with one of each allele, shows how the two alleles interact: like one parent (complete), in between (incomplete) or both at once (codominance).

COMMON MIX-UPDominance is between alleles of one gene. It doesn't mean an allele is common, better or "stronger". Effects between different genes are epistasis (Part 3).
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Complete dominance

When the heterozygote looks exactly like the dominant homozygote: one copy of the dominant allele is enough (Mendel's peas, Aa yellow like AA).

Incomplete dominance

When the heterozygote's phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygotes (red × white four o'clocks give pink). Genotype and phenotype ratios are the same, 1 : 2 : 1.

Codominance

When the effects of both alleles are visible in the heterozygote, side by side (blood type AB shows sugar A and sugar B; AS makes both hemoglobin shapes).

WHY

Allele → protein → trait: what decides the heterozygote

An allele is a version of a gene's DNA. It shows up in a trait only through the protein it makes. In four o'clocks, "each allele codes for a different form of a protein. One allele allows a purple pigment to be formed, the other doesn't. Heterozygotes produce only half as much pigment." Whether half is enough decides the kind of dominance.2

STEP THROUGH IT · 4 STEPS

Count the working copies, then the pigment

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 4
TWO WORKING ALLELESLots of enzyme, lots of pigmentEach working allele makes enzyme, and the enzyme builds pigment. Two copies: a red flower.
ONE WORKING · INCOMPLETE DOMINANCEHalf the enzyme, half the pigmentThe broken allele makes no working enzyme, so the heterozygote has half as much pigment: pink. This is the four o'clock.
ONE WORKING · COMPLETE DOMINANCEHalf is already enoughFor many genes, one working copy makes enough protein for the full trait. Then Aa looks exactly like AA: complete dominance, as in Mendel's peas.
NO WORKING ALLELESNo enzyme, no pigmentaa makes no working enzyme: a white flower. Same chain every time: allele → protein → trait.
Check yourself: How does protein production and function relate to an individual's phenotype?

Each allele is read to make a version of a protein. The phenotype depends on how much working protein the cell has and what that protein does: two working copies, one, or none. Whether one copy is "enough" decides if the trait shows complete or incomplete dominance.

INCOMPLETE DOMINANCE

Four o'clocks: red × white → pink → 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white

With incomplete dominance every genotype has its own look, so you can read the genotype straight off the flower.1,2

STEP THROUGH IT · P TO F2

Four o'clock flower color

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 4
PARENTAL CROSSTrue-breeding red × true-breeding whiteNeither allele is dominant, so we don't use capital and lowercase letters. One letter for the gene, a superscript for each allele: FR (red) and FW (white).
F1All pinkEvery F1 is FRFW, with half the pigment of the red parent.
F1 × F1The same 2×2 square as alwaysSegregation still works exactly as Mendel said: each pink parent gives FR half the time and FW half the time.

Check yourself: Why do we use a different notation (like FWFP) for incomplete dominance and codominance?

Capital vs. lowercase means "this one is dominant, that one is recessive". With incomplete dominance or codominance neither allele is dominant, so both get the same letter and are told apart by superscripts.

THE ONE IDEAIncomplete dominance changes how the heterozygote looks. It doesn't change how alleles are passed on: same gametes, same squares, same 1 : 2 : 1 genotypes.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Incomplete dominance

When the heterozygote's phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygotes (red × white four o'clocks give pink). Genotype and phenotype ratios are the same, 1 : 2 : 1.

NOT BLENDING

Why pink isn't blending inheritance

A pink F1 looks like paint mixing. The old idea of blending inheritance said exactly that: traits mix and can never be separated again. The F2 is what proves it wrong.1,2

STEP THROUGH IT · TWO PREDICTIONS

Blending vs. incomplete dominance

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 2
P AND F1Same result either wayBoth ideas predict red × white → pink. One cross can't tell them apart.
F2Red and white come backBlending would give pink forever. Real F2s are 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white, so the FR and FW alleles stayed separate and intact inside the pink plants.
Check yourself: Why do we need two crosses and the F2 to rule out blending inheritance?

The first cross (P) gives pink F1s under both ideas. Only the second cross (F1 × F1) can show the difference: if red and white reappear in the F2, the alleles were never blended, just sitting together in the heterozygote.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Blending inheritance

An old, wrong idea that parents' traits mix like paint and can't be unmixed. Ruled out because recessive or extreme traits come back in the F2.

CODOMINANCE

Sickle cell: codominant proteins, recessive disease

The β-globin gene makes part of hemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in red blood cells. Allele A makes normal β-globin; allele S changes one amino acid, which changes the protein's shape. How you classify the alleles depends on which level you look at.3,2

STEP THROUGH IT · THREE LEVELS

AA · AS · SS

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 3
PROTEIN LEVELAS makes both shapesEach allele is read and makes its own β-globin, so an AS person has normal and sickle-shaped molecules: codominance.
CELL LEVELAS has some sickled cellsSS red blood cells sickle badly. AS cells are mostly normal, with some sickling.
WHOLE PERSONThe disease is recessiveOnly SS has sickle-cell disease, so at the level of the person the disease behaves as recessive. AS people are usually healthy and resist malaria.
Check yourself: Explain why the AS genotype shows proteins with two different shapes.

An AS person has one A allele and one S allele, and the cell reads both. A makes normal β-globin; S makes β-globin with one different amino acid, which folds into a different shape. Both proteins end up in the same red blood cells.

COMMON MIX-UP"Codominant" or "recessive" depends on the level. At the protein level A and S are codominant; for the disease, S is recessive. Questions about mode of inheritance "at the level of protein production vs. the whole organism" want exactly this.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Codominance

When the effects of both alleles are visible in the heterozygote, side by side (blood type AB shows sugar A and sugar B; AS makes both hemoglobin shapes).

Lock it in · Part 1

  1. Dominance is how two alleles of one gene behave together; the heterozygote tells you which kind.
  2. Complete: Aa looks like AA (3 : 1). Incomplete: Aa is in between (1 : 2 : 1 for genotype and phenotype). Codominance: Aa shows both.
  3. Allele → protein → trait. One working copy may make enough (complete) or only half the product (incomplete).
  4. Incomplete dominance isn't blending: red and white come back in the F2.
  5. Sickle cell: A and S are codominant at the protein level; the disease is recessive at the level of the person.
  6. Two genes with different kinds of dominance: still one 2×2 per gene, then multiply.

MODULE 3.4 · PART 2

Multiple alleles, blood types and pedigrees

THE QUESTION If a person carries only two alleles, how can there be four blood types? And how do we study inheritance in people?

In this part: many alleles in a population, ABO blood types, predicting blood types, reading pedigrees, and telling the kind of dominance from data.

MANY ALLELES

Multiple alleles: many in the population, two per person

Mutation keeps making new versions of genes, so a population can hold far more than two alleles of one gene. But a diploid person has only two homologs, so only two alleles, and a gamete only one.1,2

STEP THROUGH IT · ZOOM IN

Population → person → gamete

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 3
THE POPULATIONMany allelesAcross everyone, ABO has 3 alleles and β-globin has about 500.
ONE PERSONTwo allelesOne locus on each of two homologs: one allele from mom, one from dad.
ONE GAMETEOne alleleSegregation still puts exactly one allele of each gene in each gamete.
Check yourself: If each individual has at most two alleles of a gene, how can a population have multiple alleles?

Different people carry different pairs. With three alleles in the population (IA, IB, i), one person might carry IAi and another IBIB. Each person holds two; the population holds them all.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Multiple alleles

More than two alleles of one gene in a population (ABO has three; β-globin about 500). Each person still carries only two, and each gamete one.

ABO BLOOD TYPES

3 alleles, 6 genotypes, 4 blood types

The ABO gene (I) makes the enzyme that puts a sugar on glycoproteins (sugar + protein) on red blood cells. Allele IA adds sugar A, IB adds sugar B, and i adds nothing. One gene shows multiple alleles, complete dominance and codominance at once.3,2

INTERACTIVE · TAP A BLOOD TYPE

TYPE AIAIA or IAiSugar A only. The i allele adds nothing, so IAi looks like IAIA.
TYPE BIBIB or IBiSugar B only.
TYPE ABIAIBBoth sugars: codominance. Only one genotype gives AB.
TYPE OiiNo sugar added. Only one genotype gives O (Part 3 shows a second way to look type O).
Check yourself: How do 3 alleles give 4 phenotypes and 6 genotypes?

Genotypes: IAIA, IAi, IBIB, IBi, IAIB, ii (every pair of the 3 alleles, including doubles). Phenotypes: A (IAIA, IAi), B (IBIB, IBi), AB (IAIB), O (ii). Dominance of IA and IB over i makes two genotypes look the same.

NOTATIONClass uses IA, IB and i. The textbook's table uses A, B and O for the same alleles.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Blood type

The ABO phenotype: which sugars (A, B, both or neither) sit on the glycoproteins of red blood cells. Three alleles, six genotypes, four blood types.

From IAIA: A or AB. From IAi: A, AB, or B (IBi). So the children could be type A, AB, or B, never O (he always passes IA or IB).

PEDIGREES

Reading a pedigree: dominant or recessive?

People can't be crossed like peas, so for humans we read family trees. The pedigree in the slides comes from the textbook's Figure 14.26.4,2

STEP THROUGH IT · 5 STEPS

From the symbols to the mode of inheritance

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 5
THE SYMBOLSSquare, circle, shaded, linesSquares are males, circles females, shaded = affected. A line between two = they had children; children hang below, left to right by birth order.
THE FAMILYFour generationsTwo people in generation II and one in IV have the disease.
THE CLUEUnaffected parents, affected childNeither parent in generation I has the disease, but a son does. A dominant allele can't hide: an affected child of a dominant trait has an affected parent. So the allele must be recessive.
WORK OUT GENOTYPESAffected = aa; their parents = AaThe son is aa. He got one a from each parent, and both parents are unaffected, so both are Aa carriers. Unaffected people in the tree are A_ (AA or Aa) until a child proves which.
THE COUSINSDouble line, affected sonIn generation III two cousins, both unaffected, have an affected son. A shared ancestor's hidden recessive allele was passed down both sides and met again.
DOMINANTShows up in every generationEvery affected child has an affected parent. Affected parents can have unaffected children.
RECESSIVECan skip generationsAffected children can have two unaffected (carrier) parents. Often appears after relatives have children together.
Check yourself: Using the pedigree, how can you tell the mode of inheritance?

Find an affected child with two unaffected parents. That can only happen if both parents carry a hidden allele: recessive. If instead every affected person has an affected parent, and two affected parents have an unaffected child, the trait is dominant.

COMMON MIX-UP"The next child" of two carriers is a sibling from the same Aa × Aa square: 1/4 chance of aa, every time. It isn't the F2. Each child is a separate, independent coin flip.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Pedigree

A family tree for one trait: squares are males, circles females, shaded symbols affected. Used to work out how a trait is inherited and who carries it.

MODULE 3.4 · PART 3

Epistasis: genes that affect other genes

THE QUESTION Can the alleles of one gene decide whether another gene shows at all?

In this part: the H gene and ABO, how epistasis changes ratios (13 : 3), a question that uses the whole module, and gene × environment.

GENE × GENE

Epistasis: one gene can hide another

Dominance is about two alleles of one gene. Epistasis is about two different genes that act in the same pathway, so one can mask the other. Blood type is the class example: the ABO gene can only work if the H gene has done its job first.1,2

STEP THROUGH IT · THE H GENE AND ABO

Stem first, then sugar

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 4
STEP 1 · H GENEBuild the stemThe H gene makes the precursor molecule, the stem that sugars attach to. H = working, dominant; h = broken, recessive.
STEP 2 · I GENEAdd the sugarThen the ABO (I) gene adds sugar A, sugar B, or nothing, as in Part 2.
hhNothing to attach toWith hh there is no stem. Even an IA allele has nowhere to put sugar A, so the cells look type O.
THE RULEH first, then IH_ → the I gene decides the blood type. hh → type O, whatever the I alleles are.
Check yourself: What blood type would a person with genotype IAi hh have?

Type O. hh makes no working precursor, so the IA allele has nothing to add sugar A to.

Check yourself: Explain how one gene can affect the expression of another (epistasis).

When two genes' proteins act in the same pathway, an early gene can block the later one. If the early step fails (hh), the later gene's alleles can't show, no matter what they are.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Epistasis (gene × gene interaction)

When one gene changes or masks the phenotype of another gene. It changes phenotype ratios (9 : 3 : 3 : 1 can become 13 : 3) but not genotype ratios.

WHAT IT CHANGES

Epistasis changes phenotype ratios, not genotype ratios

The textbook's example: two breeds of white chicken, white for different reasons. Their F2 is not 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 in looks, even though the genotypes come out exactly as Mendel predicts.2

STEP THROUGH IT · WHITE × WHITE

From 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 to 13 : 3

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 4
TWO GENES, ONE TRAITPigment and an inhibitorC makes pigment, c makes none. I makes an inhibitor that blocks the pigment; i makes none.
F1Cc Ii, whiteThe inhibitor I blocks the pigment, so the F1 is white too. The F2 is Cc Ii × Cc Ii: solve each gene with its own 2×2.
GENOTYPE GROUPSStill 9 : 3 : 3 : 1Multiply: 9/16 C_ I_, 3/16 C_ ii, 3/16 cc I_, 1/16 cc ii. Independent assortment works as usual.
PHENOTYPES13 white : 3 coloredOnly C_ ii has pigment and no inhibitor. The other three groups (9 + 3 + 1 = 13) are white.
COMMON MIX-UPEpistasis doesn't change which genotypes appear or how often. It changes which genotypes look alike, so phenotype ratios like 13 : 3, 9 : 3 : 4 or 12 : 3 : 1 replace 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

GENE × ENVIRONMENT

The same allele can help in one place and not another

Genes interact with the environment as well as with each other. The S allele of β-globin protects carriers against malaria, so it is beneficial where malaria is common and offers no such benefit where it is absent.3

ANOTHER EXAMPLE FROM THE TEXTBOOKLactase persistence (digesting milk as an adult) is favored where people kept dairy animals, and may be close to neutral elsewhere.

Lock it in · Part 3

  1. Epistasis = one gene changes or masks another gene's phenotype, usually because their proteins act in the same pathway.
  2. H gene → stem; I gene → sugar. hh looks type O whatever the I alleles are.
  3. Epistasis changes phenotype ratios (13 : 3, 9 : 3 : 4), never the genotype ratios.
  4. Two-gene problems are still one 2×2 per gene, then multiply.
  5. Gene × environment: the same allele can be beneficial, neutral or harmful depending on where you live.

Q1  Why do we use the heterozygous phenotype when determining whether a gene exhibits complete dominance, codominance, and incomplete dominance?

The two homozygotes look the same no matter which kind of dominance the gene has (red is red, white is white). Only the heterozygote carries one of each allele, so only it shows how the two alleles interact.

Heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote → complete dominance. Heterozygote is in between → incomplete dominance. Heterozygote shows both alleles' traits at once → codominance.

Q2  Explain why incomplete dominance is not the same as blending inheritance. Why do we need to do two crosses and examine the F2 generation to rule out blending inheritance?

Blending inheritance says the parents' traits mix like paint and can never be separated again. With incomplete dominance the alleles stay separate and intact inside the heterozygote; only the phenotype is in between.

One cross can't tell the two ideas apart: red × white gives pink F1s under both. The second cross, F1 × F1, can: blending predicts all pink forever, but incomplete dominance gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Red and white coming back in the F2 rules out blending.

Q3  Why do we use a different notation (e.g. FWFP) for incomplete dominance and codominance?

Capital vs. lowercase letters mean one allele is dominant and the other recessive. With incomplete dominance and codominance neither allele is dominant, so both get the same gene letter and are told apart by superscripts: FWFP, IAIB.

Q4  How does protein production and function relate to an individual’s phenotype?

Each allele is read to make a version of a protein (often an enzyme). The phenotype depends on how much working protein the cell has and what it does.

Two working copies make the full amount. If one working copy already makes enough, the heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote (complete dominance). If one copy makes only half the product, as with four o'clock pigment, the heterozygote is intermediate (incomplete dominance). If both alleles make working but different proteins, both show (codominance, like sugar A and sugar B in blood type AB, or both β-globin shapes in AS).

Guiding questions · Multiple alleles, pedigrees and epistasis

Q5  If each individual only has, at most, two unique alleles for a given gene, how can there be multiple alleles in the entire population?

Each person has two homologs, so two alleles of a gene; each gamete has one. But different people carry different pairs. Across the whole population many alleles can exist: ABO has 3 (IA, IB, i), β-globin about 500.

Q6  How do the 3 unique glycoprotein alleles in blood type correspond to 4 phenotypes and 6 different genotypes? For each genotype, what is the corresponding phenotype?

The alleles add sugars to glycoproteins on red blood cells: IA adds sugar A, IB adds sugar B, i adds nothing. IA and IB are codominant; both are completely dominant to i.

The 6 genotypes are every pair of the 3 alleles: IAIA and IAi → type A; IBIB and IBi → type B; IAIB → type AB; ii → type O. Two pairs of genotypes look alike because i is recessive, so 6 genotypes give 4 phenotypes.

Q7  Using the pedigree in the lecture slides, explain how you can use the information in a pedigree to determine the mode of inheritance.

Look for an affected child whose parents are both unaffected. That can only happen if both parents carry a hidden allele, so the trait is recessive; the affected child is aa and both parents are Aa carriers. In the lecture pedigree, unaffected parents in generation I have an affected son, and unaffected cousins in generation III have an affected son.

A dominant trait shows in every generation: every affected person has an affected parent. Recessive traits can skip generations and often show up after relatives have children together.

Q8  Explain how one gene can impact the expression of another gene (i.e. epistasis).

When two genes act in the same pathway, an early gene can block a later one. In ABO blood types the H gene makes the precursor (the stem) that the I gene adds sugars to. A person who is hh makes no stem, so the I alleles have nothing to attach sugar to and the blood looks type O, whatever the I genotype.

Epistasis changes the phenotype ratios (9 : 3 : 3 : 1 can become 13 : 3, as in the textbook's chickens) but not the genotype ratios.
