MODULE 3.5 · PART 1

Sex chromosomes

THE QUESTION Mendel's peas had matching chromosome pairs in both sexes. What changes when one pair doesn't match?

In this part: autosomes vs. sex chromosomes, how the X and Y came to differ, and how they move through meiosis.

TWO KINDS OF CHROMOSOME

Autosomes vs. sex chromosomes

Mendel's peas have no sex chromosomes: every pair matches in both sexes. Humans and fruit flies also have one unmatched pair, the sex chromosomes X and Y. Females are XX, males XY. Every other chromosome is an autosome.1,2

FEMALEXXTwo X chromosomes: a matching pair, just like an autosome pair.
MALEXYThe X is large with many genes; the Y is small with very few. They match only near the tips, and those matching regions let them pair in meiosis.
Check yourself: What are autosomes and sex chromosomes? How does inheritance of a gene on a sex chromosome differ?

Autosomes are the matched pairs found in both sexes; sex chromosomes (X, Y) differ between the sexes. A gene on the X is present twice in females but once in males, so its inheritance depends on sex: sons get their X only from their mother.

NOTATIONFor a gene on the X, write the allele on the X: XA. A male is XAY (no letter on the Y). Two genes on the same X: XAB.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Autosome

Any chromosome that isn't a sex chromosome. Autosomes come in matching pairs (22 in humans), the same in females and males.

Sex chromosome

A chromosome that differs between the sexes and helps determine sex: X and Y in humans and fruit flies.

X chromosome

The larger sex chromosome, with many genes. Females are XX, males XY.

Y chromosome

The smaller sex chromosome, with very few genes: a degraded relative of the X that still matches it at the tips, so X and Y pair in meiosis.

WHERE THE Y CAME FROM

X and Y are a homologous pair that drifted apart

How do we know X and Y are a homologous pair? Their DNA still matches near the tips, and that's why they pair in meiosis. Research in the last decade shows why they match: they evolved from an ordinary pair of autosomes.1,2

STEP THROUGH IT · 4 STAGES

Check yourself: How do we know the human X and Y chromosomes are a homologous pair?

They share DNA sequence near their tips, and those regions pair (and cross over) in meiosis, just as homologs do. The Y is a degraded version of the X: both evolved from one pair of autosomes.

THROUGH MEIOSIS

X and Y in meiosis: half the sperm carry X, half carry Y

X and Y pair and separate just like any homologous pair. That alone explains the 1 : 1 ratio of females to males at conception.1,2

Check yourself: Diagram the X and Y at metaphase I and metaphase II. What fraction of gametes carry each?

Metaphase I: X and Y paired across the middle, each with 2 chromatids. Metaphase II: in one cell an X lines up alone, in the other a Y. A male's gametes are 1/2 X and 1/2 Y; a female's are all X.

Lock it in · Part 1

  1. Autosomes: matching pairs in both sexes. Sex chromosomes: XX female, XY male.
  2. The X is large with many genes; the Y is small with very few. They match only at the tips, which lets them pair in meiosis.
  3. X and Y evolved from a pair of autosomes; the Y degraded after it gained a male-determining gene.
  4. In a male, X and Y separate in meiosis I: half the sperm are X, half Y, so offspring are 1 XX : 1 XY.
  5. Notation: XA for an allele on the X; XAY for a male; XAB for two genes on one X.

MODULE 3.5 · PART 2

X-linked inheritance

THE QUESTION What happens to a gene that sits on the X, which males have only one of?

In this part: fruit flies as a model organism, Morgan's white-eyed fly, his test cross, reciprocal crosses, X-linked traits in people, and Bridges' proof that genes are on chromosomes.

THE MODEL ORGANISM

Why fruit flies?

Mendel's peas had no sex chromosomes, so he could never have found X-linked inheritance. Thomas Hunt Morgan and his students used the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, the first genetic model organism.1,2

WHAT MAKES A GOOD MODEL

Small, easy, fast

Check yourself: What are some advantages of using fruit flies as a model organism in genetics?

They are small (millions in a room), easy to raise and cross (a male and a female in a vial with food breed on their own), and fast (about 2 weeks per generation, so P, F1 and F2 in about 6 weeks).

NAMINGFly geneticists name a gene after its mutant phenotype: Morgan's first mutant had white eyes, so the eye-color gene is called white, even though the common (wild-type) allele makes red eyes.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Drosophila melanogaster

The fruit fly: small, easy to raise and cross, with a 2-week generation. Morgan's lab used it to discover X-linkage and linkage.

Model organism

A species studied in depth because it is easy to raise and breed, with a short generation time, so its findings shed light on many other species.

THE WHITE-EYED FLY

Morgan's crosses: white eyes come back only in males

In 1910 Morgan found one white-eyed male in a bottle of red-eyed flies. His crosses gave a pattern Mendel's rules couldn't explain, until he put the gene on the X.2,3

STEP THROUGH IT · P TO F2

Red-eyed female × white-eyed male

F1All redEvery F1 fly has red eyes, so the white allele is recessive.

THE HYPOTHESISThe gene is on the X; the Y has no copySons get their only X from their mother. Daughters get one X from each parent. Write red XR, white Xr.
F1 × F1XRXr × XRYDaughters always get dad's XR: all red. Sons get mom's XR or Xr: 1 red : 1 white. Exactly what Morgan saw.
Check yourself: Do you think the first white mutant fly Morgan found was male or female?

Male. A male needs only one Xr to show white eyes. A female would need two copies, one from each parent, which is far less likely for a brand-new mutation.

FAMOUS EXPERIMENT · MORGAN, 1910The white-eyed fly gave the first experimental link between one gene and one chromosome. Morgan won the 1933 Nobel Prize for showing that chromosomes carry genes.Morgan, T. H. (1910). Science, 32(812), 120-122.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
X-linked

Describes a gene located on the X chromosome. Males have one copy, so an X-linked recessive allele always shows in males.

Sex-linkage

Inheritance of a gene that sits on a sex chromosome; its pattern differs between females and males and between reciprocal crosses.

TESTING THE IDEA

A second cross: white-eyed females appear

A good hypothesis predicts new results. If the gene is on the X, crossing an F1 female (XRXr) with a white male (XrY) should give white-eyed females.2,1

STEP THROUGH IT · THE TEST

THE RESULT1 red : 1 white in both sexesWhite females did appear, as predicted. So white isn't "male only": males just show it more easily.
Check yourself: Why do males (XY) show X-linked recessive diseases much more often than females (XX)?

A male has one X. If it carries the recessive allele, nothing masks it. A female needs the recessive allele on both X's; with one, her other X makes the working protein and she is a carrier.

THE TEST FOR X-LINKAGE

Reciprocal crosses: swap the sexes, compare the results

Mendel found that it didn't matter which parent was male. For autosomal genes that is true. For X-linked genes it isn't, and that difference is the test.1,2

STEP THROUGH IT · RABBITS, BROWN IS RECESSIVE

Two crosses, two possible answers

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 3
A RECIPROCAL CROSSSwap which sex has which traitCross 1: brown female × white male. Cross 2: white female × brown male. Both parents true-breeding.
IF AUTOSOMALSame result both waysbb × BB and BB × bb both give all Bb, white offspring.
IF X-LINKEDDifferent resultsCross 1 (XbXb × XBY): daughters white carriers, sons brown, because sons get mom's X. Cross 2 (XBXB × XbY): everyone white.
Check yourself: Why would a geneticist do a reciprocal cross? What does it tell us?

To find out whether a gene is X-linked. If swapping the sexes of the parents changes the offspring's phenotypes (especially by sex), the gene is on the X. If both crosses give the same result, it is autosomal.

Check yourself: Following Morgan's convention, what should we call this rabbit gene?

Name it after the mutant phenotype: the brown gene (white is the wild type).

Check yourself: Robber flies (class session): banded is dominant. Banded × unbanded gives 37 banded and 39 banded, 0 unbanded; the reciprocal cross gives 45 banded and 43 unbanded. Autosomal or X-linked?

X-linked. The two reciprocal crosses gave different results. Autosomal true-breeding crosses would give all banded both ways.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Reciprocal cross

A pair of crosses that swap which sex carries which trait (brown female × white male, and white female × brown male). Different results point to an X-linked gene.

IN PEOPLE

X-linked traits in humans

"The X chromosome is much larger than the Y and contains many more genes. A male inheriting an allele located on the X chromosome (from his mother) will express that trait." So far more males than females have X-linked disorders.1,2

ONE COPY VS. TWO

Why sons are affected more often

EXAMPLESRed-green color blindness (about 1 in 20 males), hemophilia (about 1 in 7000 males; famous in Queen Victoria's descendants), Duchenne muscular dystrophy, SCID.
COMMON MIX-UPA son never gets his X from his father: dad gives him the Y. So an X-linked allele passes from father to daughters only, and sons get theirs from mom.
GENES ARE ON CHROMOSOMES

Bridges' rare flies: the proof

Morgan's pattern showed that the white gene acts like the X. Calvin Bridges, a student in Morgan's lab, proved it is in the X, using rare mistakes in meiosis: nondisjunction.2,4

STEP THROUGH IT · 1 IN 2000

White female × red male

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 3
EXPECTEDRed daughters, white sonsXrXr × XRY gives XRXr daughters and XrY sons.

Check yourself: How did sex linkage support the idea that genes are on chromosomes?

Morgan showed the white gene is inherited exactly like the X chromosome. Bridges then showed that when the X chromosomes were inherited abnormally (nondisjunction), the eye color followed the abnormal X's. Genes travel with chromosomes because they are part of them: the chromosomal theory of inheritance.

FAMOUS EXPERIMENT · BRIDGES, 1916The first experimental evidence of nondisjunction, and the proof that a gene is physically on a chromosome. He started in Morgan's lab as a college freshman.Bridges, C. B. (1916). Genetics, 1(1), 1-52.

MODULE 3.5 · PART 3

Linkage: genes on the same chromosome

THE QUESTION If two genes share a chromosome, can they still assort independently?

In this part: linked genes, how crossing over makes recombinants, the testcross that detects linkage, allele arrangements, genetic maps, and genes that are linked and X-linked.

SAME CHROMOSOME

Linked genes: when independent assortment fails

Independent assortment works because separate chromosome pairs line up independently. But two genes on the same chromosome are on one DNA molecule. Unless something cuts between them, they travel together.1,2

INTERACTIVE · AaBb, TWO WAYS

Different chromosomes vs. the same one

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 2
DIFFERENT CHROMOSOMES4 kinds of gametes, 1/4 eachMendel's case: AB, Ab, aB, ab, because each pair faces either way at metaphase I.
SAME CHROMOSOMEOnly AB and ab, 1/2 eachWith no crossing over, A stays with B and a stays with b. Linkage = dependent assortment.
Check yourself: Why is linkage a violation of independent assortment? How does it change the gamete genotypes?

Independent assortment needs the two genes on separate chromosome pairs. Linked genes are on one chromosome, so the alleles on that chromosome go to the same gamete. Instead of four kinds at 1/4 each, the parental combinations are common and the new combinations rare (or absent).

NOTATIONLinked genes are written with a slash between the two homologs: AB/ab means A and B on one chromosome, a and b on the other. Unlinked genes are written AaBb.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Linkage (genetic linkage)

When two genes sit close together on the same chromosome and tend to be inherited together, breaking independent assortment.

CROSSING OVER

Recombination breaks up linkage, but only in some cells

Crossing over in prophase I swaps pieces between non-sister chromatids of a tetrad. If a crossover lands between A and B, it makes new combinations.2,1

STEP THROUGH IT · ONE GERM CELL, THEN MANY

Parental (P) or recombinant (R)?

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 4
THE GERM CELLAB/ab, after SA and B are on the pink chromosome, a and b on the blue one.
NO CROSSOVER BETWEEN A AND BAll 4 gametes parentalAB, AB, ab, ab: the same combinations as the parent's chromosomes.
A CROSSOVER BETWEEN A AND B2 parental, 2 recombinantTwo non-sister chromatids swap ends: Ab and aB are new. The other two chromatids are untouched.
ACROSS MANY CELLSParental common, recombinant rareMost cells have no crossover in that stretch, so most gametes are parental.
Check yourself: Which parts of a homologous pair engage in crossing over?

Non-sister chromatids: one chromatid from each homolog, inside a tetrad (bivalent) in prophase I. Sister chromatids don't exchange with each other.

Check yourself: How do you know which gametes or offspring are parental and which are recombinant?

Parental = the allele combinations on the parent's own chromosomes (for AB/ab, AB and ab). Recombinant = new combinations (Ab, aB). The parental types are also the more common ones.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Recombination

The making of new allele combinations. For linked genes it happens by crossing over between non-sister chromatids in prophase I.

Parental type

A gamete or offspring with the same combination of alleles as one of the parent's chromosomes. Also called non-recombinant.

Non-recombinant type

Another name for the parental type: an allele combination unchanged by crossing over.

Recombinant type

A gamete or offspring with a new combination of alleles, made by a crossover between the two genes.

Bivalent

A pair of replicated homologous chromosomes joined in prophase I of meiosis (2 chromosomes, 4 chromatids). Same as a tetrad.

Tetrad

Another name for a bivalent: the four chromatids of a homologous pair joined in prophase I. Tetrads form in prophase I and separate in anaphase I.

Chiasma

The X-shaped spot where two non-sister chromatids have crossed over (plural chiasmata).

TEST FOR IT

The breeding experiment: is it 1 : 1 : 1 : 1?

How can you tell if two genes are on the same chromosome? (1) Cross AABB × aabb to get AaBb F1s. (2) Testcross: AaBb × aabb. (3) The aabb parent only gives ab, so each offspring shows the F1's gamete directly. Unlinked: 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. Linked: mostly parental, few recombinant.1,2,3

REAL DATA · STURTEVANT'S FLIES

Two X-linked genes: white and crossveinless

Check yourself: Why does a ratio other than 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 in a testcross of a double heterozygote indicate linkage?

1 : 1 : 1 : 1 means the F1 made all four gametes equally: independent assortment. If two classes (the parental ones) are much bigger than the other two, the alleles traveled together: the genes are linked.

Check yourself: Sturtevant's counts: what fraction of the sons are recombinant?

(52 + 45) / (357 + 341 + 52 + 45) = 97 / 795, about 12.2%. That's the map distance between white and crossveinless.

FAMOUS EXPERIMENT · STURTEVANT, 1913As an undergraduate in Morgan's lab, Alfred Sturtevant realized recombination frequency could measure distance, and drew the first genetic map.Sturtevant, A. H. (1913). Journal of Experimental Zoology, 14, 43-59.
WHICH ALLELES SIT TOGETHER

AB/ab vs. Ab/aB: same genes, different arrangement

A double heterozygote for linked genes can carry its alleles two ways. The arrangement decides which gametes are parental.1

SIDE BY SIDE

Two arrangements of AaBb

Check yourself: A plant is heterozygous for two linked genes. Which arrangements are possible: Aa/Bb, Ab/aB, Ab/Ba, AB/ab?

Ab/aB and AB/ab. Each side of the slash is one chromosome, so it must carry one allele of each gene (one A-gene allele and one B-gene allele). Aa/Bb puts both A alleles on one chromosome; Ab/Ba lists the second chromosome's genes in the wrong order (always write the same gene first on both).

Check yourself: An individual is Ab/aB. With no recombination, which crosses could have produced it: AABB × aabb, AaBb × AaBb, or AABb × aaBb?

It got an Ab chromosome from one parent and aB from the other. AaBb × AaBb could give that, and so could AABb × aaBb: the AABb parent can pass an Ab chromosome and the aaBb parent an aB. AABB × aabb can only give AB/ab.

GENETIC MAPS

Distance = how often two genes recombine

The farther apart two genes are, the more room there is for a crossover between them, so the more often they recombine. Morgan's group used that as a ruler: 1 map unit = 1% recombination. Calculating distances is beyond this course; reading a map isn't.2,1

Find the two genes and their distance. Parental combinations (those on the parent's chromosomes) are common; recombinant ones are rare, and rarer the closer the genes are. The recombinant total is about the map distance in percent.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Genetic distance

How far apart two genes are, measured by how often they recombine: 1 map unit = 1% recombinant gametes.

Linkage mapping

Ordering genes along a chromosome from their recombination frequencies; the result is a genetic map.

BOTH AT ONCE

Can two genes be linked and sex-linked?

Yes: two genes close together on the X are linked (same chromosome) and X-linked (that chromosome is the X). Sons show their mother's X directly, which is exactly how Sturtevant could read recombinants off the sons.1,2

TWO GENES ON THE FLY X

Eye color and eye shape

Check yourself: This female mates with a recessive male, Xr ec⁻Y. Which offspring are recombinant: Xr ec⁻Y, XR ec⁺Xr ec⁻, XR ec⁺Y, Xr ec⁻Xr ec⁻?

All of them. Each one got a recombinant X from mom (R ec⁺ or r ec⁻). The father only adds Xr ec⁻ or Y, which doesn't change what mom's X was.

COMMON MIX-UPLinked = two genes near each other on any one chromosome. X-linked = one gene on the X. Different words; a pair of genes can be both.

Lock it in · Part 3

  1. Linked genes are close on one chromosome and tend to be inherited together: they break independent assortment.
  2. Crossing over between non-sister chromatids (prophase I) makes recombinant gametes, but only in cells with a crossover between the genes.
  3. Parental types are common, recombinant types rare. Which is which depends on the arrangement: AB/ab vs. Ab/aB.
  4. Test for linkage: AaBb × aabb. 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 = unlinked; mostly parental = linked.
  5. 1 map unit = 1% recombination. Farther apart = more recombination. Short distances add.
  6. Genes can be linked and X-linked at the same time.

Y chromosome The smaller sex chromosome, with very few genes: a degraded relative of the X that still matches it at the tips, so X and Y pair in meiosis.

Guiding questions · Sex chromosomes and X-linkage

Q1  What are autosomes? What are sex chromosomes? How does inheritance of genes located on sex chromosomes differ from inheritance of genes on autosomes?

Autosomes are the chromosomes that come in matching pairs in both sexes (22 pairs in humans). Sex chromosomes differ between the sexes: XX in females, XY in males.

A gene on an autosome has two copies in everyone and is inherited the same way from either parent. A gene on the X has two copies in females but one in males, and a son always gets his X from his mother, so the pattern depends on sex and reciprocal crosses give different results.

Q2  How do we know the human X and Y chromosomes are a homologous pair?

The X and Y share DNA sequence near their tips, and those regions let them pair (and cross over) in meiosis, just as homologs do. They are similar because they evolved from one pair of autosomes; the Y is a degraded version of the X that gained a gene triggering male development.

Q3  What is the notation for 2 genes both located on the X chromosome?

Write the alleles as superscripts on the X, one after the other: for one gene XA; for two genes on the same X, XAB. A male is written XABY; a female XABXab.

Q4  What are some advantages of using fruit flies as a model organism in genetics?

Fruit flies are small (millions fit in one room), easy to raise and cross (a male and a female in a vial with food breed on their own) and fast (about 2 weeks per generation, so P, F1 and F2 in about 6 weeks).

Q5  Describe how TH Morgan figured out the white gene (that codes for eye color in fruit flies) was located on the X chromosome. To do this, trace the logic of the crosses he did.

Morgan crossed a true-breeding red-eyed female with his white-eyed mutant male: all F1 were red, so white is recessive. Crossing the F1 gave an F2 in which all females were red and males were 1 red : 1 white: white came back only in males.

He proposed the gene is on the X and the Y has no copy. Then sons get their only X from their mother, which explains every result: P cross XRXR × XrY gives red daughters (XRXr) and red sons; F1 cross XRXr × XRY gives all red daughters and 1 red : 1 white sons.

He tested it: an F1 female (XRXr) × a white male (XrY) gave red and white in a 1 : 1 ratio in both sexes, including white-eyed females, exactly as predicted.

Q6  Why would a geneticist do a reciprocal cross? What does it tell us?

A reciprocal cross swaps which sex carries which trait (dominant female × recessive male, then recessive female × dominant male). If both crosses give the same offspring, the gene is autosomal. If they differ, especially between sons and daughters, the gene is on the X.

Q7  Why do males (individuals who have XY sex chromosomes) exhibit X linked recessive diseases at a much higher frequency than females (individuals with two X chromosomes)?

A male has only one X. If that X carries the recessive allele, there is no second copy to mask it, so he shows the trait. A female needs the recessive allele on both X's; with one copy she has a working allele on her other X and is an unaffected carrier.

Guiding questions · Linkage and maps

These are the questions this module is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q8  Explain why linkage is a violation of independent assortment, and how that affects the frequency of the resulting gamete genotypes.

Independent assortment depends on genes being on different chromosome pairs, which line up independently in metaphase I. Linked genes are on the same chromosome, one DNA molecule, so the alleles on that chromosome go into the same gamete.

So instead of four gamete types at 1/4 each, the parental combinations are common and the recombinant combinations rare.

Q9  If two genes are linked and are very close together on the same chromosome, they exhibit dependent assortment. How does dependent assortment affect the gamete genotypes, compared to independent assortment?

Very close linked genes almost never have a crossover between them, so a double heterozygote makes almost only the two parental gamete types (for AB/ab: AB and ab, about 1/2 each), instead of the four equal types (1/4 each) of independent assortment.

Q10  Which parts of a homologous pair engage in crossing over?

Non-sister chromatids: one chromatid from each homolog of a tetrad (bivalent), in prophase I. Sister chromatids don't exchange with each other.

Q11  How do you know which gametes or offspring should be designated as parental and which gametes should be designated as recombinant?

Parental (non-recombinant) types carry the same allele combinations as the parent's own chromosomes; recombinant types carry new combinations made by crossing over.

For AB/ab the parental types are AB and ab and the recombinants Ab and aB; for Ab/aB it's the reverse. Parental types are also the more common ones.

Q12  Describe a breeding experiment you could do to determine if two genes are linked.

Cross a homozygous dominant with a homozygous recessive (AABB × aabb) to get AaBb F1s. Testcross an F1 with aabb. Because aabb only gives ab gametes, each offspring shows the F1 gamete it got. If the four classes aren't about 1 : 1 : 1 : 1, the genes are on the same chromosome.

Q13  Why does a ratio of something other than 1:1:1:1 in a test cross of a double heterozygote (when tracing the inheritance of two genes) indicate linkage?

A double heterozygote whose genes assort independently makes AB, Ab, aB and ab gametes equally, so a testcross gives 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. If two classes (the parental ones) are much bigger than the other two, the alleles traveled together: the genes are linked. Sturtevant's flies: 357 and 341 parental vs. 52 and 45 recombinant.

Q14  How does linkage notation work? Why do we use a different notation with linked genes?

A slash separates the two homologs, and each side lists the alleles on one chromosome in the same gene order: AB/ab means A and B on one chromosome, a and b on the other; Ab/aB means A with b and a with B.

We need it because for linked genes the arrangement matters: it decides which gametes are parental (common) and which recombinant (rare). Writing AaBb would hide that.

Q15  How do we use information on a genetic map? How do you use a genetic map to determine whether a specific gamete or offspring phenotype will be common or rare?

A genetic map shows genes in order along a chromosome, with distances in map units: 1 map unit = 1% recombination. Close genes rarely recombine; far-apart genes recombine more often.

To predict a gamete or offspring: find the two genes, work out the parental combinations from the parent's chromosomes (those are common), and the recombinant ones (rare, about the map distance in percent in total). For AE/ae, AE and ae are common and Ae and aE rare.

Q16  Can two genes be linked and sex linked at the same time?

Yes. Two genes close together on the X chromosome are both linked (same chromosome) and X-linked (that chromosome is the X), like the fly's white and crossveinless genes.
