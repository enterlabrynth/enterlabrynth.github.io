MODULE 2.3 · PART 1

What DNA is made of

THE QUESTIONWhat carries the genetic information, and how is that molecule built?

Seven steps: the two experiments that showed DNA is the genetic material, the nucleotide (and DNA's sugar vs. RNA's), phosphodiester bonds, base pairing, the building blocks as cards, and the antiparallel double helix.

STEP 1 · THE BIG PICTURE · EXPERIMENT 1

What is the genetic material?

Every cell has the same instructions for making its proteins: its chromosomes. Every time a cell divides, those instructions have to be copied.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
WHAT IS THE GENETIC MATERIAL?Early experiments pointed to DNAGenetic material is passed from parent to offspring (it's heritable) and determines an organism's characteristics (its phenotype). Is it DNA, RNA, carbohydrate or protein? Tap through the experiments.
1 · LIVE SSmooth colonies, pathogenicStreptococcus comes in two forms. The S (smooth) strain has a capsule and forms smooth, shiny colonies. It's pathogenic: it causes disease.
2 · LIVE RRough colonies, harmlessThe R (rough) strain has no capsule and forms rough, dry colonies. It's non-pathogenic.
3 · HEAT-KILLED SDead cells do no harmKill the S cells with heat and they're no longer pathogenic. So far, no surprises.
4 · HEAT-KILLED S + LIVE RSomething transforms R into SMix dead S with live R and the mix is pathogenic, and live S cells are recovered. Some molecule in the dead S cells transformed the R cells: the transforming factor, likely the genetic material. Which molecule is it? That's the next experiment.
THE CENTRAL DOGMA
Information flows DNA → RNA → protein. Genes are transcribed to RNA; RNA is translated to protein; proteins do the work in the cell. DNA is copied by replication.
YOUR GENOME
23 pairs of chromosomes, each a double-stranded DNA polymer of millions of nucleotides, with hundreds to thousands of genes. About 3 billion base pairs: 1 to 3 meters of DNA in each cell. (Bacteria have one chromosome.)
STEP 2 · EXPERIMENT 2

What is the transforming factor?

Three hypotheses, three enzymes. Destroy one molecule at a time, and the one that stops transformation is the genetic material.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
EXPERIMENT 2 · AVERY, McCARTY, MacLEODWhat is the transforming factor?Griffith showed that some molecule in heat-killed S cells can transform rough R bacteria into smooth S bacteria. Was it DNA, RNA, carbohydrate or protein?The test: does transformation require RNA, DNA or protein? That's three hypotheses. Take the dead S extract, destroy one molecule with an enzyme, mix in live R cells, and see if S cells still appear.
1 · PROTEASE DESTROYS PROTEINStill transforms: protein is ruled outWith the protein cut up, the extract still has its RNA and DNA. The R cells are still transformed into S: the S information still gets through. So protein isn't the transforming factor.
2 · RNase DESTROYS RNAStill transforms: RNA is ruled outWith the RNA cut up, the extract still has its protein and DNA. The R cells are still transformed into S. So RNA isn't it either.
3 · DNase DESTROYS DNANo transformation: it's DNAWith the DNA cut up, the protein and RNA are still there, but nothing transforms. Only rough R cells grow. Without the DNA, the S information never reaches the R cells.Treatment with DNase abolishes transformation ability.
4 · PURIFIED DNADNA alone does itFurther experiment: purified DNA, with no protein or RNA, is able to transform the R strain.DNA is the transforming factor: DNA is the genetic material, for every living cell on the planet.

THE LOGIC
An enzyme removes one suspect. If transformation still happens, that molecule wasn't needed, so it's not the transforming factor. If transformation stops, you've found it.
If RNA were the genetic material, which mix would still give live S bacteria: heat-killed S + live R treated with DNase, or with RNase?

DNase. DNase destroys DNA, not RNA, so if RNA carried the information, the RNA would survive and transform the R cells. (In reality it's the RNase mix that still transforms, because DNA is the genetic material.)

STEP 3 · THE MOLECULE

The nucleotide, and DNA's sugar vs. RNA's

DNA and RNA are built from almost the same parts. One oxygen on the sugar makes the difference, and gives each its name.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
WHAT IS A NUCLEOTIDE?Three parts: a phosphate, a sugar and a baseDNA is a polymer of nucleotides. Each one has a phosphate, a five-carbon sugar and a nitrogenous base. Tap each part.
1 · THE SUGARDeoxyribose, carbons 1′ to 5′The sugar's five carbons are numbered with primes: 1′ ("one prime") to 5′. Those numbers name the ends of a DNA strand later: the 5′ end and the 3′ end.Watch the 3′ carbon's OH: that's where the next nucleotide attaches.
2 · THE PHOSPHATEOn the 5′ carbonThe phosphate hangs off the 5′ carbon. In the strand, it links this sugar to the 3′ OH of the sugar before it.
3 · THE BASEOn the 1′ carbon: A, T, G or CThe base hangs off the 1′ carbon. DNA has four: A (adenine), G (guanine), C (cytosine) and T (thymine). The information is in the sequence of the bases.A and G are purines (two rings); C and T are pyrimidines (one ring).

DNA VS. RNADeoxyribose and ribose

THE NAMES
Deoxyribose has no OH on the 2′ carbon ("de-oxy"). Its nucleotides are deoxyribonucleotides, and the polymer is deoxyribonucleic acid: DNA. Ribose keeps the OH: ribonucleotides, ribonucleic acid, RNA.
COMMON MIX-UP
The difference between DNA and RNA nucleotides is the 2′ carbon, not the 3′. Both sugars have the 3′ OH that the next nucleotide attaches to.
How is a DNA nucleotide different from an RNA nucleotide?

Its sugar is deoxyribose (H on the 2′ carbon) instead of ribose (OH on the 2′ carbon), and it uses T where RNA uses U.

STEP 4 · THE MOLECULE

Phosphodiester bonds: building a strand 5′ → 3′

Nucleotides are joined sugar to phosphate, and every strand ends up with a direction.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 3
1 · A NUCLEOTIDE TRIPHOSPHATE ARRIVESdNTPs bring three phosphatesNew nucleotides arrive as dNTPs (deoxynucleoside triphosphates): each carries three phosphates on its 5′ carbon. It joins the growing strand at the last sugar's 3′ OH.
2 · THE PHOSPHODIESTER BONDThe energy comes from the incoming nucleotide's phosphatesThe innermost phosphate bonds to the 3′ OH: a phosphodiester bond (sugar–O–P–O–sugar), the link in the sugar-phosphate backbone. The other two phosphates leave as pyrophosphate.Breaking those phosphates off releases the energy that makes the bond. The energy comes from the phosphate groups on the incoming nucleotide, not from a separate ATP.
3 · EVERY STRAND HAS A DIRECTIONA 5′ end and a 3′ endOne end of a strand has a free phosphate on a 5′ carbon (the 5′ end); the other has the free 3′ OH (the 3′ end). New nucleotides join only at the 3′ end, so every strand grows 5′ → 3′.That's the one absolute rule in this module: leading strands, lagging strands and telomeres all follow from it.
SAME FOR RNA
RNA's nucleotides are joined by the same phosphodiester bonds, paid for the same way (by the incoming NTP's phosphates), and RNA also grows 5′ → 3′.
WHO MAKES THE BOND?
In DNA replication, DNA polymerase catalyzes it. It needs a template, dNTPs, and a free 3′ OH to add onto.
What would happen if a nucleotide missing its 3′ OH were added to a growing strand?

The strand would stop growing. The next nucleotide has nowhere to attach: new nucleotides only join a free 3′ OH.

STEP 5 · THE MOLECULE

Base pairing: a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine

Why A goes with T and G with C comes down to space, and then to hydrogen bonds.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
1 · PURINES AND PYRIMIDINESTwo sizes of basePurines (A, G) have two rings; pyrimidines (C, T) have one. A trick: the pure-As-Gold purines are the big ones.
2 · PURINE + PYRIMIDINEJust rightThe two backbones of the helix stay a fixed distance apart. One big base plus one small base fills that space exactly, so the helix is the same width all the way along.
3 · PURINE + PURINEToo muchTwo big bases are too wide to fit between the backbones. So G can't pair with G (or with A).
4 · PYRIMIDINE + PYRIMIDINEToo littleTwo small bases leave a gap: they're too far apart to hydrogen bond. So C can't pair with T.
5 · THE RIGHT PARTNERA with T, G with CAmong the purine-pyrimidine pairs, only the right partner lines up its hydrogen bonds: A–T makes two, G–C makes three. So G pairs with C, not with T.That's why the amounts match: A = T and G = C in any DNA (Watson-Crick).
HOW THE TWO STRANDS HOLD TOGETHER
Hydrogen bonds between paired bases hold the two strands together. Within each strand, nucleotides are joined by covalent phosphodiester bonds. RNA is usually a single strand, though it can pair with itself in places.
Why can't G pair with G?

Two purines (two rings each) are too wide to fit between the backbones. A pair has to be one purine and one pyrimidine, and G's partner is C (3 hydrogen bonds).

STEP 7 · THE MOLECULE

Two antiparallel strands

Put it together: two strands, paired base to base, running in opposite directions.

The two strands are complementary: knowing one strand's sequence tells you the other's. And they're antiparallel: where one strand has its 5′ end, its partner has its 3′ end.

That structure suggested how DNA could be copied: pull the strands apart, and each one is a template for a new partner.

ALWAYS LABEL THE ENDS
Write 5′ and 3′ on every strand you draw. If one strand's ends are labeled, the partner's are the opposite.
THE INFORMATION
The information is in the sequence of the bases. Each chromosome is one long double-stranded DNA; each gene is hundreds to thousands of nucleotides of it.
MODULE 2.3 · PART 2

DNA replication

THE QUESTIONHow does a cell copy about 3 billion base pairs, quickly and accurately, every time it divides?

Six steps: semiconservative copying, the primer as a docking site (and the one exception), the replication bubble, one fork step by step, the seven-protein crew, and the end problem (telomerase).

STEP 1 · COPYING DNA

Each strand is a template: semiconservative replication

The structure itself suggests how to copy it. Blue is always the parent (old) strand, orange the daughter (new) one.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
1 · THE PARENT DNATwo old strandsBefore a cell divides, it must copy its chromosomes so each new cell gets an exact copy. Start with the parent double helix: two strands, hydrogen bonded base to base. Parent strands are blue on this page.
2 · THE STRANDS SEPARATEEach strand becomes a templateBreak the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs and the helix comes apart. Each single strand can now serve as a template.
3 · BUILD ON EACH TEMPLATEComplementary base pairingFree nucleotides pair with each template by complementary base pairing (A with T, G with C) and are joined into a new sugar-phosphate backbone. New, daughter strands are orange on this page.
4 · TWO IDENTICAL COPIESOne old strand, one new strandThe original molecule has been copied. Each copy has one strand from the parent and one new daughter strand, and the two copies are identical.That's called semiconservative replication: each copy conserves half of the original.

WHICH MODEL?Conservative, semiconservative or dispersive

PARENT VS. DAUGHTER
Parent (template) strand: the old strand being read. Daughter strand: the new strand being built on it. After replication each double helix has one of each.
FAST AND ACCURATE
About 3 billion base pairs to copy every time a cell divides, and DNA polymerase is very fast and very accurate.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
The three models, and the experiment that settled it: Meselson and Stahl grew bacteria on heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N), moved them to light nitrogen (¹⁴N), and weighed the DNA. After one round every molecule was half heavy, half light; after two, half were all light. Only semiconservative replication fits.
STEP 2 · COPYING DNA

The primer: DNA polymerase's docking site

DNA polymerase can only add onto something. Watch what happens on a bare template, and how primase gets around it.

DNA polymerase needs three things: a template to read, dNTPs to build with, and a primer with a free 3′ OH to add onto. It can't start a strand from nothing.

Primase makes the primer: a short stretch of RNA, base-paired to the template. Making RNA is the one exception to "needs a primer": RNA polymerase (transcription, next module) starts new strands without one.

ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
Primase is itself a kind of RNA polymerase, which is why it can start the primer from nothing.

The leading strand needs a primer once. The lagging strand needs one for every Okazaki fragment.

THE ABSOLUTE
New strands always grow 5′ → 3′, DNA and RNA alike: each nucleotide is added to the 3′ end. The polymerase reads the template 3′ → 5′. When a question gives you a strand, write 5′ and 3′ on it.
What would happen if primase were missing?

No primers, so no free 3′ OH, so DNA polymerase couldn't start any new strand: no leading strand, no Okazaki fragments, no replication.

PRIMERS ARE RNA
Made of ribonucleotides (ribose, and U instead of T). That's how they get recognized and removed later: DNA polymerase I replaces them with DNA.
STEP 3 · COPYING DNA

The replication bubble: leading and lagging strands

Watch the whole bubble get built, protein by protein. Blue: parent. Orange: daughter. Pink: RNA primer.

  1. Topoisomerase relieves the twisting forces ahead of each fork.
  2. Helicase opens the double helix by breaking the hydrogen bonds. The strands separate into a replication bubble with a fork at each side, moving apart (bidirectional): 2 helicases per bubble.
  3. SSBPs (single-strand DNA-binding proteins) hold the single strands apart so they don't pair back up.
  4. Primase lays an RNA primer on each template at the origin, and one near each fork for the first Okazaki fragment.
  5. DNA polymerase III adds DNA 5′ → 3′. On each template, the half whose 5′ → 3′ heads toward a fork is a leading strand: one primer, made continuously.
  6. Lagging strands point away from their fork, so they're made in pieces: a new primer near the fork, then another Okazaki fragment. A leading and a lagging polymerase at each fork: 4 DNA polymerases per bubble.
  7. DNA polymerase I removes the RNA primers and replaces them with DNA, still 5′ → 3′.
  8. DNA ligase closes the gaps in the sugar-phosphate backbone. Each new double helix has one parent strand and one daughter strand.
WHICH IS LEADING?
Find the template's 3′ end. The new strand grows 5′ → 3′, so it runs toward the template's 5′ end. If that's toward the fork, it's the leading strand; away from the fork, lagging.
HOW MANY PRIMERS?
Leading strand: one primer (at the origin). Lagging strand: one per Okazaki fragment, laid down near the fork, not at the origin.
COMMON MIX-UP
"Primers are always at the origin" is only true for the first ones. Every Okazaki fragment on the lagging strand needs its own primer, wherever the fork has reached.
STEP 4 · COPYING DNA

At one fork: leading and lagging strand synthesis

Zoom in on one fork. This is the order on the slides.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 7
1 · OPENED, UNWOUND AND PRIMEDTopoisomerase, helicase, SSBPs, primaseHelicase opens the double helix by breaking the hydrogen bonds between bases. Ahead of it, topoisomerase relieves the twisting forces. Single-strand DNA-binding proteins (SSBPs) stabilize the single strands. Primase synthesizes an RNA primer.
2 · THE LEADING STRANDOne primer, continuousDNA polymerase extends the primer 5′ → 3′, toward the fork. As the fork keeps opening, it just keeps going: one continuous leading strand.
3 · LAGGING STRAND: A PRIMERPrimase lays a primer near the forkOn the other template, 5′ → 3′ points away from the fork. So each time the fork opens up a new stretch, primase lays a new RNA primer near the fork.
4 · AN OKAZAKI FRAGMENTDNA polymerase docks on the primer and builds away from the forkDNA polymerase docks on the primer's 3′ OH (held on by a sliding clamp) and builds 5′ → 3′ until it reaches the previous piece: an Okazaki fragment.
5 · ANOTHER PRIMER, ANOTHER FRAGMENTWhich fragment came first?As the fork moves, primase and DNA polymerase make another Okazaki fragment, nearer the fork. So the fragment farthest from the fork was made first, and each fragment's RNA primer sits at its 5′ end.
6 · RNA OUT, DNA INDNA polymerase replaces the primerDNA polymerase removes the ribonucleotides of the primer and replaces them with deoxyribonucleotides, still working 5′ → 3′.It doesn't go backwards: it keeps extending the previous fragment's 3′ end, taking out the RNA primer just ahead of it (that primer is the next fragment's 5′ end).
7 · LIGASE SEALS THE GAPOne continuous strandWhen DNA polymerase reaches the next fragment, one bond is still missing: a gap (a nick) in the sugar-phosphate backbone. DNA polymerase can only add a nucleotide to a free 3′ OH; it can't join that 3′ OH to the next fragment's 5′ phosphate.DNA ligase makes that last phosphodiester bond, joining the fragments into one lagging strand.
WHY TWO WAYS?
The strands are antiparallel and synthesis only goes 5′ → 3′. So one new strand is made continuously (leading) and the other discontinuously (lagging).
WHERE EACH PRIMER SITS
At the 5′ end of its Okazaki fragment (and of the leading strand). Primers are RNA, so they're all removed and replaced with DNA later.
COMMON MIX-UP
Nothing in replication runs 3′ → 5′. Swapping a primer for DNA is done by DNA polymerase (not RNA polymerase), still 5′ → 3′; the lagging strand only looks backwards because its pieces are made away from the fork.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
All the proteins working together at a fork (helicase, primase, the polymerases, the clamp, SSBPs) are often called the replisome, the replication machine.

REPLICATION CREW · 7 / 77

Telomerase

THE END KEEPER
At the ends of linear chromosomes
POWERCarries its own RNA template and extends the 3′ end of the chromosome, so the last primer's gap can be filled.
IF IT'S MISSINGChromosomes get a little shorter every time they're copied.
THE ORDER AT A FORK
Topoisomerase (untwist ahead) → helicase (unzip) → SSBPs (hold apart) → primase (primer) → DNA polymerase (build, then swap primers for DNA) → ligase (seal). Telomerase only at the ends.
REPLISOME VS. SPLICEOSOME
Replisome (a textbook word, not on the slides): the replication machine at a fork (helicase, primase, DNA polymerases and their helpers) that copies DNA. Spliceosome: an RNA/protein complex that cuts introns out of a new mRNA and joins the exons (RNA processing, after transcription, next module). Both are big complexes; one copies DNA, the other edits RNA.
EXONS VS. INTRONS
A new eukaryotic mRNA has exons and introns. Introns are removed by the spliceosome; exons are spliced back together into the mature mRNA that the ribosome reads. Ex-ons are ex-pressed; in-trons stay in the nucleus.
Which protein's absence would leave the lagging strand in pieces?

DNA ligase. Without it the Okazaki fragments are never joined: gaps stay in the backbone.

STEP 6 · COPYING DNA

The end problem, and telomerase

Why the very ends of a linear chromosome can't be finished normally, and the enzyme that fixes it. Watch it first, then step through it.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
1 · THE VERY END IS A PROBLEMThe last primerOn the lagging strand, the last Okazaki fragment starts from an RNA primer laid down right at the end of the template.
2 · A GAP NOTHING CAN FILLThe template's 3′ overhangPrimers are RNA, and they're removed. Removing the last one leaves a gap at the end, opposite the template's 3′ overhang. No new primer can go beyond the end, so there's no 3′ OH to fill it from.That piece is lost every round: without help, the chromosome would get shorter every time it's copied.
3 · TELOMERASE EXTENDS THE 3′ ENDIt brings its own RNA templateTelomerase carries its own RNA template. It binds the overhang and extends the template's 3′ end, working repetitively to make a long extension (the telomere).
4 · FILL IT INPrime it like normalNow there's template beyond the gap. Primase lays an RNA primer on the extension, and DNA polymerase completes the lagging strand. The telomere just has to be long enough that no part of a gene is lost.
5 · BOTH ENDSEvery linear chromosome needs itBoth ends of a linear chromosome have this problem, so telomerase works at both, always extending a 3′ end. Organisms with linear chromosomes need telomerase activity; bacteria, with one circular chromosome, don't have ends.
DNA replication at the ends of linear chromosomes needs telomerase because…

removing the last RNA primer on the lagging strand leaves a recessed end that DNA polymerase can't fill. (The clicker answer, b.)

WHICH END?
Telomerase extends the 3′ end of the template strand, the overhang. Label it on any drawing.
