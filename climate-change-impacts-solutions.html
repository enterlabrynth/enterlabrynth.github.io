What climate change is already doing

THE QUESTION The planet has warmed by about one degree. What has that actually changed for species, for ecosystems, and for people?

Eight steps: what is visible now, the ocean's producers, phenology, three ways to respond, a range shift in data, local extinctions, coral reefs, and human health.

Module 2.5 showed that the climate is changing and why. This module is about what that is doing to living things, including us, and what can be done.

STEP 2 · THE OCEAN'S PRODUCERS

Why ocean NPP is falling

Phytoplankton do almost half of the planet's photosynthesis (Module 2.3). Warming does not hurt them by being too hot. It cuts off their supply.

INTERACTIVE · THE SAME OCEAN, COOLER AND WARMER

What stratification does

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 2
sunlight reaches only the surfaceDEEP WATER · cold, dark, rich in nutrientsSURFACESURFACE · warmer, lightermixing brings
nutrients upwarm water floats on top: little mixingnutrients stay down here
A COOLER OCEAN · WELL MIXEDNutrients reach the lightPhytoplankton need two things: light, which is only at the surface, and nutrients, which are mostly in the deep water. When the surface and the deep are not too different in temperature, the water mixes, and nutrients are carried up to where the light is.
A WARMER OCEAN · STRATIFIEDLight on top, nutrients below, nothing connecting themWarm water is lighter than cold water. As the surface warms it floats more firmly on top, and the ocean separates into layers. This is vertical stratification.Less mixing means fewer nutrients reach the sunlit surface. Phytoplankton run short, grow less, and ocean NPP goes down. Everything that eats them has less to eat.
THE CHAIN OF CAUSE AND EFFECT

Five links

1Surface water warms
The ocean absorbs most of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases.
2Layers form
Warm, light water sits on top of cold, dense, nutrient-rich water.
3Mixing weakens
Less water moves between the layers.
4Nutrients stay deep
Fewer reach the sunlit surface.
5NPP falls
Phytoplankton are nutrient-limited, so they grow less.
CONNECT IT This is the limiting nutrient idea from Module 2.3 in reverse. There, adding a limiting nutrient raised NPP. Here, warming takes the supply away, and NPP drops.
STEP 3 · THE CALENDAR IS MOVING

Phenology: the timing of life

Phenology is the timing of biological events: when flowers open, when birds migrate, when insects emerge, when lakes freeze. Many of those are set by temperature, so as the climate warms, the calendar shifts.

DATA · CHERRY BLOSSOM IN KYOTO, JAPAN · 800 TO 2016

People in Kyoto have recorded the date of peak cherry blossom for more than 1,200 years. For most of that time the trend stayed within a band of about a week. It is now earlier than at any time in the record.

A SECOND EXAMPLE · LAKE ICE

Lakes that stop freezing

With 2 °C of warming · lakes that no longer freeze every winter
With 8 °C of warming

Across the Northern Hemisphere, lakes that have reliably frozen every winter are projected to stop doing so, starting along the southern edge of the frozen zone and spreading north as warming increases.

Bars show the pattern only. They are not measured values.

WHY TIMING MATTERS

Not everything shifts together

Species respond to different cues. If a flower opens earlier but its pollinator does not arrive earlier, or chicks hatch after the insects they eat have peaked, partners that depend on each other can fall out of step.

STEP 4 · WHAT CAN A SPECIES DO?

Three possible responses

Every species affected by climate change faces the same short list of options.

INTERACTIVE · MOVE, ADJUST, OR NEITHER

The three responses, and what each one needs

Tap a button to explore

RESPONSE 1 · MOVEFollow the climate you are suited toA population can shift its range to where conditions still suit it: toward the poles, or up a mountain.It requires: enough time, the ability to disperse, and contiguous habitat to move through. A species boxed in by cities and farmland, or one that cannot travel far, may not be able to.
RESPONSE 2 · STAY, BUT ADJUSTChange yourself instead of your addressThere are two very different ways to adjust in place.Plasticity: individuals change their behavior or physiology within their own lifetimes. Fast, but limited, and nothing genetic changes.Adaptation: the population evolves by natural selection over generations. It needs genetic variation and enough generations, so it is slow for long-lived species.
IF A SPECIES CAN DO NEITHERExtinctionA species that cannot move fast enough and cannot adjust fast enough declines. First it is lost from parts of its range, which is local extinction. If that happens everywhere, it is extinction.

THE PAIR TO KEEP STRAIGHT

Plasticity vs. adaptation

PLASTICITYADAPTATION

Check yourself: a bird species starts nesting two weeks earlier in warm years and at the old time in cool years. Plasticity or adaptation?

Plasticity. The same individuals are changing their behavior in response to conditions, and switching back. Nothing has changed genetically.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Plasticity

An individual changing its behavior, body or physiology in response to its environment, within its own lifetime. No genetic change is involved.

Adaptation

A population changing genetically over generations through natural selection, so that it is better suited to its environment.

Extinction

The loss of every individual of a species, everywhere.

STEP 5 · A RANGE SHIFT IN DATA

WHAT TO TAKE FROM IT

Three conclusions

MOST MOVED UPThe majority of dots are above zero. Ranges are shifting, in the direction warming predicts.
BUT NOT ALLSome did not move, and a few moved down. Species differ.
THE HIGHEST CANNOTBirds living near 2,500 m in 1965 changed least. They were already close to the summit.
WATCH THE TRAP "Species at high elevations are unaffected by climate change" is the wrong reading of the right-hand side. Small change in range is not the same as no effect. A species with nowhere left to go is in the most trouble.
STEP 6 · WHEN A POPULATION DISAPPEARS

Local extinctions are already widespread

A species does not vanish all at once. It disappears from one place at a time, starting where conditions have become worst for it.

A local extinction is the loss of a species from part of its range, while it survives elsewhere. They happen first at the warm edge. At the same time the species may spread at its cold edge. The range is moving.

STEP 7 · AN ECOSYSTEM ON A DEADLINE

Coral reefs

Coral reefs are hit by both things that extra CO₂ does to the ocean at once: it warms the water, and it makes it more acidic.

DIAGRAM · THE SAME CORAL, BEFORE AND AFTER

Bleaching

HEALTHYBLEACHEDcolourful: living algae insidewhite: the algae are gone

Corals are animals that get most of their food from algae living inside their tissues. In water that is too warm, the coral loses those algae and turns white. A bleached coral is starving, and if the heat lasts, it dies.

TWO PRESSURES FROM ONE CAUSE

Warmer and more acidic

WARMINGCauses bleaching.
OCEAN ACIDIFICATIONA lower pH makes it harder for corals to build their calcium carbonate skeletons, the structure the whole reef is made of.
THE PREDICTION FOR US REEFS

Decades, not centuries

Climate scientists do not expect reefs in the United States to last another hundred years. At the current rate the estimate is closer to 20 or 30 years, with reef health severely compromised by the middle of this century. The first steps of that process are already visible.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Coral reef

A marine ecosystem built by corals, animals that make skeletons of calcium carbonate. Warming and acidifying oceans threaten them.

Ocean acidification

The fall in the pH of the ocean as it absorbs CO₂ from the air.

STEP 8 · IT IS A HEALTH ISSUE

Climate change affects human health

Rising temperatures, more extreme weather and rising seas reach people through at least nine routes. Medical journals now compare the threat to the lack of sanitation and clean water a century ago.

TAP EACH ONE TO OPEN IT · NINE ROUTES FROM CLIMATE TO HEALTH

How a changing climate makes people sick

Heat

Heat stress, cardiovascular failure.

Severe weather

Injuries and fatalities.

Air pollution

Asthma, cardiovascular disease.

Vector-borne diseases

Malaria, dengue, encephalitis, hantavirus, Rift Valley fever, Zika virus. Mosquitoes and ticks spread into newly warm places.

Allergies

Respiratory allergies, poison ivy.

Water-borne diseases

Cholera, cryptosporidiosis, campylobacter, leptospirosis.

Water and food supply

Malnutrition, diarrhea, harmful algal blooms.

Mental health

Anxiety, despair, depression, post-traumatic stress.

Environmental refugees

Forced migration, civil conflict.

WHO IS HIT HARDEST

Not everyone is affected equally

The populations most affected are generally the ones least responsible for the emissions that caused the problem.

Indigenous peoples are among the first to face the direct consequences, because of their dependence on, and close relationship with, the environment and its resources. Climate change adds to difficulties they already face: political and economic marginalization, loss of land and resources, discrimination and unemployment.

THE QUESTION BEHIND THE QUESTION Who suffers most, and are they the ones who emitted most? No. That mismatch is why climate change is treated as a question of fairness as well as of science.

MODULE 2.6 · PART 2

Reasons for hope and action

THE QUESTION If the problem is this big, is there any realistic way out of it?

Five steps: what needs to be done, climate literacy, a problem we already solved, why renewable energy changes the picture, and who does what.

STEP 1 · THE ANSWER IS NOT A MYSTERY

What needs to be done

After eight steps of bad news, the most important fact in this module: we know what the solution is.

WHAT WE NEED TO DO

Stop adding greenhouse gases to the air

Burn less fossil fuelReplace it with renewable energyEmissions to zero

Climate change is caused by the carbon we add to the atmosphere. Halting it means bringing those emissions down, rapidly, to zero. The main way to do that is to stop getting our energy from fossil fuels.

WHY IT IS HARD

The challenging parts

ENERGY NEEDS KEEP GROWINGEfforts to cut carbon so far have not been strong enough to outpace rising global demand for energy.
THE SYSTEM IS BUILT ON FOSSIL FUELSPower plants, vehicles, buildings and industry all have to change. That is a huge infrastructure job.
IT TAKES EVERYONEIndividual choices help, but they are not enough without larger, systemic changes. And the longer we wait, the harder and more expensive it becomes.
IS CATASTROPHE GUARANTEED? No. Climate catastrophe is not a foregone conclusion, but we need to act soon. Every action matters, every bit of warming matters, every year matters, every choice matters.
STEP 2 · WHAT ONE PERSON NEEDS

Climate literacy

You do not have to be a climate scientist to take part in solving this. You do have to be climate literate. A climate-literate person has four traits.

1Understands the essentials
Understands the essential principles of Earth's climate system.
2Can judge information
Knows how to assess scientifically credible information about climate.
3Can talk about it
Communicates about climate and climate change in a meaningful way.
4Makes informed decisions
Is able to make informed and responsible decisions about actions that may affect climate.

STEP 3 · A PROBLEM WE ALREADY SOLVED

The ozone hole and the Montreal Protocol

Has humanity ever fixed a global environmental problem that required almost every country to change what it manufactured? Yes.

DATA · PRODUCTION OF OZONE-DEPLETING SUBSTANCES

From a million tonnes to almost nothing

05001,000PRODUCTION (thousand tonnes a year)198820002012Montreal Protocol, 1987almost zero

The curve shows the shape of the record, not exact values.

WHAT HAPPENED

The Montreal Protocol, 1987

THE PROBLEMChemicals used in refrigerators and spray cans were destroying the ozone layer, which shields the surface from the sun's ultraviolet rays.
THE RESPONSECountries agreed to phase those chemicals out.
THE RESULTGlobal production of the major ozone-depleting substances fell to almost zero within about twenty years.
WHY IT BELONGS HERE It is proof that large-scale, effective, worldwide action on an atmospheric problem is possible. We have tackled big, hard problems before. We can do it again.
DO NOT MIX THEM UP The ozone hole is not the cause of climate change, and it is not part of the greenhouse effect. It is here only as a precedent.

Batteries: the sun does not shine at night

Solar and wind only produce power some of the time. Battery storage lets it be used later. Storage prices have dropped much faster than anyone expected, and battery capacity has grown with them. Cheap generation plus cheap storage is what makes a fossil-free grid realistic.

STEP 5 · WHO DOES WHAT

Individual actions and societal actions

Slowing climate change needs both kinds of action at once. Neither is enough without the other.

INDIVIDUAL LEVEL

What one person can do

LEARN YOUR NUMBERSFind out about your own carbon emissions and your ecological footprint.
CHANGE HOW YOU GET AROUNDUse mass transit.
CHANGE WHERE YOUR POWER COMES FROMSwitch to green energy.
SPEAK UPContact your elected representatives, at the local, state and federal level. Talk about it.
SOCIETAL LEVEL

What only larger changes can do

ENERGYReplace fossil-fuel power with renewable generation and storage across the whole grid.
BUILDINGS AND TRANSPORTEfficient building standards, public transit, electric vehicles.
POLICYLaws and agreements that bring emissions down, as the Montreal Protocol did for ozone.
FAIRNESSSupport for the communities that are hit hardest and contributed least.

Lock it in · Part 2

  1. We know what to do: bring emissions rapidly to zero, mainly by replacing fossil fuels with renewable energy. Catastrophe is not guaranteed.
  2. A climate-literate person understands the climate system, can judge information, can communicate about it, and makes informed decisions.
  3. The Montreal Protocol (1987) shows that worldwide action on an atmospheric problem can work.
  4. Solar is 200 times cheaper than in 1977 and now as cheap as or cheaper than coal or gas. Battery storage has dropped steeply in cost too.
  5. Solar works in northern places: Germany went from 4% to 32% wind and solar in sixteen years.
  6. It takes individual actions and societal changes together, and many fields beyond science.
