STEP 2 · THE OCEAN'S PRODUCERS

Why ocean NPP is falling

Phytoplankton do almost half of the planet's photosynthesis (Module 2.3). Warming does not hurt them by being too hot. It cuts off their supply of nutrients, and the reason is about density.

START HERE · LIGHT ON TOP, NUTRIENTS AT THE BOTTOMWhy the nutrients end up deepPhytoplankton have to live in the top layer, because that is the only place with light. As they grow they use up the nutrients in that layer.When they die, and when the animals that eat them die or produce waste, all of it sinks. Decomposers break it down in the deep water and release the nutrients down there.So the ocean keeps separating the two things producers need: light at the top, nutrients at the bottom.
A COOLER OCEAN · WELL MIXEDMixing is the delivery systemThe only way those nutrients get back to the light is for the water itself to mix. Wind, storms and winter cooling stir deep water up toward the surface.That is easy when the surface and the deep are close in temperature, because then they are close in density. Water of similar density stirs together readily.
A WARMER OCEAN · STRATIFIEDThe delivery system jamsWarm water is less dense than cold water. The warmer the surface gets, the bigger the density difference between it and the cold water below, and the harder it is to stir the two together. Think of oil sitting on water.The ocean settles into layers that do not mix. This is vertical stratification. Water rising from below turns back at the boundary.Fewer nutrients reach the sunlit surface. Phytoplankton run short, grow less, and ocean NPP goes down. Everything that eats them has less to eat.
THE CHAIN OF CAUSE AND EFFECT

Five links

1Surface water warms
The ocean absorbs most of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases.
2The density gap grows
Warm water is less dense. It floats more firmly on the cold, dense, nutrient-rich water below.
3Mixing weakens
The bigger the density difference, the harder the layers are to stir together.
4Nutrients stay deep
Fewer reach the sunlit surface.
5NPP falls
Phytoplankton are nutrient-limited, so they grow less.
CONNECT IT This is the limiting nutrient idea from Module 2.3 in reverse. There, adding a limiting nutrient raised NPP. Here, warming takes the supply away, and NPP drops.

Extinction

The loss of every individual of a species, everywhere.

WHY NOT EVERY SPECIES MOVED UP

Species differ

Moving is only one of the three responses in Step 4, and it needs time, the ability to disperse and connected habitat. Temperature is also not the only thing that sets where a bird lives: its food, the plants it nests in and the other species around it matter too. So some species shifted a lot, some stayed put, and a few moved down.

LESS ROOM, AND MORE NEIGHBORS A mountain narrows toward its summit, so there is less area the higher you go. As lower species move up, they arrive in the ranges of the species already living there. Ranges overlap more, niches overlap more, and competition (Module 2.1) can increase, all inside a shrinking space. The species at the top are squeezed from below with nowhere to go.

STEP 7 · AN ECOSYSTEM ON A DEADLINE

Coral reefs

Coral reefs are hit by both things that extra CO₂ does to the ocean at once: it warms the water, and it makes it more acidic.

DIAGRAM · THE SAME CORAL, BEFORE AND AFTER

Bleaching

HEALTHYBLEACHEDcolorful: living algae insidewhite: the algae are gone

Corals are animals that get most of their food from algae living inside their tissues. In water that is too warm, the coral loses those algae and turns white. A bleached coral is starving, and if the heat lasts, it dies.

TWO PRESSURES FROM ONE CAUSE

Warmer and more acidic

WARMINGCauses bleaching.
OCEAN ACIDIFICATIONA lower pH makes it harder for corals to build their calcium carbonate skeletons, the structure the whole reef is made of.
THE PREDICTION FOR US REEFS

Decades, not centuries

Climate scientists do not expect reefs in the United States to last another hundred years. At the current rate the estimate is closer to 20 or 30 years, with reef health severely compromised by the middle of this century. The first steps of that process are already visible.

WHO IS HIT HARDEST

Not everyone is affected equally

The populations most affected are generally the ones least responsible for the emissions that caused the problem.

Indigenous peoples are among the first to face the direct consequences, because of their dependence on, and close relationship with, the environment and its resources. Climate change adds to difficulties they already face: political and economic marginalization, loss of land and resources, discrimination and unemployment.

THE QUESTION BEHIND THE QUESTION Who suffers most, and are they the ones who emitted most? No. That mismatch is why climate change is treated as a question of fairness as well as of science.

MODULE 2.6 · PART 2

Reasons for hope and action

THE QUESTION If the problem is this big, is there any realistic way out of it?

Five steps: what needs to be done, climate literacy, a problem we already solved, why renewable energy changes the picture, and who does what.

STEP 1 · THE ANSWER IS NOT A MYSTERY

What needs to be done

After eight steps of bad news, the most important fact in this module: we know what the solution is.

WHAT WE NEED TO DO

Stop adding greenhouse gases to the air

Shift away from fossil fuelsReplace them with renewable energyEmissions to zero

Climate change is caused by the carbon we add to the atmosphere. Halting it means bringing those emissions down, rapidly, to zero. The main way to do that is to stop getting our energy from fossil fuels.

Less, or none? The end point is none: zero CO₂ emissions. "Shift away from" describes the route there. Fossil fuels cannot be switched off overnight, so they are replaced with renewable energy, as fast as possible, until none are being burned. Burning less is a step on the way, not the goal.

WHY IT IS HARD

The challenging parts

ENERGY NEEDS KEEP GROWINGEfforts to cut carbon so far have not been strong enough to outpace rising global demand for energy.
THE SYSTEM IS BUILT ON FOSSIL FUELSPower plants, vehicles, buildings and industry all have to change. That is a huge infrastructure job.
IT TAKES EVERYONEIndividual choices help, but they are not enough without larger, systemic changes. And the longer we wait, the harder and more expensive it becomes.
IS CATASTROPHE GUARANTEED? No. Climate catastrophe is not a foregone conclusion, but we need to act soon. Every action matters, every bit of warming matters, every year matters, every choice matters.

BEFORE · WHAT THE OZONE LAYER DOESA sunscreen for the whole planetThe ozone layer is a layer of ozone gas high in the atmosphere. It absorbs most of the sun's harmful ultraviolet rays, the UV-B, before they reach the ground. Gentler UV-A mostly passes through.
THE PROBLEM · CFCsChemicals we made were destroying itCFCs were chemicals used in refrigerators, air conditioners and spray cans. Once released they drifted up and broke ozone apart. By the 1980s the layer had thinned, with a large "hole" over Antarctica.A thinner layer lets more UV-B reach the surface, which means more skin cancer and eye damage, and harm to crops and plankton.
THE FIX · THE MONTREAL PROTOCOL, 1987Stop making the chemicalsCountries agreed to phase out the production of CFCs and switch to other chemicals. Production fell to almost nothing, which is what the graph below shows.With far less of those chemicals going up, the ozone layer has stopped thinning and is slowly recovering. It is expected to be back to its earlier state around the middle of this century.
SO WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGED?

What factories made

THE PROBLEMChemicals called CFCs, used in refrigerators and spray cans, were destroying the ozone layer, which shields the surface from the sun's ultraviolet rays.
THE RESPONSEIn the Montreal Protocol (1987), countries agreed to phase those chemicals out.
WHAT CHANGEDProduction. People did not give up refrigerators or spray cans. Manufacturers switched to different chemicals, everywhere, because the agreement required it.
THE RESULTGlobal production of the major ozone-depleting substances fell to almost zero within about twenty years, and the ozone layer is recovering.
DO NOT MIX THEM UP The ozone hole is not the cause of climate change, and it is not part of the greenhouse effect. The ozone layer is about ultraviolet light coming in. The greenhouse effect is about heat going out. It is here only as a precedent.

THE BIG IDEA

Climate change is already altering species, ecosystems and human health. A species can move, adjust or go extinct. We know the solution: bring emissions rapidly to zero by shifting from fossil fuels to renewable energy, which is now cheap enough to make that feasible. It takes both individual and societal action.

SECTION 1OBJECTIVE 1

How climate change is impacting species and ecosystems now

  • Already visible: melting of the Arctic ice pack and loss of glaciers, rising sea levels (with people already displaced as climate refugees), changes in species ranges, changes in phenology, and decreased NPP on land and in the ocean.
  • Why local effects matter: polar bears and bleached reefs are striking but distant. Local effects show it is happening where you live, now.

Why ocean NPP is falling, as a chain:

  • Phytoplankton need light (only at the surface) and nutrients (mostly in deep water, because dead organisms sink and decompose there).
  • Mixing between the layers carries nutrients up to the light.
  • Warm water is less dense. A warmer surface floats more firmly on the cold water below, so the ocean stratifies and mixes less.
  • Fewer nutrients reach the surface, phytoplankton grow less, and NPP falls.
PHENOLOGYThe timing of biological events. Warming shifts it. Kyoto's cherry blossom now peaks earlier than at any time in 1,200 years of records, and lakes are ceasing to freeze every winter.
SPECIES DISTRIBUTION (RANGE)Where a species lives. Ranges are shifting toward the poles and up mountains.
  • Local extinction is the loss of a species from part of its range while it survives elsewhere. It is already widespread in every group studied, but not equally: highest in fish and insects, lowest in mammals and amphibians. "n =" is the number of species studied.
  • Coral reefs are hit twice: warming causes bleaching, and ocean acidification makes skeletons harder to build. Reefs in the United States are not expected to last another hundred years. The estimate is closer to 20 or 30.

Guiding questions · What climate change is doing

These are the questions this module is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q1  How has arctic sea ice and sea level changed as a result of climate change? As a result of climate change, how are species ranges changing?

Arctic sea ice is shrinking and glaciers are being lost. Sea level is rising, and low-lying islands are already losing ground.

Species ranges are shifting, toward the poles and up mountains, as species follow the climate they are suited to.

Q2  Why should we focus not only on striking examples of climate change, like polar bears or bleached coral reefs, but also on more local effects on people and animals?

Striking examples are far away from most people, which makes climate change easy to file under "somewhere else".

Local effects, on the people, animals and seasons where you live, show that it is happening here, to everyone, now.

Q3  Why is ocean NPP decreasing as a result of climate change? In your answer, consider vertical stratification of the ocean due to differences in heat and nutrient content.

Phytoplankton need light, which is only at the surface, and nutrients, which collect in deep water because dead organisms sink and decompose there.

Mixing carries nutrients up. But warm water is less dense than cold water, so a warmer surface floats more firmly on top and the ocean stratifies into layers that do not mix.

Fewer nutrients reach the sunlit surface, phytoplankton grow less, and NPP falls.

Q4  What is phenology and how is it affected by climate change? What are some examples of tracking of phenology that have shown changes due to climate?

Phenology is the timing of biological events: flowering, migration, breeding. Many are cued by temperature, so warming shifts them, usually earlier in spring.

Examples: peak cherry blossom in Kyoto, recorded for more than 1,200 years, is now earlier than at any time in the record. Lakes that reliably froze every winter are projected to stop doing so.

Q5  Broadly, what are the 3 types of responses organisms are expected to have in response to climate change? What are the conditions for each of these 3 types of responses?

Move: requires time, the ability to disperse, and contiguous habitat.

Stay, but adjust: by plasticity, or by adaptation, which needs genetic variation and enough generations.

Extinction: if a species can neither move nor adjust.

Q6  What is the difference between plasticity & adaptation?

Plasticity: an individual changes its behavior, body or physiology within its own lifetime. No genetic change.

Adaptation: a population changes genetically over generations, through natural selection.

Q7  For the study of the upper elevational limit of birds in New Guinea, why did birds who were living at ~2500m in 1965 not change as much by 2012 as birds that were living at ~1000m in 1965? What does the zero (0) line represent on the y-axis?

Birds at 2,500 m were already near the top of the mountain. There was little mountain left above them, so they could not move up much. Birds at 1,000 m had far more room.

The zero line means no change in a species' upper elevation limit. Above it the species moved up. Below it the species moved down.

Q8  How common is local extinction in different groups of taxa, such as plants, insects, invertebrates, etc.? Are all taxa impacted by the same amount? What does “n = ” refer to at the top of each bar?

Climate-related local extinctions are already widespread: between about a third and well over half of species in every group studied.

Not equally. Fish (about 60%) and insects (about 56%) are highest. Mammals (about 35%) and amphibians (about 37%) are lowest.

n = is the number of species studied in that group.

Q9  What is the prediction for coral reefs in the US in the future?

They are not expected to survive another hundred years. At the current rate the estimate is closer to 20 or 30 years, with reef health severely compromised by the middle of this century.

Q10  What are the predicted effects of climate change on human health? List and describe several health impacts.

Heat: heat stress and cardiovascular failure.

Air pollution: asthma and cardiovascular disease.

Vector-borne diseases: mosquitoes and ticks spread malaria, dengue and other diseases into newly warm places.

Severe weather: injuries and deaths.

Water and food: water-borne diseases such as cholera, and malnutrition.

Also allergies, mental health effects, and forced migration.

Q11  What people are expected to suffer the most from the negative effects of climate change? Are they the same people who are responsible for the most carbon emissions?

The most affected populations are generally the least responsible for the emissions. Indigenous peoples are among the first to face the direct consequences, because of their close relationship with the environment and its resources.

So no, they are not the same people.

Q12  Do we know what we need to do to address climate change? If so, what is it? What are some challenging aspects to solving this problem? Is climate catastrophe a foregone conclusion?

Yes. Bring greenhouse gas emissions rapidly to zero, mainly by shifting away from fossil fuels to renewable energy.

Challenges: energy demand keeps growing, the whole energy system is built on fossil fuels, and it takes both individual and systemic change.

No. Catastrophe is not guaranteed, but we need to act soon.

Q13  What are the traits of a climate-literate person? How can a person increase their climate literacy?

A climate-literate person:

1. understands the essential principles of Earth's climate system;

2. knows how to assess scientifically credible information about climate;

3. communicates about climate and climate change in a meaningful way;

4. is able to make informed and responsible decisions about actions that may affect climate.

To increase it: learn the science, check sources, talk about it, and let it inform your choices.

Q14  Is there any evidence that humanity has been able to solve difficult ecological problems in the past? What were we able to achieve as a result of the Montreal Protocol in 1987?

Yes: the ozone hole. Chemicals called CFCs were destroying the ozone layer, which shields the surface from ultraviolet rays.

Under the Montreal Protocol, countries phased those chemicals out. Global production of the major ozone-depleting substances fell to almost zero, and the ozone layer is recovering.

Q15  What other fields, beyond science, can contribute to solving the climate crisis? How do those other fields contribute?

Architecture: buildings designed to need far less energy.

Engineering: cheaper solar panels, better batteries, a smarter grid.

Economics and business: showing that renewables are the cheaper choice and investing accordingly.

Policy and law: agreements and rules that change what whole countries do.

Communication: helping people understand the problem and what they can do.

Q16  How has the cost of renewable energy changed over the past ~40 years? How has increased battery capacity changed in recent years? How are changes in the cost of renewables and capacity of batteries relevant to solving the climate crisis?

The price of solar has fallen by a factor of about 200 since 1977 and by a factor of 5 since 2011. It is now as cheap as or cheaper than coal or gas.

Battery storage has become much cheaper and battery capacity has grown.

Together they make it feasible to replace fossil fuels: cheap generation, plus a way to use that power when the sun is not shining.

Q17  For businesses, do models show that it is more economically viable to use renewable energy or fossil fuels? What is the time horizon for economically viable full-scale grid defection?

Renewable energy. Partial grid defection already makes economic sense.

Full-scale grid defection becomes economically viable around 2030.

Q18  Is solar power a viable renewable energy source in a place like Michigan?

Yes. Germany gets less sun than Alaska, and it went from 4% wind and solar in 2000 to 32% in 2016.

Q19  Approximately what proportion of our current energy comes from renewable sources? Have we ever had to make a large infrastructure adjustment before?

Only a small share. Most of our energy still comes from coal, oil and natural gas.

Yes. In the 1800s cities such as New York built piped water systems, an entirely new kind of infrastructure that every building had to be connected to.
