MODULE 2.1 · PART 1

Chemistry foundation to learn first

THE QUESTIONHow can moving electrons be where all of our energy comes from?

Ten steps: where energy comes from, redox, where chemical energy lives (with a closer look at electrons, electronegativity, bonds, glucose and Lewis dots), fuel bonds vs exhaust bonds, and two fires that use them.

STEP 1 · WHERE ENERGY COMES FROM

Phototrophs and chemotrophs

Every living thing needs energy, and there are only two places to get it: light, or chemicals. Either way, it ends up stored in the chemical bonds of sugar.

VOCABPhototrophAn organism that captures energy from sunlight.photo = light · troph = feeder. Plants, algae and some bacteria.
VOCABChemotrophAn organism that gets its energy from chemical compounds, like the glucose in food.chemo = chemical · troph = feeder. Animals, fungi and many bacteria.
GO DEEPER · ECOLOGY & EVOLUTIONPhoto, chemo, auto, hetero →Module 2.3, Energy, Matter & Trophic Levels, breaks down all four words and how they combine.
STEP 2 · REDOX

Redox reactions are coupled electron transfers

Moving an electron always takes two molecules: one gives it, one takes it. Watch both arrows. On the red one, A's electrons slide over and pile up on B. On the blue one, each electron B takes leaves A one + charge behind. Both charges change at once.

The giver is oxidized: it loses negative electrons, so its charge goes up. The taker is reduced: it gains them, so its charge goes down. One can't happen without the other, which is why redox reactions are always coupled.

Every time bonds change in a cell, electrons are moving, and moving electrons moves energy.

A WAY TO REMEMBERReduced = charge reduced (it gained negative electrons). Oxidized = charge raised (it lost them, usually to oxygen). The same rule as LEO says GER.
COMMON MIX-UPReduced doesn't mean "has less". A reduced molecule gained electrons and usually holds more potential energy. Glucose is reduced; CO₂ is oxidized.
STEP 3 · CHEMICAL POTENTIAL ENERGY

Where the energy actually lives

So what does moving electrons have to do with energy? An electron's potential energy depends on where it sits.

Electrons far from a positive nucleus, or shared evenly between two atoms, are like a stretched rubber band: loaded. Electrons pulled in close, toward an atom like oxygen, are slack: little energy left.

So the energy in food is in its nonpolar bonds (C–H and C–C). Moving those electrons into polar bonds with oxygen releases it.

LOOK CLOSER BELOWThe next five sections unpack this one idea: where an electron's energy lives, why some atoms pull harder, tension in a bond, glucose bond by bond, and the dots in H₂O, CO₂ and CH₄.

SNAPLet it move closer: energy comes outIf the electron gets the chance to move closer to a positive nucleus, the band snaps back. The energy it held is released.This is all chemical energy is: electrons moving to spots where they are held more tightly.

THE IDEA TO HOLDThe energy is not in the atom. It is in where the electron sits compared with positive and negative charges. Far from a nucleus = stretched band = high potential energy. Close = slack band = low.
STABLE MEANS LOW ENERGYA ball at the bottom of the stairs is stable: it has nowhere lower to fall. An electron held tightly is the same. Atoms are also most stable when their outer shell is full: 2 electrons for hydrogen, 8 for carbon, nitrogen and oxygen. That is why carbon makes 4 bonds, nitrogen 3, oxygen 2 and hydrogen 1.

Why some atoms pull harder

Most definitions stop at "electronegativity is an atom's tendency to attract electrons." That says what, not why. Here is the why: count the protons, and see what stands between them and the electrons.

HYDROGEN1 proton, but nothing in the wayHydrogen's nucleus is a single proton, so its pull is small. But its electron sits in the first shell, very close, with no other electrons in between.Small pull, very close. It ends up about level with carbon.

NITROGENOne more proton, same shellNitrogen has 7 protons. The outer electrons are in the same shell as carbon's, but now they feel about +5.A stronger pull at the same distance draws the shell in tighter. That is why atoms get smaller across a row: the protons make the radius small.
OXYGENThe strongest pull in biologyOxygen has 8 protons. Its outer electrons feel about +6, still in the second shell, close to the nucleus.So oxygen is the most electronegative atom in biological molecules. In any bond with carbon or hydrogen, the shared electrons are dragged toward oxygen.
SULFURMore protons, but weaker. Why?Sulfur has 16 protons, twice oxygen's. But 10 inner electrons in two shells cancel out most of them, and its outer shell is one level farther out.About the same net pull, from farther away, is a weaker grip. So sulfur is less electronegative than oxygen even with more protons.
SO, IS IT PROTONS OR RADIUS?You are right that it starts with the protons. Electronegativity is how hard a nucleus pulls on shared electrons, and the protons do the pulling. The full answer has two parts:
1 · How much charge the electrons feel: protons minus the inner electrons that cancel some of them.
2 · How far away they are: a pull gets weaker with distance.
Across a row, the protons win and also shrink the atom. Down a column, a new shell farther out wins, so the pull drops.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALSThe textbook explains the row: more protons hold electrons more tightly. The idea that inner electrons cancel part of the nucleus (called shielding, or effective nuclear charge) is from general chemistry, not the slides. The +4, +5 and +6 are a simple count (protons minus inner electrons), not measured values.
Rank the atoms of life by electronegativity, the way the slides do.

O > N, S, P > C ≈ H. Oxygen is the most electronegative; carbon and hydrogen are about equal.

THE TENSION, IN ONE LINEEven sharing keeps electrons far from a strong pull: high tension, high energy. Uneven sharing lets them sit close to oxygen: tension released, low energy.
WHERE OUR ENERGY COMES FROMWe cannot turn sunlight into chemical energy the way plants do. So we eat molecules full of nonpolar bonds and turn them into molecules with polar bonds, like CO₂ and H₂O. Changing bonds from nonpolar to polar releases energy.
THINK ABOUT ITA stable molecule is one with nothing left to give. CO₂ and water are the exhaust: every electron is already pulled toward oxygen.

Where is the fuel in glucose?

Which bonds in glucose hold the most potential energy?

The nonpolar ones: C–H and C–C. Their shared electrons sit in the middle, far from both nuclei.

WHY WE BURN IT: NATURAL GASMethane is the main gas in natural gas. On a gas stove, CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Every shared pair moves from the middle of a C–H bond to the oxygen end of a bond, the bands snap, and the energy leaves as heat and light. Glucose is full of the same C–H and C–C bonds.
COMMON MIX-UPLewis dots show where electrons are, not the shape. Water is really bent, not a straight line. And the shaking is a picture: the stored energy is in where the electrons sit, not in the motion.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALSNatural gas and the stove equation are general chemistry, not from the slides.
NH₃ and NaCl were also on the slide. Why are they lower than CH₄?

N–H bonds are polar: nitrogen pulls the shared pair toward itself. In NaCl, chlorine has taken sodium's electron completely. In both, electrons already sit close to a strong pull.

STEP 10 · USING ENERGY

Nonpolar in, polar out: two fires

Here is redox doing work. Two fires we use, from gentle to explosive. Same chemistry in both.

Example 1 · a gas stove. Natural gas is mostly methane. Before, every shared pair sits in the middle of its bond. After, every pair has moved toward an oxygen. The energy comes from electrons moving out of nonpolar bonds into polar ones.

Example 2 · a rocket. Strip fuel down to hydrogen and oxygen. Same move, nonpolar to polar, but all at once: the energy explodes out in every direction, and the exhaust is just water. This is what fuels a rocket.

WHO WAS OXIDIZED?In both fires the fuel gives its electrons up to oxygen: the fuel is oxidized (its carbon goes from −4 to +4). Oxygen pulls them in: oxygen is reduced. Burning is one big redox.
GLUCOSE IS FUEL TOOGlucose is full of the same C–H and C–C bonds as methane. Burn sugar in a flame and you get the same CO₂, water, heat and light.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALSThe stove and rocket examples are general knowledge, not from the slides: the Space Shuttle's main engines burned liquid hydrogen with liquid oxygen; some newer rockets burn methane with oxygen.

But… a cell can't blow up to get its energy.

It has to take fuel apart a little at a time, and catch the energy in small packets as it goes: energy-carrying molecules. That's where the biology begins.

MODULE 2.1 · PART 2

How a cell takes glucose apart, and harnesses its energy

THE QUESTIONIf a cell can't explode, how does it get the energy out of glucose, and catch it?

Nine steps: the whole plan, where each stage happens, glycolysis and its budget, the map of glucose's journey, pyruvate processing, the citric acid cycle, who makes the carriers, and where the energy is now.

STEP 1 · THE WHOLE PLAN

Glucose, taken apart

A cell runs the same reaction as a fire, just slowly. Here is the whole plan in one line.

Looks complicated? Don't worry: this page breaks down every step.

Same reaction as the fires: glucose and oxygen in, CO₂ and water out. The difference is the last term. Instead of heat and light, the cell gets energy-carrying molecules: ATP, NADH and FADH₂.

Watch glucose shrink, 6 carbons to 3 to 2 to 1, and watch the green bars: the energy drains a little at each stage, into the carriers.

OXIDIZEDGlucose is oxidized more and more at every step: it keeps losing electrons, until its carbons are CO₂.
REDUCEDThe carriers are reduced: NAD⁺ → NADH, FAD → FADH₂. They pick up the electrons glucose lost. Oxygen is reduced at the very end, becoming water.
STEP 2 · WHERE IT HAPPENS

Where each stage happens

Now follow glucose itself. Respiration has four stages, and they don't all happen in the same place.

Stage 1, glycolysis, runs out in the cytoplasm, as soon as glucose gets into the cell, and needs no oxygen.

Its two pyruvates then move into a mitochondrion for the other three stages: 2 · pyruvate processing, 3 · the citric acid cycle, and 4 · oxidative phosphorylation. This page starts with stage 1.

THE ONE LINE TO REMEMBERGlycolysis: cytoplasm. Pyruvate processing, citric acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation: inside the mitochondria.
SIDE NOTEBacteria have no mitochondria, so they run stages 2 to 4 in their cytosol.
STEP 3 · STAGE 1 OF 4 · IN THE CYTOPLASM

Glycolysis: splitting sugar

Glucose goes in. It is split in two. Two pyruvates come out, and on the way the cell catches some energy as ATP and NADH. That’s all of glycolysis; the rest is detail.

Which carries the smallest share of glucose's energy: acetyl-CoA, NADH, ATP, pyruvate or FADH₂?

ATP. NADH holds about 3× and FADH₂ about 2× the energy of one ATP, and pyruvate and acetyl-CoA hold far more.

MODULE 2.1 · PART 3

Cashing in the carriers

THE QUESTIONThe shuttles are loaded. How do they turn into ATP, and why do we need oxygen to do it?

Seven steps: the electron transport chain, oxygen at the end of it, what happens without oxygen, the three sizes of energy and what each is worth, ATP up close, and the whole story of one electron pair.

STEP 1 · THE ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN

Harnessing the snap, one step at a time

Stage 4 is where most of the energy is caught. The loaded shuttles drop their electrons at the top of a staircase, and the electrons step down to oxygen.

COMPLEX INADH hands its electrons to the top of the chainThe chain is a series of protein complexes in the inner membrane of the mitochondrion. NADH gives 2 high-energy electrons to Complex I, at the top of the stairs.
COMPLEX IIFADH₂ gets on a step lowerFADH₂ hands its electrons to Complex II, which passes them to Q. They skip the first step.That is why FADH₂ is worth less: each NADH holds about 3 times the energy of an ATP, each FADH₂ about 2 times.
EACH HAND-OFFEvery step is a little lowerEach carrier hands the electrons to the next, which is more electronegative and pulls harder. Every hand-off is a small snap, so the electrons have less energy at every step.Complexes I, III and IV use the energy of each snap to pump protons (H⁺) across the membrane, crowding them into the space outside.
THE PAYOUTProtons rush back, and the motor spinsThe crowded protons want back in, like water behind a dam. Their only way through is ATP synthase. As they flow through, it turns like a motor and joins ADP and phosphate into ATP.So the electrons do not make ATP directly. Electron flow pumps protons, and protons make ATP. That link is the second kind of coupling.
TWO KINDS OF COUPLING1 · Redox coupling: every oxidation is paired with a reduction, so electrons always land somewhere. 2 · Energy coupling: the energy of electrons falling is used to pump protons, and the protons flowing back power ATP synthase. Together this is called chemiosmosis.
True or false: the energy of electrons moving down the chain is used directly to make ATP.

False. It pumps protons. The proton gradient powers ATP synthase.

PLANTS ONLY? THE PHOTOSYSTEMSYou're right that the photosystems are for plants (and algae and some bacteria), not us. In photosynthesis, light hits Photosystem II and Photosystem I and kicks electrons back up to a high energy level, then they run down a chain much like ours. Plants run both chains. We only run this one, and in ours electrons only ever go downhill. Photosynthesis gets its own module.

NO LANDING PADThe electron traffic jamTake oxygen away and Complex IV has nowhere to put its electrons. It stays loaded. Then Complex III cannot hand off, then Q, then Complex I. The whole chain backs up.No electron flow means no proton pumping, so ATP synthase stops.
THE BACKUPNo NAD⁺ comes backNADH can only unload at the chain. With the chain jammed, NADH piles up and the cell runs out of empty NAD⁺. Glycolysis needs NAD⁺, so even glycolysis would stall.

A poison that blocks the chain causes the same jam. Cyanide does exactly this: it blocks Complex IV, so even with plenty of oxygen the electrons can't land.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALSCyanide blocking Complex IV is general biology, not from the slides. The slides ask what a poison that blocks the chain would do.
If the first step of the citric acid cycle were blocked, what would happen to NAD⁺?

NAD⁺ would pile up in its oxidized form: nothing would be loading it. That cuts off the electrons to the chain, so oxidative phosphorylation would drop.

ATP. Then FADH₂, NADH, acetyl-CoA, pyruvate, and glucose at the top.

THE BIG IDEA

Food stores energy in electrons that are shared evenly between carbon and hydrogen. Respiration moves those electrons, one controlled step at a time, onto carriers the cell can spend. What is left of the glucose leaves as CO₂.

SECTION 1

Energy lives in electrons

  • An electron's potential energy depends on where it sits. Far from a positive nucleus, it has a lot. Pulled in close to one, it has little.
  • In a nonpolar bond (C–C, C–H) the two atoms share electrons almost evenly, so the electrons stay relatively far from either nucleus. These bonds are energy-rich.
  • In a polar bond (O–H, C=O) oxygen pulls the electrons toward itself. These bonds are energy-poor.
  • Respiration turns fuel bonds into exhaust bonds. Glucose goes in, CO₂ and H₂O come out, and the energy difference is what the cell captures.
  • Plants build fuel bonds using sunlight. Animals can't, so we eat molecules that already have them.
THINK ABOUT IT Gasoline is almost all C–C and C–H bonds. Water is all O–H. That is why one burns and the other puts out fires.
SECTION 2

Redox reactions

OILOxidation Is Loss of electrons
RIGReduction Is Gain of electrons
  • Redox reactions are electron hand-offs. They are always paired: if one molecule loses electrons, another gains them.
  • In cells, electrons usually travel with a proton as a hydrogen atom. Losing H means oxidized. Gaining H means reduced.
  • Example: a carbon in sugar gives up 2 electrons and 2 H⁺. NAD⁺ takes the 2 electrons and 1 H⁺, becoming NADH. The other H⁺ floats off into solution.
  • In respiration, glucose is the first donor and oxygen is the last acceptor. When O₂ is reduced, it becomes water.

SECTION 4 CYTOPLASM · NO O₂ NEEDED

Glycolysis

"Sugar splitting." Ten enzyme steps turn one glucose (6 carbons) into two pyruvate (3 carbons each). You only need the three phases, not every step.

1 · INVEST2 ATP add phosphates to glucose, making fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. This is the one intermediate worth knowing by name.
2 · SPLITThe 6-carbon sugar breaks into two 3-carbon molecules of G3P (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate).
3 · PAYOFFEach G3P becomes pyruvate, making 1 NADH and 2 ATP along the way.
GOES IN1 glucose · 2 ATP · 2 NAD⁺
COMES OUT2 pyruvate · 4 ATP (net 2) · 2 NADH
  • Glycolysis makes ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation: an enzyme moves a phosphate directly from a sugar molecule onto ADP.
  • Only partial oxidation happens here. The two pyruvates still hold most of glucose's energy.
SECTION 5 MITOCHONDRIAL MATRIX

Pyruvate processing

  • Pyruvate moves into the mitochondrion. One of its 3 carbons is cut off and leaves as CO₂.
  • The remaining 2-carbon piece is attached to coenzyme A (built from a B vitamin), giving acetyl-CoA.
  • Each pyruvate makes 1 NADH, so 2 per glucose.
  • Bacteria have no mitochondria, so they run this step and the next one in their cytosol.
SECTION 6 MITOCHONDRIAL MATRIX

Citric acid cycle

  • Acetyl-CoA feeds into a loop of reactions. Its 2 carbons are stripped off as 2 CO₂, and the loop resets for the next one.
  • One turn makes 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂ and 1 ATP. One glucose gives two acetyl-CoA, so two turns.
  • Its real product is loaded electron carriers, not ATP.
2 acetyl-CoA + 6 NAD⁺ + 2 FAD + 2 ADP + 2 Pᵢ → 4 CO₂ + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ + 2 ATP
