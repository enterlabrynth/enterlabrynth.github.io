MODULE 2.1 · PART 1

How a cell takes glucose apart, and harnesses its energy

THE QUESTIONIf a cell can't explode, how does it get the energy out of glucose, and catch it?

Nine steps, in the order of the lectures: the whole plan, where each stage happens, ATP (the energy the cell spends), then each stage on its own (glycolysis, pyruvate processing, the citric acid cycle, the electron transport chain), then the whole story as one path with the bank of everything caught, and one staircase from glucose to oxygen.

STEP 1 · THE WHOLE PLAN

Glucose, taken apart

A cell runs the same reaction as a fire, just slowly. Here is the whole plan in one line.

Looks complicated? Don't worry: this page breaks down every step.

Same reaction as the fires: glucose and oxygen in, CO₂ and water out. The difference is the last term. Instead of heat and light, the cell gets energy-carrying molecules: ATP, NADH and FADH₂.

Watch glucose shrink, 6 carbons to 3 to 2 to 1, and watch the green bars: the energy drains a little at each stage, into the carriers.

OXIDIZED
Glucose is oxidized more and more at every step: it keeps losing electrons, until its carbons are CO₂.
REDUCED
The carriers are reduced: NAD⁺ → NADH, FAD → FADH₂. They pick up the electrons glucose lost. Oxygen is reduced at the very end, becoming water.
HIGH-ENERGY ELECTRON CARRIERS
Three to know: NADH and FADH₂ (loaded in respiration) and NADPH (loaded in photosynthesis). NADP⁺ and NADPH are very similar to NAD⁺ and NADH (one extra phosphate). Each carries high-energy electrons from where they're picked up to where they're spent.
CATABOLIC AND EXERGONIC
Respiration is catabolic (breaking a big molecule down) and exergonic overall (−ΔG, spontaneous: energy is released). Building molecules, as photosynthesis builds sugar, is anabolic and endergonic (+ΔG, not spontaneous: energy has to go in).
STEP 2 · WHERE IT HAPPENS

Where each stage happens

Now follow glucose itself. Respiration has four stages, and they don't all happen in the same place.

Stage 1, glycolysis, runs out in the cytoplasm, as soon as glucose gets into the cell, and needs no oxygen.

Its two pyruvates then move into a mitochondrion for the other three stages: 2 · pyruvate processing, 3 · the citric acid cycle, and 4 · oxidative phosphorylation. This page starts with stage 1.

THE ONE LINE TO REMEMBER
Glycolysis: cytoplasm. Pyruvate processing, citric acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation: inside the mitochondria.
SIDE NOTE
Bacteria have no mitochondria, so they run stages 2 to 4 in their cytosol.

PYRUVATE STILL HAS MOST OF IT
After glycolysis, most of glucose's energy is still in the two pyruvates. The next stages, inside the mitochondrion, take it the rest of the way.

THE SCORE
One turn: 2 CO₂, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP. Per glucose (2 turns): 4 CO₂, 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 2 ATP.

You don't need the exact numbers, just the relative ones: this is where the cell makes most of its NADH and FADH₂.
WHERE, AND WHAT ELSE FEEDS IN
In the mitochondrial matrix (in bacteria, the cytosol). Glucose isn't the only fuel: amino acids and fatty acids are broken down to acetyl-CoA too, and enter the same cycle.
If the first step of the citric acid cycle were blocked, what would most likely happen?

Oxidative phosphorylation would drop. With acetyl-CoA unable to enter the cycle, few NADH and FADH₂ are made to feed the electron transport chain. Glycolysis could still partly oxidize glucose, and NAD would pile up in its oxidized form (NAD⁺), not its reduced form.

STEP 7 · STAGE 4 OF 4 · IN THE INNER MITOCHONDRIAL MEMBRANE

The electron transport chain

The loaded carriers finally cash in. Their electrons are passed down a chain of carriers in the membrane, and the energy is turned into a proton gradient, then into ATP.

Start with the whole chain, then follow the electrons.
STAGE 4 · OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATIONA chain of carriers in the inner membraneThe electron transport chain is a series of protein complexes sitting in the inner mitochondrial membrane (the membranes of the cristae). NADH and FADH₂ drop off their high-energy electrons, and the electrons are handed down the chain to oxygen.The energy released at the hand-offs pumps protons (H⁺) out of the matrix. The protons flowing back in power ATP synthase. Tap each part.REDOX CHECKEvery hand-off is a redox: each carrier is oxidized as it passes the electrons on, and the next one is reduced.
1 · COMPLEX INADH drops its electrons at Complex INADH gives 2 high-energy electrons to Complex I and becomes NAD⁺ again, free to go back and be loaded in glycolysis and the citric acid cycle.Complex I uses the energy to pump H⁺ out of the matrix, against their concentration gradient. It is a uniporter: one kind of ion, H⁺, moved one way (active transport, paid for by the electrons, not by ATP).REDOX CHECKNADH is oxidized to NAD⁺; Complex I is reduced.
2 · COMPLEX IIFADH₂ drops its electrons at Complex IIFADH₂ hands its electrons directly to Complex II, and becomes FAD. They join the chain a step lower down.Complex II doesn't pump protons. That's one reason FADH₂ is worth less: about 2× the energy of an ATP, against about 3× for NADH.REDOX CHECKFADH₂ is oxidized to FAD; Complex II is reduced.
3 · QQ carries electrons through the membraneQ (ubiquinone) is a small carrier that moves within the membrane. It picks up electrons from Complex I and from Complex II and carries them to Complex III.Complex III pumps more H⁺ out: another uniporter, H⁺ one way, matrix → intermembrane space.
4 · CYTOCHROME CCyt c shuttles electrons from III to IVCytochrome c is a small protein sitting on the outer face of the inner membrane, in the intermembrane space. It shuttles electrons from Complex III to Complex IV.
5 · THE FINAL ELECTRON ACCEPTOROxygen takes the electronsComplex IV hands the electrons to oxygen, which pulls on electrons harder than anything in the chain. With protons from the matrix, it becomes water:O₂ + 4 e⁻ + 4 H⁺ → 2 H₂O (on the slide, per pair of electrons: ½ O₂ + 2 e⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O). Complex IV pumps H⁺ out too: the third uniporter.REDOX CHECKOxygen is reduced to water. It is the last acceptor: the electrons end here.
6 · THE GRADIENTProtons crowd outside the matrixComplexes I, III and IV pump H⁺ out of the matrix into the intermembrane space. Now there is a high H⁺ concentration outside and a low one inside.Moving H⁺ against its gradient takes energy (+ΔG), paid for by the falling electrons. That energy is now stored in the gradient, like water behind a dam.
7 · OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATIONProtons flow back through ATP synthaseAt the end of the chain sits one more membrane protein, ATP synthase: the only way the H⁺ can get back into the matrix. They flow through its channel from high to low concentration, down their gradient (−ΔG); that turns its head like a motor, which joins ADP + Pᵢ → ATP.This is oxidative phosphorylation: about 30 ATP per glucose, most of the total. The electrons never make ATP directly; they pump protons, and the protons make ATP (this is called chemiosmosis).
TWO WAYS TO MAKE ATP
Substrate-level phosphorylation: an enzyme moves a phosphate from a substrate onto ADP (glycolysis, the citric acid cycle). Oxidative phosphorylation: a proton gradient drives ATP synthase (here). This one makes most of the ATP.
WHO MOVES H⁺?
Complexes I, III and IV are uniporters: each pumps H⁺ one way, out of the matrix, against the gradient (low → high). Complex II does not pump. ATP synthase lets H⁺ back in, down the gradient (high → low), and makes ATP. Q and cyt c are the shuttles: Q inside the membrane, cyt c on its outer face.
WHICH STEPS BUILD THE GRADIENT?
Only Complexes I, III and IV. Each sits at a big drop in the electrons' energy, enough to pay for pushing H⁺ uphill, and each is built as a pump. At Complex II, FADH₂'s electrons drop only a little: not enough, and II isn't a pump. That's one reason FADH₂ is worth less (about 2× an ATP, against 3× for NADH). So it works like a threshold: a hand-off pumps only if the drop is big enough and a pump is there to use it.
True or false: the energy from electrons moving down the chain is used directly to make ATP.

False. It pumps protons. The proton gradient powers ATP synthase.

GLYCOLYSIS, FOR THE EXAM
Cytoplasm, no oxygen needed. 10 steps in 3 phases: preparatory (1–3), cleavage (4–5), payoff (6–10). Why spend 2 ATP? The phosphates trap glucose in the cell and make it unstable, ready to split. PFK runs the 2nd ATP-consuming reaction, and is the one enzyme to know by name. Net: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate: a partial oxidation.
WHY SPEND 2 ATP FIRST?
Adding a phosphate to glucose is endergonic on its own (+ΔG, not spontaneous). Coupled to ATP → ADP + Pᵢ, which is exergonic (−ΔG), the pair is exergonic overall, so it runs: reaction coupling. The phosphates make glucose less stable, ready to be split. That's the energy investment phase; the payoff phase earns the 2 ATP back and more.
PYRUVATE PROCESSING
Across both mitochondrial membranes into the matrix. Per pyruvate: 1 CO₂, 1 NADH, and coenzyme A (made from a B vitamin) joins: acetyl-CoA, which still has lots of potential energy.
THE CITRIC ACID CYCLE
Matrix (in bacteria, the cytosol). Per turn: 2 CO₂, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP; two turns per glucose. Each NADH and FADH₂ marks an oxidation of the cycle's molecules. Its major products are NADH and FADH₂ (NADH ≈ 3×, FADH₂ ≈ 2× an ATP). One step: succinate + FAD → fumarate + FADH₂ (succinate oxidized, FAD reduced). Amino acids and fatty acids also enter as acetyl-CoA.
WHICH MEMBRANE?
Mitochondria have two membranes. Pyruvate crosses both into the matrix, where the cycle runs. The ETC and ATP synthase sit in the inner membrane, the same one, folded into cristae: they face the matrix on one side and the intermembrane space on the other.
THE NAMES TO KNOW
From the slides: phosphofructokinase (PFK), the 2nd ATP-consuming reaction, is the one glycolysis enzyme to know by name. The rest go by what they do: the 1st ATP-consuming reaction, the NADH-producing reaction, the ATP-producing reactions (substrate-level phosphorylation). In the ETC: Complexes I to IV, Q, cytochrome c and ATP synthase (the purple turbine: oxidative phosphorylation, driven by H⁺).
HOW MUCH ATP?
About 30 from the electron transport chain (your professor's number; some slides say about 28) plus 4 by substrate-level phosphorylation: about 34 per glucose. Why "about"? The 4 made directly are exact. The ETC's share isn't: it depends on how many H⁺ get pumped per NADH and how many H⁺ ATP synthase needs per ATP (neither is a whole number), some H⁺ leak back across the membrane, the gradient also powers other work, and NADH made in the cytoplasm costs a little to bring into the mitochondrion. You need the relative amounts: the ETC makes most of it.

Same start, two endings: animals and yeast

Glycolysis needs NAD⁺ as a substrate. With oxygen, the ETC turns NADH back into NAD⁺. Without oxygen, the ETC is stopped, so fermentation does it instead: pyruvate (or a piece of it) takes NADH's electrons, and NAD⁺ is regenerated.

These are anaerobic reactions. They make no extra ATP; the cell gets only glycolysis' 2 ATP per glucose.

ANIMALS VS. YEAST
Animals and many bacteria: pyruvate → lactic acid (3 C). No CO₂.
Yeast: pyruvate → CO₂ + acetaldehyde → ethanol (2 C). CO₂ released.
Both: NADH → NAD⁺, 2 ATP per glucose from glycolysis.
REDOX CHECK
NADH is oxidized back to NAD⁺. What takes its electrons is reduced, its charge reduced: pyruvate in animals, acetaldehyde in yeast.
WHY LACTIC ACID BUILDS UP
It's the leftover of the NAD⁺ loop. Every time NADH is emptied into pyruvate, one lactic acid is left behind. No oxygen means no way to send it on, so it accumulates as long as glycolysis keeps going this way.
RESPIRATION OR FERMENTATION?
Same glycolysis either way. If an electron acceptor like oxygen is there, pyruvate goes on to respiration and lots of ATP. If not, fermentation keeps glycolysis going.
Why does fermentation make so little ATP?

Because it stops after glycolysis. Pyruvate's energy is not taken apart in the cycle or cashed in at the ETC; fermentation only recycles NAD⁺ so glycolysis can make its 2 ATP.

STEP 4 · CONTROL · REGULATING RESPIRATION

Regulating respiration: PFK and feedback inhibition

Is respiration happening all the time, as fast as it can? No: it's regulated. If there's plenty of ATP already, the cell waits to break down glucose until it needs more. The big example is PFK, which is regulated allosterically and sets the pace of glycolysis.

1Feedback inhibition: the product comes back to the start

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
1 · A PATHWAY IS A CHAIN OF ENZYMESStart → intermediates → productMost of the steps in glycolysis and the citric acid cycle are each run by an enzyme. A pathway is a chain: enzyme 1 turns the starting molecule into an intermediate, enzyme 2 turns that into the next one, and so on to the product.
2 · THE PRODUCT COMES BACKFeedback: the end feeds back to the beginningEnzymes can be regulated, and sometimes the regulator is one of the products of the pathway itself. When the product builds up, it comes back to enzyme 1, at the very start.
3 · ENZYME 1 CHANGES SHAPEFeedback inhibition: presence of product inhibits enzyme 1The product doesn't block enzyme 1's active site. It binds a different place, the regulatory (allosteric) site. That changes enzyme 1's shape, the active site no longer fits its substrate, and enzyme 1 slows down. Because every later step waits on enzyme 1, the whole pathway slows.So an allosteric inhibitor acts like a noncompetitive inhibitor: it doesn't compete with the substrate; it changes the enzyme. When the product is used up, it lets go, and enzyme 1 speeds back up. That's the loop.
4 · THE BIG ONE: GLYCOLYSISATP feeds back on PFKRespiration's main product is ATP. When ATP piles up, it binds PFK's regulatory site, PFK changes shape and slows, and glycolysis (and everything after it) slows. That's how PFK regulates glycolysis. Next step: PFK up close.

THE BIG IDEA
PFK regulates glycolysis. Lots of ATP (the product) binds PFK's regulatory site and slows it; lots of ADP (ATP spent) binds there instead and activates it. The cell makes ATP only when it needs it.
FEEDBACK INHIBITION
The product of a pathway inhibits an enzyme near the start of that pathway. Lots of product → the pathway slows. Product used up → it speeds back up.
COMPETITIVE VS. ALLOSTERIC
Competitive: active site; same Vmax, higher Km; beat it with more substrate.
Allosteric (noncompetitive): another site, shape change; lower Vmax; more substrate doesn't help.
WHY PFK?
PFK's step is early in glycolysis, in the energy investment phase: once fructose gets its second phosphate, it's headed down glycolysis. Controlling it controls the whole pathway.
The relative levels of what two molecules regulate PFK?

ATP and ADP. Both can bind the regulatory site: ADP activates PFK, ATP represses it.

The 4 ATP made directly (substrate-level) are exact. WHY KEEP ATP SMALL?
Most jobs in a cell only need a little energy. Breaking glucose's energy into small ATP coins means the cell can pay for each job without wasting the rest.

THE BIG IDEA

Food stores energy in electrons that are shared evenly between carbon and hydrogen. Respiration moves those electrons, one controlled step at a time, onto carriers the cell can spend. What is left of the glucose leaves as CO₂.

SECTION 1

Energy lives in electrons

  • An electron's potential energy depends on where it sits. Far from a positive nucleus, it has a lot. Pulled in close to one, it has little.
  • In a nonpolar bond (C–C, C–H) the two atoms share electrons almost evenly, so the electrons stay relatively far from either nucleus. These bonds are energy-rich.
  • In a polar bond (O–H, C=O) oxygen pulls the electrons toward itself. These bonds are energy-poor.
  • Respiration turns fuel bonds into exhaust bonds. Glucose goes in, CO₂ and H₂O come out, and the energy difference is what the cell captures.
  • Plants build fuel bonds using sunlight. Animals can't, so we eat molecules that already have them.
THINK ABOUT IT Gasoline is almost all C–C and C–H bonds. Water is all O–H. That is why one burns and the other puts out fires.
SECTION 2

Redox reactions

CHARGE ↑Oxidized: its charge goes up
CHARGE ↓Reduced: its charge goes down
  • Redox reactions are electron hand-offs. They are always paired: if one molecule's charge goes up, another's goes down.
  • In cells, electrons usually travel with a proton as a hydrogen atom. Losing H means oxidized. Picking up H means reduced.
  • Example: a carbon in sugar gives up 2 electrons and 2 H⁺. NAD⁺ takes the 2 electrons and 1 H⁺, becoming NADH. The other H⁺ floats off into solution.
  • In respiration, glucose is the first donor and oxygen is the last acceptor. When O₂ is reduced, it becomes water.

SECTION 4 CYTOPLASM · NO O₂ NEEDED

Glycolysis

"Sugar splitting." Ten enzyme steps turn one glucose (6 carbons) into two pyruvate (3 carbons each). You only need the three phases, not every step.

1 · INVEST2 ATP add phosphates to glucose, making fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. This is the one intermediate worth knowing by name.
2 · SPLITThe 6-carbon sugar breaks into two 3-carbon molecules of G3P (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate).
3 · PAYOFFEach G3P becomes pyruvate, making 1 NADH and 2 ATP along the way.
GOES IN1 glucose · 2 ATP · 2 NAD⁺
COMES OUT2 pyruvate · 4 ATP (net 2) · 2 NADH
  • Glycolysis makes ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation: an enzyme moves a phosphate directly from a sugar molecule onto ADP.
  • Only partial oxidation happens here. The two pyruvates still hold most of glucose's energy.
SECTION 5 MITOCHONDRIAL MATRIX

Pyruvate processing

  • Pyruvate moves into the mitochondrion. One of its 3 carbons is cut off and leaves as CO₂.
  • The remaining 2-carbon piece is attached to coenzyme A (built from a B vitamin), giving acetyl-CoA.
  • Each pyruvate makes 1 NADH, so 2 per glucose.
  • Bacteria have no mitochondria, so they run this step and the next one in their cytosol.
SECTION 6 MITOCHONDRIAL MATRIX

Citric acid cycle

  • Acetyl-CoA feeds into a loop of reactions. Its 2 carbons are stripped off as 2 CO₂, and the loop resets for the next one.
  • One turn makes 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂ and 1 ATP. One glucose gives two acetyl-CoA, so two turns.
  • Its real product is loaded electron carriers, not ATP.
2 acetyl-CoA + 6 NAD⁺ + 2 FAD + 2 ADP + 2 Pᵢ → 4 CO₂ + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂ + 2 ATP
