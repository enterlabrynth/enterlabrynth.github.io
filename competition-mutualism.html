Competition & Mutualism

Two ways species affect each other. Competition lowers fitness for both; mutualism raises it for both. Each one has its own flow chart below.

PART 1

Competition

Two questions decide how competition ends. How much do the two niches overlap? And is the competition even? Follow the arrows to see when one species is excluded and when both coexist.

Species 1 is the strong competitor in every graph Species 2 is the weak competitor
START · COMPETITION IS A −/− INTERACTION

Two species need the same limited resource

Both species lose fitness. It doesn't take fighting: using up a shared resource is enough.

Intraspecific · within one species Interspecific · between two species
QUESTION 1

How much do the two niches overlap?

The more two niches overlap, the stronger the competition. In nature, overlap is usually partial.

Completely: go to Track A Partially: go to Track B
TRACK A

Complete niche overlap

The two species need exactly the same resources. Question 2: is the competition even?

ASYMMETRIC COMPETITION One species loses much more fitness than the other. This is the more common case in nature.
GRAPH 1 · FITNESS, ALONE VS. TOGETHER

Both drop, because competition is −/−. Species 2 drops much further, so Species 1 is the stronger competitor.

GRAPH 2 · NICHE OVERLAP

The weak competitor is drowned out

↑ INDIVIDUALS USING THE RESOURCE
NICHE RANGE →
Species 1 · strong competitor Species 2 · weak, shrinking to zero

Species 2's whole niche sits inside Species 1's, so there is nothing it can use that Species 1 doesn't take first.

RESULT

Competitive exclusion

The stronger competitor drives the weaker one to local extinction. This is the competitive exclusion principle: two species with identical niches cannot coexist.

Classic example: Paramecium aurelia and P. caudatum grown in one flask. P. caudatum dies out, and even the winner grows to a lower density than it does alone.

SYMMETRIC COMPETITION Both species lose about the same amount of fitness. This is the less common case in nature.
GRAPH 1 · FITNESS, ALONE VS. TOGETHER

Same fitness level

↑ FITNESS Same fitness
Species 1 alone1 + 2 togetherSpecies 2 alone

Both drop by the same amount. Neither species is the stronger competitor.

GRAPH 2 · NICHE OVERLAP

Both shrink by the same amount

↑ INDIVIDUALS USING THE RESOURCE
NICHE RANGE →
Species 1 Species 2 Either species alone

The two niches are the same, and each population ends up equally smaller than it would be alone.

RESULT

Both lose, no clear winner

Each species is held to the same lowered fitness. Competitive exclusion needs asymmetric competition, so this branch does not end with one species driving out the other.

TRACK B

Partial niche overlap

The two species share some resources and each has some to itself. This is what we usually see in nature.

GRAPH 1 · BEFORE COMPETITION PLAYS OUT

Two niches, one shared zone

↑ INDIVIDUALS USING THE RESOURCE
overlap
NICHE RANGE →
Species 1 · strong competitor Species 2 · weak competitor Overlap, where they compete

Each curve is that species' fundamental niche: everything it could use if the other species weren't there. They compete only in the overlap.

GRAPH 2 · AFTER COMPETITION PLAYS OUT

The niches separate

↑ INDIVIDUALS USING THE RESOURCE
Fundamental niche Realized niche
NICHE RANGE →
Species 1 · niche unchanged Species 2 · fundamental (solid) Species 2 · realized (dashed)

The weak competitor gives up the shared zone and keeps only what Species 1 doesn't use. That smaller, dashed area is its realized niche.

RESULT

Species coexistence

Both species persist, because the weaker competitor uses the non-overlapping resources. Its realized niche is smaller than its fundamental niche. Splitting resources this way is called niche partitioning (or resource partitioning).

Classic example: Connell's barnacles. The upper-shore species can survive lower down, so the lower shore is in its fundamental niche. Competition from the lower-shore species keeps it high, so its realized niche is only the upper shore.

Competition vocab · 12 terms
Niche The range of resources and conditions a species uses. It can be plotted on axes such as temperature, rainfall or food type. A niche is not a habitat.
Niche overlap The part of two niches that both species use. More overlap means stronger competition.
Competition (−/−) An interaction that lowers the fitness of both species. If only one is harmed, it isn't competition.
Intraspecific vs. interspecific Competition within one species vs. between two different species.
Fitness How well individuals survive, grow and reproduce. It is the y-axis of the fitness graphs.
Asymmetric competition One species loses much more fitness than the other. More common in nature.
Symmetric competition Both species lose about the same amount of fitness.
Competitive exclusion principle Two species with identical niches cannot coexist. It happens when niches overlap completely and competition is asymmetric.
Fundamental niche Everything a species could use with no other species present.
Realized niche What a species actually uses once competitors are present. It is usually smaller than the fundamental niche.
Niche partitioning Competing species end up using different parts of the niche range, so their realized niches no longer overlap. Also called resource partitioning.
Coexistence Both species persist in the same place. It is the usual outcome when niches overlap only partly.
PART 2

Mutualism

A mutualism is a trade between two species. Whether it stays a mutualism comes down to one question: is the benefit bigger than the cost?

VIDEO · 5 MIN Mutualism: trade, cost, cheating This part follows the video scene by scene. Video walkthroughs are on the EnterLabrynth YouTube channel.
START · MUTUALISM IS A + / + INTERACTION

Two species trade, and both gain

Each species has something the other needs. Each one gives a benefit and gets one back, so both end up with higher fitness.

GRAPH · FITNESS, ALONE VS. TOGETHER

Both do better together

↑ FITNESS Both gain fitness
Species 1 alone1 + 2 togetherSpecies 2 alone

This is the competition graph flipped: there, both lines go down. Here, both go up.

THE PRICE

Nothing is free

COST
Making nectar, giving up sugars, housing a partner
BENEFIT
Nutrients, pollen delivered, protection

A mutualism always has a cost. It lasts because the benefit is bigger.

SYMBIOSIS A very close interaction between two species, evolved over a long time. Many mutualisms are one.
THE QUESTION

Which is bigger, the benefit or the cost?

Every trade has both. The same two species can move from one answer to the other when conditions change.

The benefit: go to Track A The cost: go to Track B
TRACK A · BENEFIT > COST

The trade holds

Three classic trades, all between a plant and a partner.

TRADE 1 · NITROGEN

Rhizobium + legumes

Plants need nitrogen but can't use the N₂ gas in air. Rhizobium bacteria live in bumps on legume roots called nodules, where they fix nitrogen: N₂ becomes ammonia the plant can use.

N₂ NH₃ inside a nodule
PLANT GETSNitrogen it can use
BACTERIA GETSugars and a home
TRADE 2 · PHOSPHATE

Mycorrhizal fungi

Roots can only reach the nutrients right next to them. Fungal threads grow out from the roots, reach much farther, and carry phosphate back.

90%+ of land plants have these fungal partners
PLANT GETSPhosphate and other nutrients
FUNGI GETSugars
TRADE 3 · POLLEN

Flowers + pollinators

A flower offers nectar, and the pollinator that comes for it carries pollen to the next flower.

  1. Pollination: pollen moves from an anther to a stigma.
  2. A pollen tube grows down to the ovule, where the egg waits.
  3. Fertilization: sperm meets egg.
  4. The ovule becomes the seed.
PLANT GETSPollen delivered
POLLINATOR GETSNectar
OVER TIME · SHAPED BY EACH OTHER

Coevolution

Partners shape each other. Each species is natural selection acting on the other, so the two evolve together.

COMET ORCHID Hides its nectar at the bottom of a foot-long spur
ITS MOTH Has a tongue just as long

Fundamental niche · alone Realized niche · with a mutualist

A mutualist can stretch a species' realized niche beyond its fundamental niche. That is the opposite of competition, which shrinks it.

RESULT

Mutualism, + / +

Both species gain fitness, and the realized niche can grow beyond the fundamental niche.

TRACK B · COST > BENEFIT

The trade tips over

Two ways it happens: a partner cheats, or conditions change.

WAY 1 · CHEATING

Taking the benefit without paying the cost

Because a mutualism has a cost, some partners skip it.

~22,000 orchid species
About half reward pollinators About half give no reward

The orchids that give nothing get pollinated anyway. This is deceit pollination.

WAY 2 · CONDITIONS CHANGE

Ants + Acacia trees

The tree gives food and hollow thorns to live in; the ants defend it from herbivores. But some ants also farm sap-sucking insects on the tree, so more ants means more sap lost.

↑ COST AND BENEFIT TO THE TREE
Mutualism + / + Parasitism
+ / −
NUMBER OF ANTS →
Benefit · levels off Cost · keeps rising

Few ants: benefit wins. Too many ants: cost wins, and the same partnership is now parasitism. The curves show the shape of the idea, not measured data.

RESULT

Parasitism, + / −

One species gains and the other loses. The same two species can move between the two sides as conditions change.

Lock it in

  1. Mutualism raises fitness for both species: + / +.
  2. There is always a cost. It lasts while the benefit beats the cost.
  3. Rhizobium fixes nitrogen. Mycorrhizal fungi supply phosphate.
  4. Pollen travels anther → stigma. The ovule becomes the seed.
  5. If the cost beats the benefit, mutualism becomes parasitism.
Mutualism vocab · 12 terms
Mutualism (+ / +) An interaction that raises the fitness of both species.
Symbiosis A very close interaction between two species, evolved over a long time.
Nitrogen fixation Turning N₂ gas into a form plants can use. Rhizobium does it inside root nodules.
Nodule A bump on a legume root that houses Rhizobium bacteria.
Mycorrhizal fungi Fungi that live on or in plant roots and supply phosphate in exchange for sugars.
Pollination Moving pollen from an anther, where it is made, to a stigma, where it is received.
Fertilization Sperm from the pollen meets the egg inside the ovule. The ovule then becomes the seed.
Coevolution Two interacting species each put selective pressure on the other, so they evolve together.
Cheating Taking the benefit of a mutualism without paying the cost.
Deceit pollination A flower gets pollinated without rewarding its pollinator. About half of orchid species do this.
Parasitism (+ / −) One species gains and the other loses. A mutualism becomes this when its cost outgrows its benefit.
Realized niche, with a mutualist Can be larger than the fundamental niche, because the partner lets the species live where it couldn't alone.
