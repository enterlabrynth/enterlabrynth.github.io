PART 1

Competition

THE QUESTION Two species want the same thing. Why is one sometimes driven to extinction, while other times both find a way to coexist?

Two competing species, two possible outcomes. Five steps get you to the answer: what a niche is, how niches overlap, whether the competition is even, how it ends, and two real examples.

STEP 1 · START HERE

First, what is a niche?

A nicheⓘThe unique set of environmental requirements of a species, both abiotic and biotic: the conditions and resources under which it can survive, grow and reproduce. A niche is not a habitat. is the unique set of environmental requirements of a species, both abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living): everything it needs to survive, grow and reproduce. Before two species can compete, each one has a niche of its own.

Overlap creates the potential for competition, and more overlap means stronger competition. In nature, niches usually overlap only partly.

WHAT COMPETITION IS

A − / − interaction

Two species need the same limited resource, each leaves less for the other, and both lose fitnessⓘHow well individuals survive, grow and reproduce. It is the y-axis of the fitness graphs.. It doesn't take fighting: using up a shared resource is enough.

Intraspecific · within one speciesInterspecific · between two species
KEEP THESE THREE APART

Three separate questions

  1. How much do the niches overlap? A little, a lot, or completely.
  2. Is the competition symmetric or asymmetric? That is about who is hurt more. Step 3.
  3. What is the outcome? Exclusion or coexistence. Step 4.

Overlap alone answers only the first question. It does not tell you whether the competition is even.

TWO THINGS THIS DOES NOT MEAN
  • Total overlap is not automatically symmetric. How much two niches overlap and how even the competition is are separate questions.

Mutualism

THE QUESTION If helping a partner always costs something, why do species do it, and why does it sometimes stop being help?

What counts as a mutualism?

HOW DEPENDENT IS EACH SPECIES?

Obligate vs. facultative

OBLIGATE MUTUALISMAt least one species depends on the relationship and can't get by without it.
FACULTATIVE MUTUALISMBoth species benefit, but each can live on its own.

This is about dependence only. It says nothing about how large or how equal the benefits are.

A RELATED WORD

Symbiosis

A very close interaction between two species that has evolved over a long time. Many mutualisms are symbioses. The two words are not the same, though: symbiosis is about how close the relationship is, and mutualism is about who benefits. A parasite living inside its host is a symbiosis too, and it is not a mutualism.

How do you tell what an interaction is? Compare each species' fitness with the other species present and without it. Up for both is mutualism. Down for both is competition.

STEP 2 · IT DEPENDS ON CONDITIONS

The same partnership can change sign

Costs and benefits are not fixed. They shift with the environment and with how many partners there are. A mutualism is a mutualism under those conditions.

WHY THE BALANCE MOVES

Context dependence

  • Under some conditions, the benefits outweigh the costs for both species. That is mutualism.
  • Change the conditions, for example the density of one of the species, and a cost can grow or a benefit can shrink.
  • If one partner's benefit only just cancels its cost, the interaction has slid to commensalism. If its cost is now bigger, it is parasitism.

It can run the other way too. Interactions can move between positive, neutral and negative as conditions change.

OVER MANY GENERATIONS

Coevolution: how a flower and its pollinator shape each other

In a close interaction, each species acts as natural selection on the other, so the two evolve in response to each other. That is coevolution. Here is how a flower and its pollinator can push each other to extremes.

1The flower hides its nectar deep
A moth has to push all the way in to reach it, and picks up pollen as it does.
2Deeper flowers get pollinated better
So natural selection favors longer nectar spurs.
3Longer tongues reach more nectar
So natural selection favors moths with longer tongues.
4Repeat for many generations
Each change in one species pushes the other. Both end up extreme.
REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE · THE MADAGASCAR COMET ORCHID
the orchid's nectar spur · about a foot longits moth's tongue · just as longnectar

A spur that deep needs a pollinator with a tongue that long, and a tongue that long needs a flower that deep. It is the classic picture of coevolution: each species acting as a selective pressure on the other.

STEP 4 · WHEN THE TRADE TIPS

From + / + to + / −

Because a mutualism has a cost, a partner can gain by skipping its side of the trade. That is cheatingⓘTaking the benefit of a mutualism without paying the cost.. Cheating can turn a mutualism into parasitism: if the cheated partner's costs now exceed the benefit it still gets, its + becomes a −, while the cheater keeps its +.

WHEN EXACTLY DOES IT TIP?

When one partner's cost passes its benefit

WAY 1 · A PARTNER CHEATSIt keeps taking the reward but stops paying its side. Its partner still pays the cost and now gets less back.
WAY 2 · CONDITIONS CHANGEThe benefit levels off while the cost keeps climbing, for example as one partner's numbers grow. Past the crossing point, cost wins.

Either way the signs go from + / + to + / −. The partner that is still gaining keeps its +.

THE BIG IDEA

Two species that live together change each other's fitness. Competition lowers it for both (− / −). Mutualism raises it for both (+ / +). To name any interaction, compare each species with the other one present and with it absent. And remember the answer can change when conditions change.

SECTION 1

The niche

  • A niche is the unique set of environmental requirements of a species, both abiotic (temperature, precipitation, light) and biotic (food, competitors, predators, parasites, mutualists).
  • A niche has many dimensions, one for every condition and resource that matters. A two-axis graph shows only two of them.
  • The niche reflects the interactions between a population and its physical and biological surroundings. Those interactions lead to adaptations, and adaptations let different species coexist by using different combinations of resources.
FUNDAMENTAL NICHEThe full range of conditions, habitats and resources a species could use.
REALIZED NICHEThe habitats it actually occupies and the resources it actually uses, given the species it interacts with.
  • The fundamental niche is usually bigger, because competitors, predators and parasites cut a species back.
  • The exception: a species with a mutualist. Its realized niche can be bigger than its fundamental niche.
COMMON MIX-UPA niche is not a habitat. The habitat is the place. The niche is everything the species needs and how it uses that place.
SECTION 2

Competition: − / −

  • Competition is an interaction in which both species have reduced fitness: lower growth, survival, reproduction or population growth rate.
  • Species compete for limited resources: food, space (including nesting sites), light, nutrients, water.
  • It does not need contact or fighting. Using up a shared resource is enough.
  • Intraspecific competition is within one species. Interspecific competition is between two species.
  • Competition is strongest where the niches overlap. More overlap, stronger competition. In nature, niches usually overlap only partly.
SYMMETRICEach species has a similar negative effect on the other. Less common.
ASYMMETRICOne species has a stronger negative effect on the other. More common in nature.
KEEP THESE THREE APARTHow much the niches overlap, how even the competition is, and how it ends are three separate questions. Complete overlap does not mean symmetric. Symmetric does not mean coexistence.

Connell's barnacle experiment

  • The intertidal zone is the strip of shore between the high tide line and the low tide line. The tide covers and uncovers it every day.
  • Barnacles are suspension feeders. They filter food from the water, so they can only feed while underwater. The lower shore is the better place to be.
  • What Connell noticed: two species in separate bands. The smaller Species A on the upper shore, the larger Species B on the lower shore.
HYPOTHESIS 1 · BIOTICSpecies B competitively excludes A from the lower shore.
HYPOTHESIS 2 · ABIOTICSpecies A cannot thrive in the physical conditions of the lower shore.
  • Setup: he removed Species B from one half of each rock and left the other half alone, then followed Species A.
  • Control: the untouched half, with both species present. Treatment: the half with B removed.
  • Result: where B was removed, A survived well on the lower shore. Where B stayed, A did poorly.
  • Conclusion: Hypothesis 1 is supported. Competition, not the physical conditions, keeps A off the lower shore.

B dries out too easily to live higher up, so its two niches are the same. The two species coexist because their niches overlap only partly: A keeps the zone B cannot use.

SECTION 5

Mutualism: + / +

  • A mutualism is an interaction in which both species have increased fitness: higher growth, survival, reproduction or population growth rate.
  • There is always a cost. Each partner pays something to take part.
  • The benefits outweigh the costs for both, but they do not have to be equal.
  • Many mutualisms are symbioses: very close interactions that have evolved over a long time. Symbiosis does not mean mutualism: it describes closeness, not who benefits.
  • The balance can change with conditions, so mutualisms are open to cheating.
OBLIGATEAt least one species depends on the relationship and cannot get by without it.
FACULTATIVEBoth benefit, but each can live on its own.
EFFECT ON THE NICHECompetition usually shrinks the realized niche. A mutualist can expand it. A legume with Rhizobium can grow in soil too poor in nitrogen for it to manage alone.

The same partnership can change sign

Costs and benefits are not fixed. They shift with the environment and with how many partners there are. A mutualism is a mutualism under those conditions.

WHY THE BALANCE MOVES

Context dependence

  • Under some conditions, the benefits outweigh the costs for both species. That is mutualism.
  • Change the conditions, for example the density of one of the species, and a cost can grow or a benefit can shrink.
  • If one partner's benefit only just cancels its cost, the interaction has slid to commensalism. If its cost is now bigger, it is parasitism.

It can run the other way too. Interactions can move between positive, neutral and negative as conditions change.

From + / + to + / −

Because a mutualism has a cost, a partner can gain by skipping its side of the trade. That is cheatingⓘTaking the benefit of a mutualism without paying the cost.. Cheating can turn a mutualism into parasitism: if the cheated partner's costs now exceed the benefit it still gets, its + becomes a −, while the cheater keeps its +.

14 / 15 · PART 2 · LOCK IT IN

Lock it in

  1. A niche is the conditions and resources a species needs. Fundamental is where it could live. Realized is where it does.
  2. Overlap creates competition (− / −). How much overlap, how even the competition is, and how it ends are three separate questions.
  3. Complete overlap with a stronger competitor ends in exclusion. Niches that differ enough allow coexistence.
  4. Mutualism is a net benefit for both species (+ / +). There is always a cost, and the benefits don't have to be equal.
  5. Obligate: at least one species depends on it. Facultative: both can live without it.
  6. Rhizobium fixes nitrogen. Mycorrhizal fungi supply phosphate. Pollen travels anther → stigma.
  7. If one partner's cost outgrows its benefit, + / + becomes + / −: parasitism.
  8. Competition often shrinks the realized niche. Mutualism can expand it.

QUESTION 1 OF 14A niche is best described as…

Correct. Not quite. The place is the habitat. The niche is everything the species needs, abiotic and biotic.

QUESTION 2 OF 14Two species' niches overlap a lot. What does that tell you?

Correct. Not quite. Overlap sets up competition. On its own it doesn't say whether the competition is even, or how it ends.

QUESTION 3 OF 14Competition is which kind of interaction?

Correct. Not quite. Both species end up with lower fitness.

QUESTION 4 OF 14In asymmetric competition…

Correct. Not quite. Both are still harmed. If only one were harmed, it would not be competition.

QUESTION 5 OF 14Competitive exclusion happens when…

Correct. Not quite. With complete overlap the weaker competitor has nowhere to escape to, so the stronger one drives it out.

QUESTION 6 OF 14P. aurelia won against P. caudatum. How did the winner do compared with growing alone?

Correct. Not quite. Even the winner is harmed by competition. That is why the interaction is − / −.

QUESTION 7 OF 14Removing Species B let Species A survive on the lower shore. What does that show about Species A?

Correct. Not quite. A could live in both zones. Competition from B restricts it to the upper shore.

QUESTION 8 OF 14In Connell's experiment, which half of the rock was the control?

Correct. Not quite. The control is the unmanipulated half. It shows what happens normally.

QUESTION 9 OF 14What makes an interaction a mutualism?

Correct. Not quite. There is always a cost, and the benefits don't have to be equal. What matters is a net positive for both.

QUESTION 10 OF 14What does Rhizobium give its legume host?

Correct. Not quite. It fixes N₂ gas into ammonia inside root nodules. The plant pays in sugars and a home.

QUESTION 11 OF 14Mycorrhizal fungi mainly help plants…

Correct. Not quite. Their threads reach much farther through the soil than roots do. The plant pays in sugars.

QUESTION 12 OF 14Obligate mutualism means…

Correct. Not quite. Obligate vs. facultative is about dependence, not about how big or equal the benefits are.

QUESTION 13 OF 14Ants on an Acacia farm sap-feeding insects, and the tree's fitness ends up lower with the ants than without them. The interaction is now…

Correct. Not quite. The ants still benefit. Farming sap-feeders does not always harm the tree, but here its costs are bigger than its benefits, and that is what makes it parasitism.

QUESTION 14 OF 14With a mutualist, a species' realized niche can be…

Correct. Not quite. A competitor usually shrinks the realized niche. A mutualist can expand it.

Guiding questions · competition

These are the questions this part is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q1  What is an ecological niche? What is niche overlap? Do the niches of two species usually overlap exactly?
A niche is the unique set of environmental requirements of a species, both abiotic and biotic. It can be drawn on axes such as temperature, precipitation or food type, and it has many dimensions.Niche overlap is the part of two niches that both species use.In nature, niches usually overlap only partly, not exactly.
Q2  Where is competition between two species strongest?
Where their niches overlap. The more two niches overlap, the stronger the competition.
Q3  How is competition defined? What do different species compete for?
Competition is the interaction in which both species have reduced fitness: − / −.They compete for limited resources: food, space (including nesting sites), light, nutrients and water.
Q4  Intraspecific vs. interspecific competition? Asymmetric vs. symmetric? Which is more common in nature?
Intraspecific: between individuals of the same species. Interspecific: between individuals of different species.Symmetric: each species has a similar negative effect on the other. Asymmetric: one species has a stronger negative effect on the other.Asymmetric competition is more common in nature.
Q5  Does competition always involve direct contact or fighting?
No. Using up a shared, limited resource is enough to lower the other species' fitness.
Q6  What is competitive exclusion? When does it occur?
The stronger competitor drives the weaker one to local extinction. The principle: two species with identical niches cannot coexist.It occurs when competition is asymmetric and the niches overlap completely.
Q7  In the Paramecium graph, what makes one species' two curves differ? Which species won? Was the winner's fitness higher alone or together?
The only difference between a species' two curves is whether the other species was in the flask.P. aurelia won: P. caudatum was driven out.The winner's fitness was higher alone. Even the winner is harmed by competition.
Q8  What is the intertidal zone? What did Connell notice about two barnacle species there? How do barnacles feed?
The intertidal zone is the strip of shore between the high tide line and the low tide line, covered and uncovered by the tide every day.Connell noticed the two species live in separate bands: the smaller Species A on the upper shore, the larger Species B on the lower shore.Barnacles are suspension feeders. They filter food from the water, so they can only feed while underwater.
Q9  What experiment did Connell do? What were his two competing hypotheses? Which one did the results support?
He removed Species B from one half of each rock, left the other half alone, and followed how Species A did.Hypothesis 1: Species B competitively excludes A from the lower shore. Hypothesis 2: A can't thrive in the physical conditions of the lower shore.A survived on the lower shore once B was gone, so Hypothesis 1 was supported.
Q10  How can you tell what effect an interaction has on a species?
Compare its fitness (growth, survival, reproduction) with the other species present and with it absent. If fitness is lower with the other species, the effect is negative. If it is higher, the effect is positive.
Q11  How do we know which side of the rock was the control in Connell's experiment?
The control is the side that was left unmanipulated, with both species present. The side where Species B was removed is the treatment.
Q12  What is the fundamental niche? The realized niche? Which is usually bigger, and what is the exception?
Fundamental niche: the full range of conditions, habitats and resources a species could use.Realized niche: the habitats it actually occupies and the resources it actually uses, given the other species it interacts with.The fundamental niche is usually bigger. The exception is a species with a mutualist, whose realized niche can be bigger than its fundamental niche.
Q13  For each barnacle species, which parts of the intertidal zone are its fundamental niche and its realized niche?
Species A: fundamental niche is the upper and the lower shore. Realized niche is the upper shore only.Species B: fundamental niche is the lower shore. Realized niche is the lower shore too.

Guiding questions · mutualism

These are the questions this part is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q14  What is a mutualistic interaction? What does it do to the fitness of both partners? What are the key points about mutualisms?
A mutualism is an interaction in which both species have increased fitness: + / +.Key points: there is always a cost; the benefits outweigh the costs for both species but don't have to be equal; many mutualisms are symbioses; and the balance can change with conditions, so mutualisms are open to cheating.
Q15  What are Rhizobium bacteria? Where do they live? What do they give their host, and what do they get?
Nitrogen-fixing bacteria that live in nodules on the roots of legumes such as soybeans, peas and alfalfa.They give the plant nitrogen in a usable form: they turn N₂ gas into ammonia.They get sugars and a home in the roots.
Q16  What are mycorrhizal fungi? Where do they live? What do they give their host, and what do they get? How common are they?
Fungi that live in and on plant roots.They help the plant take up phosphate and other nutrients from the soil.They get carbohydrates (sugars) and shelter.More than 90% of land plants have them.
Q17  Who fixes nitrogen in the ocean?
Trichodesmium, also called sea straw. Rhizobium is not the only nitrogen fixer: some other bacteria and some archaea can fix nitrogen too.
Q18  How have mutualisms affected land plants? What are angiosperms?
Mutualisms have been fundamentally important to the evolution of land plants. Partnerships with pollinators were critical to the diversification of the angiosperms.Angiosperms are the flowering plants.
Q19  How do angiosperms reproduce? What is pollination? What is fertilization? Which structure holds the baby plant?
Pollination: pollen, which produces the sperm, is moved from an anther to a stigma.Fertilization: a sperm fertilizes the egg inside the ovule, and a plant embryo forms.The embryo, the baby plant, is held in the seed.
Q20  What do haploid and diploid mean? What is mitosis?
Haploid: a cell with one set of chromosomes. Diploid: a cell with two sets, one from each parent.Mitosis: cell division that makes two cells genetically identical to the original.Only the basics are needed here.
Q21  What is coevolution? What is notable about the Madagascar comet orchid?
Coevolution is the mutual, reciprocal evolutionary influence between two interacting species: each one exerts selective pressure on the other, so they evolve together.The comet orchid has a nectar spur about a foot long, matched by a moth pollinator with a tongue just as long.
Q22  What does it mean to say a mutualist is cheating?
It takes the benefit of the mutualism without paying the cost. Example: about half of orchid species get pollinated without rewarding their pollinators.
Q23  Under what conditions is the ant and Acacia interaction a mutualism? Under what conditions is it parasitism?
Mutualism: when the ants protect the tree from herbivores and competing plants, so the tree gains more than its rewards cost.Parasitism: in some cases, when the ants farm phloem-feeding insects on the tree and that costs the tree more than their protection is worth. The ants still gain; the tree now loses.
