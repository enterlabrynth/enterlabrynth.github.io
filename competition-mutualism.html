Competition & Mutualism

Two ways species affect each other. Competition lowers fitness for both; mutualism raises it for both. Each part builds the idea first, then brings in the real-life examples.

PART 1

Competition

THE QUESTION When two species compete, why do we sometimes see one species driven out, but other times see coexistence?

Five steps get you to the answer: what a niche is, how niches overlap, whether the competition is even, how it ends, and two real examples.

Species 1 is blue in every graph Species 2 is yellow
STEP 1 · START HERE

First, what is a niche?

A niche is the range of environmental conditions and resources under which a species can survive, grow and reproduce. Before two species can compete, each one has a niche of its own.

A real niche has an axis for every condition and resource that matters to the species. A niche is not the same thing as a habitat: the habitat is the place, the niche is how the species uses it.

TWO KINDS OF NICHE

Fundamental vs. realized

FUNDAMENTAL NICHEWhere a species could live with no competitors or other species holding it back. The oval in the graph is one.
REALIZED NICHEWhere it actually lives once other species are there. Competition usually cuts it down to less than the fundamental niche.

STEP 2 · ADD A SECOND SPECIES

Niches can overlap, and overlap sets up competition

Put a second species on the same axes. Wherever the two niches overlap, both species use the same conditions and resources.

Overlap creates the potential for competition, and more overlap means stronger competition. In nature, niches usually overlap only partly.

WHAT COMPETITION IS

A − / − interaction

Two species need the same limited resource, each leaves less for the other, and both lose fitness. It doesn't take fighting: using up a shared resource is enough.

Intraspecific · within one speciesInterspecific · between two species
KEEP THESE THREE APART

Three separate questions

  1. How much do the niches overlap? A little, a lot, or completely.
  2. Is the competition symmetric or asymmetric? That is about who is hurt more. Step 3.
  3. What is the outcome? Exclusion or coexistence. Step 4.

Overlap alone answers only the first question. It does not tell you whether the competition is even.

STEP 3 · WHO IS HURT MORE?

Symmetric vs. asymmetric competition

Competition always lowers fitness for both species. This step asks whether it lowers them by the same amount. Fitness means how well individuals survive, grow and reproduce. Each graph compares a species grown alone with the two grown together.

SYMMETRIC COMPETITION Each species has a similar negative effect on the other. Less common in nature.
GRAPH · FITNESS, ALONE VS. TOGETHER

↑ FITNESS Same fitness

The two arrows fall equally. Neither species is the stronger competitor.

ASYMMETRIC COMPETITION One species has a stronger negative effect on the other. More common in nature.
GRAPH · FITNESS, ALONE VS. TOGETHER

One drops much further

↑ FITNESS Higher fitness Lower fitness

Both drop, because competition is − / −. Species 2 drops much further, so Species 1 is the stronger competitor.

TWO THINGS THIS DOES NOT MEAN
  • Total overlap is not automatically symmetric. How much two niches overlap and how even the competition is are separate questions.
  • Symmetric does not automatically mean coexistence. Whether both species last depends on whether their niches differ enough to reduce competition.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Symmetric competition

Each species has a similar negative effect on the other. Less common in nature.

Asymmetric competition

One species has a stronger negative effect on the other. More common in nature.

Fitness

How well individuals survive, grow and reproduce. It is the y-axis of the fitness graphs.

STEP 4 · THE OUTCOME

Exclusion or coexistence?

Now the opening question has an answer. It comes down to whether the two niches differ enough to take the pressure off.

OUTCOME A · COMPETITIVE EXCLUSION Can happen when competition is too strong to escape: the niches overlap completely and one species is the better competitor.

Species 2's whole niche sits inside Species 1's, so there is nothing it can use that Species 1 doesn't take first.

RESULT

Competitive exclusion

The stronger competitor drives the weaker one to local extinction. The competitive exclusion principle: two species with identical niches cannot coexist.

OUTCOME B · COEXISTENCE Can happen when the niches differ enough. The overlap is only partial, so each species has something the other doesn't take.

Each curve is that species' fundamental niche: everything it could use if the other species weren't there. They compete only in the overlap.

The weak competitor gives up the shared zone and keeps only what Species 1 doesn't use. That smaller, dashed area is its realized niche.

RESULT

Species coexistence

Both species persist, because the weaker competitor keeps the part of its niche the other doesn't use. Its realized niche is smaller than its fundamental niche. Splitting a niche this way is niche partitioning.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Competitive exclusion

The stronger competitor drives the weaker one out. The principle: two species with identical niches cannot coexist.

Coexistence

Both species persist in the same place, because their niches differ enough to reduce competition.

Niche partitioning

Competing species end up using different parts of the niche, so their realized niches barely overlap. Also called resource partitioning.

STEP 5 · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES

Two classic studies, and what each one teaches

Both are worth knowing by name. Each one is the standard example for one of the ideas above.

EXAMPLE 1 · TEACHES COMPETITIVE EXCLUSION

Paramecium aurelia and Paramecium caudatum

Two species of single-celled Paramecium, grown in flasks on the same food. Grown alone, each one thrives. Grown together, they need exactly the same thing.

↑ POPULATION DENSITY · CELLS PER mL
TIME · DAYS →
P. aurelia alone P. aurelia together P. caudatum alone P. caudatum together

P. caudatum is driven out: that is competitive exclusion. And the winner still loses something: P. aurelia levels off lower with a competitor than it does alone. Both fitnesses dropped, so it is − / −, and the drop was uneven, so it is asymmetric.

The curves show the shape of the result, not the measured data.

EXAMPLE 2 · TEACHES FUNDAMENTAL VS. REALIZED NICHE

Connell's barnacles

On rocky Atlantic shores, a small barnacle (Species A) lives high on the rocks and a larger one (Species B) lives lower down. Barnacles can only feed underwater, so lower is the better spot. Why isn't A down there too?

HYPOTHESIS 1 · BIOTICSpecies B outcompetes A and excludes it from the lower shore.
HYPOTHESIS 2 · ABIOTICSpecies A can't thrive in the physical conditions of the lower shore.
THE EXPERIMENTConnell removed Species B from one half of each rock and left the other half alone as the control. Where B was gone, A survived on the lower shore. Hypothesis 1 was supported.

Species A could live in both zones, but competition from B holds it to the upper shore: its realized niche is smaller than its fundamental niche. Species B dries out too easily to live high up, so its two niches are the same, and that is also why B never pushes A off the upper shore.

PART 2

Mutualism

THE QUESTION If helping a partner always costs something, why do species do it, and why does it sometimes stop being help?

Same approach as Part 1: the idea first, then the real-life examples, then what happens when the balance shifts.

STEP 1 · START HERE

What counts as a mutualism?

A mutualism is an interaction in which both species end up with a net benefit: each has higher fitness with its partner than without it. That makes it + / +.

GRAPH · FITNESS, ALONE VS. TOGETHER

Both do better together

↑ FITNESS Both gain fitness
Species 1 alone1 + 2 togetherSpecies 2 alone

Both arrows go up, the reverse of competition. They don't go up by the same amount, and they don't have to.

THE PRICE

Nothing is free

Each partner pays a cost to take part and gets a benefit back. What makes it a mutualism is that the benefit is bigger than the cost for each species.

Species 1

BENEFIT
Small cost, large benefit. Net effect: strongly positive.
Species 2
COST
BENEFIT
Bigger cost, smaller benefit. Net effect: still positive.

The trade does not have to be 50 / 50. It only has to leave each species better off overall.

HOW DEPENDENT ARE THE PARTNERS?

Obligate vs. facultative

OBLIGATE MUTUALISMAt least one partner depends on the relationship and can't get by without it.
FACULTATIVE MUTUALISMBoth partners benefit, but each can live on its own.

This is about dependence only. It says nothing about how large or how equal the benefits are.

A RELATED WORD

Symbiosis

A very close interaction between two species that has evolved over a long time. Many mutualisms are symbioses.

How do you tell what an interaction is? Compare each species' fitness with the other species present and without it. Up for both is mutualism. Down for both is competition.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Mutualism (+ / +)

An interaction in which both species end up with a net benefit: higher fitness with the partner than without it.

Obligate mutualism

At least one partner depends on the relationship and cannot get by without it. This is about dependence, not about how big or equal the benefits are.

Facultative mutualism

Both partners benefit, but each can live on its own.

Symbiosis

A very close interaction between two species, evolved over a long time. Many mutualisms are one.

STEP 2 · IT DEPENDS ON CONDITIONS

The same partnership can change sign

Costs and benefits are not fixed. They shift with the environment and with how many partners there are. A mutualism is a mutualism under those conditions.

WHY THE BALANCE MOVES

Context dependence

  • Under some conditions, the benefits outweigh the costs for both species. That is mutualism.
  • Change the conditions, for example the density of one of the species, and a cost can grow or a benefit can shrink.
  • If one partner's benefit only just cancels its cost, the interaction has slid to commensalism. If its cost is now bigger, it is parasitism.

It can run the other way too. Interactions can move between positive, neutral and negative as conditions change.

THE SLIDE, FROM ONE PARTNER'S SIDE

Watch the second sign

+ / 0Commensalism
One still gains. The other breaks even.
+ / −Parasitism

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Commensalism (+ / 0)

One species benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.

Parasitism (+ / −)

One species gains and the other has a net cost. A mutualism becomes this when one partner's cost outgrows its benefit.

STEP 3 · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES

Four mutualisms to know

Each card shows what each partner gives, what it gets, what it costs, and why both still come out ahead.

EXAMPLE 1 · NITROGEN

Legumes + Rhizobium

Plants need nitrogen but can't use the N₂ gas in air. Rhizobium bacteria live in bumps on legume roots called nodules, where they fix nitrogen: they turn N₂ into a form the plant can use.

N₂ NH₃ inside a nodule

Rhizobium is not the only nitrogen fixer. Some other bacteria and some archaea can do it, including Trichodesmium in the ocean.

EXAMPLE 3 · POLLEN

Flowers + pollinators

A flower offers nectar. The pollinator that comes for it carries pollen to the next flower.

  2. A pollen tube grows down to the ovule, where the egg waits.
  3. Fertilization: sperm meets egg.
  4. The ovule becomes the seed.

STEP 4 · WHEN THE TRADE TIPS

From + / + to + / −

Because a mutualism has a cost, a partner can gain by skipping its side of the trade. That is cheating. When it happens, one species keeps its + and the other's + turns into a −.

CASE 1 · ANTS + ACACIA

Ants that farm instead of defend

Some ants don't clear every herbivore off the tree. They raise phloem-feeding (sap-sucking) insects on it like cattle, and drink the sugary sap those insects collect from the tree.

WHEN THE ANTS DEFEND
Ants+Acacia+= mutualism
WHEN THE ANTS FARM SAP-FEEDERS
Ants+Acacia−= parasitism

Who benefits: the ants. They are still fed and housed, and now get sap as well. Who is harmed: the Acacia. It still pays for the rewards, and it loses sap, so its net fitness effect is negative. Under those conditions the ant and Acacia interaction is parasitic.

CASE 2 · ORCHIDS

Flowers that give no reward

~22,000 orchid species
About half reward pollinators About half give no reward

About half of orchid species get pollinated without offering anything. This is deceit pollination. The orchid still gains. The pollinator spends the visit and gets nothing back.

RESULT

Parasitism, + / −

One species gains and the other loses. Nothing about who the two species are has changed. What changed is the balance of cost and benefit for one of them.

Deceit pollination

STEP 5 · WHAT A PARTNER MAKES POSSIBLE

Mutualism can open doors

Competition usually restricts a species to less than its fundamental niche. Mutualism can do the reverse and expand what a species is able to do.

GRAPH · EFFECT ON THE NICHE

A partner opens doors

↑ NUMBER OF INDIVIDUALS LIVING THERE
ONE NICHE DIMENSION, E.G. SOIL NUTRIENTS →
Fundamental niche · alone Realized niche · with a mutualist

With a partner, a species can do well under conditions it could not manage alone. Its realized niche can stretch past its fundamental niche.

THE CONTRAST

Shrink it or grow it

WITH A COMPETITORRealized niche is smaller than the fundamental niche. This is the usual case.
WITH A MUTUALISTRealized niche can be larger than the fundamental niche. This is the exception to the rule.

Example: a legume with Rhizobium can grow in soil too poor in nitrogen for it to manage alone.

RESULT

Mutualism, + / +

Both species gain fitness, and a partner can let a species use conditions and resources it could not use as well alone.

Lock it in

  1. A niche is the conditions and resources a species needs. Fundamental is where it could live. Realized is where it does.
  2. Overlap creates competition (− / −). How much overlap, how even the competition is, and how it ends are three separate questions.
  3. Complete overlap with a stronger competitor ends in exclusion. Niches that differ enough allow coexistence.
  4. Mutualism is a net benefit for both species (+ / +). There is always a cost, and the benefits don't have to be equal.
  5. Obligate: at least one partner depends on it. Facultative: both can live without it.
  6. Rhizobium fixes nitrogen. Mycorrhizal fungi supply phosphate. Pollen travels anther → stigma.
  7. If one partner's cost outgrows its benefit, + / + becomes + / −: parasitism.
  8. Competition often shrinks the realized niche. Mutualism can expand it.
