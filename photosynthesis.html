MODULE 2.2 · PART 1

Where do glucose and O₂ come from?

THE QUESTIONA plant grows on water, a few nutrients and light. Where does its mass, and its O₂, come from?

Four steps, in the order of the lecture: the big questions, the whole plan (photo + synthesis), redox again, and the chloroplast.

STEP 1 · THE BIG QUESTIONS

Where do glucose and O₂ come from?

Our cells break glucose down with O₂ and give off CO₂ and H₂O (Module 2.1). Something has to build the glucose and the O₂ back up. That something is photosynthesis: the Earth's candy shop.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 3
THE BIG QUESTIONSHow does a plant grow on water and light?A hydroponic plant gets only water, a capful of nutrients and a grow light. Yet it keeps adding leaves, stems and roots. So where does all that glucose (reduced carbon) come from? And where does the O₂ it gives off come from?Big answer: photosynthesis. Tap each question.
1 · WHERE DOES GLUCOSE COME FROM?From CO₂ in the airThe carbon in every sugar the plant makes comes from CO₂, pulled out of the air. That's why biologists say all biomass comes from air: the carbon in living things was CO₂ before photosynthesis fixed it.The water and nutrients matter too, but they don't supply the carbon.
2 · WHERE DOES O₂ COME FROM?From splitting waterThe O₂ comes from water, split in the light reactions, not from CO₂. Chapman's slide: all of the O₂ on the planet comes from photosynthesis.
ALL BIOMASS COMES FROM AIR
The carbon in a tree, a leaf or you was once CO₂ in the air, fixed by photosynthesis. Discussion: "All of the carbon in living organisms came from CO₂ fixed by photosynthesis."
COMMON MIX-UP
The O₂ a plant gives off comes from water, not from CO₂. CO₂'s carbon ends up in sugar.
STEP 2 · THE WHOLE PLAN

Photo + synthesis

Light energy in, chemical energy out: the light reactions catch it, the Calvin cycle stores it in sugar.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
THE WHOLE PLAN6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂Photosynthesis takes the low-energy waste of our metabolism, CO₂ and H₂O, and builds high-energy glucose from it, giving off O₂. It's basically cellular respiration in reverse, with light paying the bill.The name has two halves, and so does the process. Tap each.
1 · PHOTOThe light reactions make O₂, ATP and NADPHIn the photo reactions, light energy is absorbed and used to make O₂, NADPH and ATP. They happen in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast.Products of the light reactions: O₂, ATP, NADPH. No sugar yet.
2 · SYNTHESISThe Calvin cycle builds sugar from CO₂In the synthesis reactions (the Calvin cycle), ATP and NADPH are the energy source used to turn CO₂ into carbohydrate. They happen in the stroma.Product of the synthesis reactions: carbohydrate, starting as G3P.
3 · HOW THEY CONNECTOne half charges, the other spendsThe light reactions charge ATP and NADPH; the Calvin cycle spends them and sends ADP, Pᵢ and NADP⁺ back to be recharged. Neither half can keep going without the other.
PHOTO VS. SYNTHESIS
Light reactions: thylakoid membranes · light → ATP + NADPH · split H₂O, release O₂.
Calvin cycle: stroma · ATP + NADPH + CO₂ → sugar · returns ADP, Pᵢ, NADP⁺.
NADPH VS. NADH
NADP⁺ and NADPH are very similar to NAD⁺ and NADH (an extra phosphate). Respiration loads NADH; photosynthesis loads NADPH.
STEP 3 · REDOX AGAIN

Track the charge: water oxidized, CO₂ reduced

The same redox idea as respiration, run the other way.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
REDOX REACTIONS AGAINElectrons move from water to CO₂Photosynthesis is a series of redox reactions. Electrons are pulled off water and end up on carbon, building sugar. Track the charge to see who is oxidized and who is reduced, the same way as in the chemistry review.
1 · WATER IS OXIDIZEDOxygen's charge goes up: −2 → 0In H₂O, oxygen's charge is −2. When water gives up its electrons, the oxygens pair up as O₂ with a charge of 0. The charge went up, so water is oxidized. That's where the O₂ comes from.REDOX CHECKWater is oxidized (oxygen −2 → 0).
2 · CO₂ IS REDUCEDCarbon's charge goes down: +4 → 0In CO₂, carbon's charge is +4. In sugar (CH₂O)ₙ it's 0. The charge went down: CO₂ is reduced, its charge literally reduced. Reduced carbon holds more potential energy.REDOX CHECKCO₂ is reduced (carbon +4 → 0).
3 · LIGHT PAYSUphill, so it takes energyIn respiration electrons run downhill, from glucose to O₂, and energy is released. Photosynthesis pushes them back uphill, from water to CO₂. That takes energy, and light supplies it.
THE SWAP IN ONE LINE
Water's oxygen: charge −2 → 0 (oxidized, becomes O₂). CO₂'s carbon: charge +4 → 0 (reduced, becomes sugar).
WHERE IT HAPPENS
In plants and algae, in chloroplasts. In some bacteria, on the plasma membrane and in the cytosol.
STEP 4 · WHERE IT HAPPENS

Inside the chloroplast

Two halves, two places: the light reactions in the thylakoid membranes, the Calvin cycle in the stroma.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
PHOTOSYNTHESIS OCCURS IN CHLOROPLASTSThe solar-powered factoryIn plants, cells that photosynthesize typically have 40–50 chloroplasts. Each one captures light energy to make organic molecules. Tap each part.
1 · THREE MEMBRANESOuter, inner, and thylakoidLike a mitochondrion, a chloroplast has an outer and an inner membrane. Inside it there's a third: the thylakoid membrane, folded into flat sacs. That third one is where light is captured.
2 · THYLAKOIDS AND GRANAFlat sacs, stacked like pancakesA thylakoid is one flattened sac. A granum is a stack of thylakoids (plural: grana). Stacking packs a huge area of light-catching membrane into a small space.
3 · STROMAThe fluid around the sacsThe stroma is the liquid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids. The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma. It's the chloroplast's version of the mitochondrial matrix.
4 · LUMENThe inside of each sacThe lumen is the space inside a thylakoid. During the light reactions, H⁺ pile up in the lumen, the way they pile up in the mitochondrion's intermembrane space.
COMMON MIX-UP
Lumen ≠ stroma. Stroma is outside the sacs (Calvin cycle). Lumen is inside the sacs (H⁺ pile up).
MATCH IT TO THE MITOCHONDRION
Intermembrane space ~ thylakoid lumen (H⁺ pile up). Matrix ~ stroma (the cycle runs). Plants have both organelles; we only have mitochondria.
MODULE 2.2 · PART 2

Catching light, making ATP, NADPH and O₂

THE QUESTIONHow does a photon of light end up as ATP and NADPH, and why does it release O₂?

Five steps: catching light, the Z-scheme, the thylakoid membrane (photophosphorylation), cyclic electron transfer, and the three ways to make ATP.

STEP 1 · CATCHING LIGHT

Three fates for an excited electron

Everything in the light reactions starts with one photon and one excited electron.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
FIRST STEP: A PHOTON IS ABSORBEDAn electron is boostedA photon of light is absorbed by a chlorophyll in a photosystem, boosting one of its electrons to a higher energy state (an excited electron). Chlorophylls sit packed together in an antenna around a reaction center.An excited electron has three possible fates. Tap each.
1 · HEAT AND FLUORESCENCEThe electron drops back downThe electron falls back to its lower energy level, and the energy is given off as heat and fluorescence (light). Nothing useful is made.
2 · RESONANCEThe energy hops to a neighborThe energy (not the electron) is transferred to a nearby pigment, then the next, like a bucket brigade, until it reaches the reaction center. This is why the chlorophylls sit so close together.
3 · REDUCTION / OXIDATIONThe electron itself is handed offAt the reaction center, the electron is transferred to a new compound, an electron acceptor. The reaction center is now oxidized (missing an electron) and can't fire again until it's refilled. In photosynthesis, the refill comes from water.REDOX CHECKThe acceptor is reduced; the reaction center is oxidized.
THE ONE THAT MATTERS
Only fate 3 makes anything: an electron leaves the reaction center and starts down a chain. Fates 1 and 2 are how the antenna funnels energy to that point (or loses it).
COMMON MIX-UP
Resonance passes energy, not an electron. Only at the reaction center does an actual electron leave.
STEP 2 · THE LIGHT REACTIONS

The Z-scheme: water → PSII → chain → PSI → NADPH

Light energy moves electrons into a higher energy state. Water's electrons go all the way up to NADPH.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 6
NONCYCLIC ELECTRON FLOW (THE Z-SCHEME)Two boosts, one downhill runThere are two major photosystems, PSII and PSI. Photons excite electrons in both. The electrons get boosted up at PSII, run downhill through an electron transport chain, get boosted up again at PSI, and end on NADP⁺. Drawn as energy, the path makes a Z.Noncyclic electron flow makes ATP and NADPH (and O₂). Step through it.
1 · PHOTOSYSTEM II FIRESP680 hands its electrons to pheophytinPSII is where higher-energy light is absorbed. Excited chlorophyll electrons in P680, PSII's reaction center, are passed to pheophytin, the primary electron acceptor. P680 is now missing electrons.
2 · WATER REFILLS ITChapman's favorite reaction: 2 H₂O → 4 H⁺ + O₂The electrons lost from PSII's chlorophyll are replaced by electrons from water, and oxygen is released. All of the O₂ on the planet comes from photosynthesis.REDOX CHECKWater is oxidized (it gives up 4 e⁻); P680 is reduced back.
3 · DOWN THE CHAIN: ATPPQ → cytochrome complex → PCElectrons pass down an electron transport chain to PSI: PQ (plastoquinone), the cytochrome complex and PC (plastocyanin). Protons are pumped across the thylakoid membrane into the lumen and used to make ATP.Proton-motive force: the same thing as in oxidative phosphorylation, where a proton gradient powers ATP synthase.
4 · PHOTOSYSTEM I FIRESLower-energy light boosts them againPhotons of lower-energy light strike the chlorophyll in PSI. P700 is PSI's reaction center. PSI absorbs light near 700 nm; PSII closer to 680 nm.
5 · FERREDOXIN MAKES NADPHFd → NADP⁺ → NADPHExcited electrons from P700 pass through a short chain to ferredoxin (Fd), which holds them in a high-energy state. Ferredoxin donates them to NADP⁺ (with H⁺) to make NADPH, off to the Calvin cycle to make glucose.REDOX CHECKNADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH.
WHY TWO PHOTOSYSTEMS?
1) Light at PSII pulls electrons away from water. 2) Light at PSI boosts them even higher, high enough that NADP⁺ can be reduced. Named in the order they were discovered, so PSII goes first.
THE LIGHT REACTIONS MAKE
O₂ (from water), ATP (by a proton gradient), and NADPH (a high-energy electron carrier). That's the clicker answer: O₂, ATP and NADPH.
STEP 3 · ON THE THYLAKOID MEMBRANE

Photophosphorylation: the H⁺ gradient makes ATP

Where the Z-scheme actually sits, and how its ATP gets made.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
PHOTOPHOSPHORYLATIONHow is ATP made during the light reactions?The same trick as oxidative phosphorylation: electron flow builds an H⁺ gradient across a membrane, and H⁺ flowing back powers ATP synthase. Here the membrane is the thylakoid membrane: low H⁺ in the stroma, high H⁺ in the lumen.
1 · THE ELECTRONSWater → PSII → PQ → cytochrome complex → PC → PSI → Fd → NADPHThe Z-scheme's carriers, now placed in the membrane. Water is split on the lumen side; NADPH is made on the stroma side, where the Calvin cycle will use it.
2 · H⁺ PILE UP IN THE LUMENA proton gradientAs electrons pass through, H⁺ is pumped across the thylakoid membrane into the lumen, and splitting water releases more H⁺ there. The lumen ends up with a high H⁺ concentration; the stroma, low.
3 · H⁺ FLOWS BACK THROUGH ATP SYNTHASEThe proton-motive force makes ATPH⁺ diffuses back down its concentration gradient, from the lumen into the stroma, through ATP synthase, which uses that energy to make ATP from ADP + Pᵢ. That's photophosphorylation.
SAME TRICK AS THE MITOCHONDRION
Both use energy from electrons to pump H⁺ across a membrane; H⁺ diffusing back down its gradient powers ATP synthase. The ATP synthase and some electron carriers are very similar.
WHICH SIDE?
ATP and NADPH are made on the stroma side, right where the Calvin cycle needs them. H⁺ piles up in the lumen.
STEP 4 · CYCLIC VS. NONCYCLIC

Cyclic electron transfer: ATP only

Chapman's slide: electrons in Photosystem I can cycle around to produce ATP, with no NADPH and no O₂.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 3
THE FERREDOXIN FORKTwo ways out of Photosystem IFerredoxin can hand its high-energy electrons to NADP⁺ (making NADPH), or back to PQ. Which way they go decides what the light reactions make.
1 · NONCYCLIC FLOWOn to NADP⁺: the Z-schemeElectrons go one way only: water → PSII → chain → PSI → ferredoxin → NADPH. It makes ATP and NADPH, and because water is split at PSII, O₂.
2 · CYCLIC ELECTRON TRANSFERBack round to Photosystem I: ATP onlyElectrons in Photosystem I cycle around: ferredoxin → PQ → cytochrome complex → PC → back to P700. The cytochrome complex still pumps H⁺, so ATP is made.But no NADPH (the electrons never reach NADP⁺) and no O₂ (PSII isn't used, so no water is split).
SIDE BY SIDE
Noncyclic (Z-scheme): PSII + PSI · water split · makes ATP, NADPH, O₂.
Cyclic: PSI only · no water split · makes ATP only.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
Why would a cell run cyclic flow? The Calvin cycle spends more ATP than NADPH (for each CO₂ fixed: 3 ATP, 2 NADPH). Cyclic flow tops up the extra ATP.
STEP 5 · THREE WAYS TO MAKE ATP

Substrate-level, oxidative, and photophosphorylation

Every ATP in a cell is made one of three ways. Two of them use an H⁺ gradient.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
CHLOROPLASTS AND MITOCHONDRIAElectron transport and chemiosmosis, twiceChloroplasts and mitochondria both use electron transport and an H⁺ gradient to make ATP. Plants do both. We only do oxidative phosphorylation.
1 · BOTH PUMP H⁺Energy from electrons moves H⁺ across a membraneIn both, energy from electrons pumps H⁺ across the membrane: into the intermembrane space (mitochondrion) or into the thylakoid lumen (chloroplast). The difference is where the electrons come from: food (NADH, FADH₂) or water, boosted by light.
2 · BOTH RUN ATP SYNTHASEH⁺ diffusing down its gradient powers itH⁺ flowing back down its concentration gradient powers ATP synthase. The ATP synthase and some electron carriers are very similar in the two organelles.
3 · THE MATCHING SPACESMatrix ~ stroma, intermembrane space ~ lumenThe low-H⁺ side where the cycle runs: matrix ~ stroma. The high-H⁺ side: intermembrane space ~ thylakoid lumen. Oxidative phosphorylation ~ photophosphorylation.
1 · SUBSTRATE-LEVEL PHOSPHORYLATION
An enzyme moves a phosphate from a substrate straight onto ADP. In glycolysis and the citric acid cycle.
2 · OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION
Electron transport (food's electrons → O₂) builds an H⁺ gradient; ATP synthase makes ATP. In mitochondria.
3 · PHOTOPHOSPHORYLATION
Electron transport (water's electrons, boosted by light) builds an H⁺ gradient; ATP synthase makes ATP. In chloroplasts.
MODULE 2.2 · PART 3

Building sugar out of thin air

THE QUESTIONHow do ATP and NADPH turn CO₂ into sugar, and what does the plant build with it?

Five steps: the Calvin cycle counted in carbons, Rubisco, what G3P becomes, the whole story with what goes in and out, and how it ties into respiration.

STEP 1 · THE SYNTHESIS HALF

The Calvin cycle: fix, reduce, regenerate

Three phases, one product: G3P, made from CO₂ with the light reactions' ATP and NADPH.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
THE CALVIN CYCLEConverting CO₂ into carbohydrateThe Calvin cycle is where CO₂ is fixed, in the chloroplast stroma. The potential energy captured in the light reactions (ATP and NADPH) is spent to build carbohydrate. Count the carbons: the red ones came from CO₂.
1 · CARBON FIXATION (CARBOXYLATION)Rubisco adds CO₂ to RuBPRubisco adds CO₂ to a 5-carbon sugar, RuBP. The 6-carbon product is unstable and splits into two 3-carbon molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Three turns: 3 CO₂ + 3 RuBP (15 C) → 6 × 3-PGA (18 C).
2 · REDUCTION3-PGA → G3P, paid for by ATP and NADPHElectrons (and energy) from ATP and NADPH turn 3-PGA into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), a 3-carbon sugar. ATP becomes ADP + Pᵢ; NADPH becomes NADP⁺. These go back to the light reactions.REDOX CHECK3-PGA is reduced to G3P; NADPH is oxidized to NADP⁺.
3 · ONE G3P LEAVESFor every three CO₂ fixed, one G3POf every six G3P, one leaves the cycle. That's the cycle's real product, a carbohydrate the cell uses to build glucose and everything else. Its three carbons are the three that came in as CO₂.
4 · REGENERATIONFive G3P → three RuBP, using ATPThe other five G3P (15 carbons) are rearranged back into three RuBP (15 carbons), which takes ATP. Now Rubisco has CO₂ acceptors again and the cycle can turn.
THE THREE PHASES
1 Carbon fixation: Rubisco adds CO₂ to RuBP (5 C) → 3-PGA.
2 Reduction: ATP + NADPH → G3P.
3 Regeneration: G3P → RuBP, using ATP.
IN AND OUT
In: CO₂, ATP, NADPH. Out: G3P, ADP, NADP⁺. Discussion: CO₂ is combined with RuBP by Rubisco, phosphorylated by ATP and reduced by NADPH.
NO LIGHT?
The Calvin cycle doesn't use light directly, but it stalls without the light reactions' ATP and NADPH. The first phase to stop is reduction.
STEP 2 · THE CARBOXYLATION ENZYME

Rubisco: the clumsy MVP

The enzyme that fixes almost all the planet's carbon can't always tell CO₂ from O₂.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 3
1 · THE CARBOXYLATION ENZYMERubisco fixes carbonRubisco (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) does carbon fixation: it adds CO₂ to RuBP. It's the most abundant enzyme on the planet, and it does the vast majority of carbon fixation in every ecosystem.
2 · ITS FLAW: IT ALSO TAKES O₂PhotorespirationRubisco can also use O₂ as a substrate instead of CO₂. That's photorespiration: no carbon is fixed, and it uses up more of the energy made in the light reactions, lowering the sugar yield.Why so clumsy? Rubisco evolved before O₂ was common in the air, when it didn't have to tell them apart. Evolution worked around the problem rather than fixing the enzyme.
3 · A FIX: CARBOXYSOMESMake Rubisco see mostly CO₂Photosynthetic bacteria (about 35% of the planet's photosynthesis) pack Rubisco into carboxysomes: protein shells loaded with CO₂ that exclude O₂, so photorespiration is minimized. A carboxysome isn't an organelle (no membrane), but its job matches the chloroplast stroma.Other organisms separate carbon fixation from the rest of the cycle in space (a different cell or compartment) or in time (light reactions by day, the cycle at night).
CARBON FIXATION
Joining an inorganic form of an element to an organic molecule is fixation. Carbon fixation happens when Rubisco joins CO₂ to a sugar. (Nitrogen fixation is another, done by bacteria.)
A BIG "CRAZY" EXPERIMENT
From the discussion interview: put bacterial carboxysomes into crop plants to improve their yield, so plants grow faster and feed more people.
STEP 3 · BEYOND THE CALVIN CYCLE

How is G3P used?

G3P is the building block for the carbon molecules in the cell.

Everything a plant is made of starts here: its sugars and the cellulose of its cell walls, the starch it stores, the sucrose it ships to its roots and fruit, and, with nitrogen from the soil, its amino acids and nucleotides.

That's the answer to the big question: the plant's growing mass is G3P, built from CO₂ in the air, rearranged.

CARBO-LOADING
Marathon runners eat lots of carbohydrate before a race because the bonds in carbohydrates have high potential energy (C–H bonds, reduced carbon), stored there by photosynthesis.
SAME G3P AS GLYCOLYSIS
G3P is the same 3-carbon sugar you met in glycolysis's payoff phase: here the cell builds it from CO₂; there it takes it apart.

STEP 5 · THE BIG PICTURE

Tying all of metabolism together

Back to the hydroponic plant: its glucose was built from CO₂ in the air, its O₂ came from water, and its own mitochondria burn that glucose to live.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
TYING ALL OF METABOLISM TOGETHEREach one's outputs are the other's inputsPhotosynthesis is the process that makes high-energy molecules like glucose from the waste of our metabolism, CO₂ and H₂O. Plants do photosynthesis so they can do the same metabolism we do: break sugar down for energy.
1 · PHOTOSYNTHESIS FEEDS RESPIRATIONGlucose and O₂Products of photosynthesis: O₂ and carbohydrate. Both are needed by animals (and by the plant's own mitochondria) to live.
2 · RESPIRATION FEEDS PHOTOSYNTHESISCO₂ and H₂OProducts of respiration: CO₂ and water. Both are needed by plants to make more sugar.
3 · ENERGY FLOWS ONE WAYLight in, heat outThe atoms go round and round, but the energy doesn't: it comes in as light, is stored in sugar, is cashed in as ATP for the cell's work, and leaves as heat. It's never gone, just no longer useful.
PLANTS DO BOTH
Plants have chloroplasts and mitochondria. They make sugar by photosynthesis and break it down by respiration, just like us. We can only do the second half.
PHOTO VS. RESPIRATION, AT A GLANCE
Photosynthesis: CO₂ + H₂O + light → sugar + O₂ (CO₂ reduced, H₂O oxidized).
Respiration: sugar + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + ATP (sugar oxidized, O₂ reduced).

Notes

The whole module on one page, in lecture order.

01

The big idea

Photosynthesis builds high-energy molecules (glucose) from CO₂ and H₂O, using light: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂. It's basically respiration in reverse. Water is oxidized (O: −2 → 0, giving O₂); CO₂ is reduced (C: +4 → 0, giving sugar). All the carbon in living things came from CO₂ ("all biomass comes from air"); all the O₂ came from splitting water.

02

Where

Plants and algae: chloroplasts (40–50 per photosynthesizing cell). Some bacteria: plasma membrane and cytosol. Chloroplast: outer and inner membrane; thylakoids (flat sacs) stacked into grana; stroma (fluid, Calvin cycle); lumen (inside the sacs, H⁺ pile up). Light reactions: thylakoid membranes.

03

Catching light

A photon boosts a chlorophyll electron (excited). Three fates: drop back (heat + fluorescence); energy passed to a neighbor (resonance); or, at the reaction center, the electron is transferred to an acceptor (redox). The reaction center, now oxidized, is refilled from water.

04

The Z-scheme (noncyclic flow)

PSII (P680) → pheophytin; water refills PSII: 2 H₂O → 4 H⁺ + O₂. Electrons run down PQ → cytochrome complex → PC, pumping H⁺ into the lumen (ATP by proton-motive force). PSI (P700, ~700 nm) boosts them again → ferredoxin → NADP⁺ + H⁺ → NADPH. Products: O₂, ATP, NADPH.

05

Cyclic electron transfer

Ferredoxin can hand electrons back to PQ instead of NADP⁺. They cycle through the cytochrome complex and PC back to P700: ATP only (no NADPH, no O₂; PSII not used).

06

Three ways to make ATP

Substrate-level (glycolysis, citric acid cycle). Oxidative phosphorylation (mitochondria). Photophosphorylation (chloroplasts). The last two: electron energy pumps H⁺ across a membrane; H⁺ flowing back through ATP synthase makes ATP. Intermembrane space ~ lumen; matrix ~ stroma. Plants do both; we only do oxidative phosphorylation.

07

The Calvin cycle (stroma)

1 Carbon fixation: Rubisco adds CO₂ to RuBP (5 C) → 3-PGA. 2 Reduction: ATP + NADPH → G3P. 3 Regeneration: G3P → RuBP, using ATP. For every three CO₂ fixed, one G3P leaves. In: CO₂, ATP, NADPH. Out: G3P, ADP, NADP⁺. Oxygen is not needed. Without light, reduction stops first.

08

Rubisco

The carboxylation enzyme; the most abundant enzyme on the planet. It can also use O₂ instead of CO₂ (photorespiration), which wastes light-reaction energy and lowers sugar yield. It evolved before O₂ was common. Carboxysomes (protein shells in photosynthetic bacteria) pack Rubisco with CO₂ and keep O₂ out.

09

What G3P becomes

G3P is the building block for the cell's carbon molecules: glucose and carbohydrates (sugars, cellulose, starch), sucrose (exported), lipids (via acetyl-CoA), amino acids (with NH₃) and proteins, nucleotides and nucleic acids.
