MODULE 3.2 · PART 1

Reading DNA, one base at a time

THE QUESTIONHow do you read the order of bases in a piece of DNA, and then in a whole genome?

The chain terminator, Sanger sequencing step by step, reading the colors, then whole genomes: the shotgun plan, Illumina, assembly, and how they all fit together.

SANGER SEQUENCING · THE TRICK

Dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs): the chain terminator

To read DNA, Sanger sequencing copies it and stops the copy at every possible spot. The stop button is a nucleotide missing one small group. It works because of one rule of DNA synthesis from Module 2.3: a new nucleotide can only attach to the 3′ OH (hydroxyl) of the one before it.1

CLICKER QUESTIONDuring DNA synthesis, DNA polymerase attaches the incoming nucleotide to the:
  1. 2′ hydroxyl on the previous nucleotide
  2. 5′ phosphate on the previous nucleotide
  3. 3′ phosphate on the previous nucleotide
  4. 3′ hydroxyl group on the previous nucleotide
  5. 5′ hydroxyl group on the previous nucleotide
Show the answer
D. The 3′ hydroxyl. The new nucleotide's phosphate bonds to the 3′ OH at the end of the growing strand. That's why strands grow 5′ → 3′, and why a nucleotide with no 3′ OH ends the strand.
KEY TERM · DIDEOXYNUCLEOTIDE (ddNTP)
"Di-deoxy" = two oxygens missing. Every DNA nucleotide already lacks the 2′ OH (that's the "deoxy" in DNA). A dideoxynucleotide also lacks the 3′ OH. It can be added to a strand, but nothing can be added after it, so it is a chain terminator. In sequencing, each of the four (ddATP, ddTTP, ddGTP, ddCTP) carries its own fluorescent dye.
COMMON MIX-UP
A ddNTP does not stop the polymerase from adding it. It gets built in normally. It's the next nucleotide that has nowhere to attach.
SANGER SEQUENCING · THE REACTION

Sanger sequencing, step by step

Sanger sequencing borrows the rules of DNA replication, like PCR in Module 3.1, but copies the template only once, with a few chain terminators mixed in.1,2

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 6
THE MIXWhat goes in the tubeThe template (the strand you want to read), one primer, DNA polymerase, lots of normal dNTPs, and a few of each of the four ddNTPs, each with its own fluorescent dye. Drawn here like the book: strands are ribbons of letters.
ANNEALHeat and cool onceJust like in PCR, the mix is heated and cooled so the primer pairs with the template. But only once (PCR repeats it 25 to 40 times). Here the primer, ACTAC, pairs with TGATG. Then DNA polymerase is added and a single round of DNA synthesis takes place.
C OR C?The polymerase adds the next baseDNA polymerase sits at the primer's 3′ end. The next template base is G, so the next base must be a C. The tube holds two kinds: lots of normal C (dCTP) and a few ddC (ddCTP, purple dye). Whichever bumps in first gets added.
TWO COPIESOne keeps going, one stopsCopy 1 got a normal C, which has a 3′ OH, so it keeps growing: G, A, G, T. Copy 2 got a ddC, which has no 3′ OH, so it stops for good.The same choice happens at every spot, on millions of copies at once.
EVERY LENGTHA strand ending at every spotThe result: strands that stop after C, after G, after A, after G, after T. Each ends in the dye of its last base. The color says which base; the length says where it is.
THE JUMBLEWhat the tube really holdsAll the strands are mixed together, and a machine can't read their letters. It can only "see" the dye on each end. Sorting them by length turns that into the sequence (next section).

THINK ABOUT IT
Why only a little ddNTP? With lots of it, nearly every strand would stop at the first few spots, and you'd never read far. With a little, a few strands stop at each spot and the rest keep going: about 1,000 bases per reaction.
SANGER SEQUENCING · READING THE RESULT

Reading the result: gel, detector, trace

The tube holds a jumble of strands, each ending in a dye. Sorting them by length turns the jumble into the sequence. This is the book's Fig. 12.8c, step by step, with the same five strands.1,2

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
LOADThe jumble goes into a gelEach strand is drawn as a bar: pale yellow for the primer part, light blue for the bases the polymerase added, and its dye at the end. They go in at the top (−) of a gel, a mesh, the same idea as gel electrophoresis in Module 3.1.
SORT BY SIZEShort strands slip through fastestDNA is negative, so the + end at the bottom pulls the strands down. Short strands wiggle through the mesh quickly; long ones are held back. They spread out by length: shortest at the bottom.
DETECTORWhat reads the colorsAt the bottom, a laser crosses the gel. As each strand comes out, the laser makes its dye glow, and the detector, a light sensor, records the color as a peak. The shortest comes out first, so its peak is first. The graph is the trace.
READ ITPeak → letter → templateEach peak gives one letter of the new strand, in order: C, G, A, G, T. Pair each one (A with T, G with C) to get the template: G, C, T, C, A. Put the known primer in front: new strand 5′ ACTAC CGAGT 3′, template 3′ TGATG GCTCA 5′.
KEY TERM · DETECTOR
A light sensor at the bottom of the gel. A laser makes each strand's dye glow as the strand comes out, and the detector "reads" the color. Its record, one peak per strand, is the trace.
CLICKER QUESTIONTo sequence a strand of DNA, all four dNTPs, ddGTP (blue), DNA polymerase and primers are added to a tube. The template with its primer is 3′ TGACTCTAGCCTAGGACTATATCG 5′, primer 5′ ACTGA. How many BLUE fragments of different lengths do you get?
  1. 1
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 5
  5. 6
Show the answer
D. 5. A blue fragment ends wherever a G goes into the new strand, which is across from every C in the template. After the primer's spot (TGACT), the template has C at 5 places: CTAGCCTAGGACTATATCG. Five Cs, five blue lengths. The G inside the primer doesn't count: the primer was made in a lab, not by the polymerase.
COMMON MIX-UP
The colors give you the new strand, read 5′ → 3′, not the template. To get the template, pair every base and flip the direction. And start from the shortest strand: the one nearest the + end.
COMMON MIX-UP
Colors are only a key. These pages use one key (A green, C purple, T red, G blue); the Illumina slides and exam questions may use another. Always read the key you're given.
WHOLE GENOMES · SHOTGUN SEQUENCING

Shotgun sequencing: the plan for a whole genome

A genome is all the genetic material of an organism. Sanger reads one short stretch at a time, so a whole genome needs a plan. The next two sections are steps 2 and 3 of that plan.1,2

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
TOO LONG FOR ONE READ3 million reactions?"Too big" means too long: a genome is one very long string of letters. One Sanger reaction reads about 1,000 of them. The human genome has about 3 billion. 3,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = 3 million reactions, one after another. Too slow.
THE PLANShotgun sequencing: cut, read, assembleShotgun sequencing is not a machine. It's a plan with three steps: cut many copies of the genome into pieces, read every piece with a reading machine, then assemble the pieces on a computer. The first genomes were read with Sanger machines; today step 2 is usually Illumina.
STEP 1 · CUTBreak the genome into small piecesMany copies of the genome are cut into small pieces. Each copy breaks in different places, so the pieces overlap. Those overlaps are what make step 3 possible.
STEP 2 · READRead every pieceEvery piece is sequenced. Now you know each piece's letters, but not where it came from. With millions of pieces, they have to be read at the same time: that's what Illumina does (next section).
WHY "SHOTGUN"
The genome is blasted into random pieces, like a shotgun spreading pellets. Nobody knows where each piece came from until the overlaps are matched. Think of shredding several copies of the same book, reading every scrap, then taping the scraps back together where their words overlap (the book uses a sentence this way in Fig. 12.9).
SHOTGUN STEP 2 · READ THE PIECES WITH ILLUMINA

Illumina sequencing: millions of pieces at once

Step 1 made millions of pieces. Illumina reads them all at the same time on one glass slide, one base per cycle, with a photo after each base.2,3

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 7
FLOW CELLMillions of pieces on one glass slideThe pieces from step 1 are stuck to a glass slide called a flow cell; liquids flow through its lanes. Zoomed in, it's covered in spots. Each spot is one DNA piece (really a tight cluster of copies of it, so it's bright enough to photograph).
ZOOM INThree spots, from the side and from aboveOn the left, three spots seen from the side: each is a template strand standing on the glass with a primer at its base. On the right, the camera's view from above: the same spots as dark dots.
ADD ONEReversibly terminating nucleotidesAll four nucleotides flow in, each with its own color and a chemical STOP block (the purple star). Each spot's polymerase adds the matching one, and the block stops anything else from joining. One base per spot. The extras are washed away.
PHOTO 1Laser on, take a pictureA laser lights the slide and the camera photographs it from above. Each spot glows the color of the base it just added.
UNBLOCKRemove the block (and the dye)The STOP block is removed chemically, and the dye comes off too, so the next photo shows only the next base. Each strand can now take exactly one more.
CYCLE 2The same moves againAdd one, photo, unblock. One base per round; each round is one cycle.
READFollow one spot through the photosAfter many cycles, follow spot 1 through the photos: purple, green, blue, red = C, A, G, T. Every spot is read this way at the same time, giving millions of short reads. Those go to step 3.
SHOTGUN STEP 3 · SEQUENCE ASSEMBLY

Sequence assembly: put the reads back in order

The last step of the shotgun plan. It's not a way of sequencing: no new letters are read. A computer takes the reads from step 2 and lines them up by their overlaps.1,2

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 7
THE PILEReads with no addressStep 2 gave a pile of reads. We know every read's letters but not where it came from. These are the same pieces as in the shotgun section.
STARTPick any pieceStart with GTTACGT and put it down.
MATCH 1End of one = start of anotherGTTACGT ends in CGT. The piece CGTATAG starts with CGT, so it slides in under it, overlapping by 3 letters.
MATCH 2Keep goingCGTATAG ends in ATAG, and ATAGACCT starts with it.
MATCH 3And againATAGACCT ends in ACCT, and ACCTAGCT starts with it.
MATCH 4The last linkACCTAGCT ends in AGCT, and AGCTAGCCC starts with it.
MERGEDOne continuous sequenceWrite each overlap once and you get the whole stretch. Two short pieces (GTTA, AGCCC) add nothing new: their letters already sit inside longer pieces. A real genome has millions of reads, so a computer does this matching (it takes heavy computing).
PRACTICE QUESTIONYou discover a new bacterium and sequence its genome by shotgun sequencing. Among the sequences you get these four fragments: 1. TAGCTTACGGT   2. ATACGTCCGTT   3. ATGCATTAG   4. CGGTACGTATA. What is their order in the genome?

Look for the end of one fragment that matches the start of another.

Show the answer
3, 1, 4, 2. 3 ends in TAG, which starts 1. Fragment 1 ends in CGGT, which starts 4. Fragment 4 ends in ATA, which starts 2. Joined: ATGCATTAGCTTACGGTACGTATACGTCCGTT.
FAMOUS EXPERIMENT · BEYOND THE COURSE
Many scientists thought you had to know where each fragment came from before sequencing it. A team at Celera Genomics bet that a powerful enough computer could assemble random fragments by their overlaps alone. It worked: they sequenced the fruit fly genome this way (Adams and colleagues, Science, 2000)4 and announced 90% of the human genome the same year.
THINK ABOUT IT
Repeats make assembly hard. If the same stretch shows up in hundreds of places (like the word "Hamlet" in the play), a read from inside it could come from any of them. Only reads that run into the unique DNA next to a repeat can be placed.
KEEP THEM STRAIGHT

Sanger, Illumina and shotgun: how they fit together

Three names, three different jobs. First the map, then Sanger and Illumina side by side, one difference at a time.1,2,3

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 6
THREE NAMES, THREE JOBSWhich is whichSanger and Illumina are two ways to read DNA. Shotgun is the plan for a whole genome: cut, read (with Sanger or Illumina), assemble. Assembly is the plan's last step, done by a computer; it's not a way to read DNA, and it isn't part of Illumina itself (Illumina just hands it the reads).
THE STOPPermanent vs. removableSanger: a ddNTP has no 3′ OH, so its strand stops for good. Illumina: the STOP block is removable, so the same strand pauses for a photo and then keeps growing.
THE STRANDSEvery length vs. all the same lengthSanger: one tube ends up with strands of every length, all made at once. Illumina: every strand grows in step, one base per cycle, so they're always the same length.
READINGGel + detector vs. a camera every cycleSanger: the strands are sorted by size in a gel, and a detector reads each color as the strand comes out. Illumina: nothing is sorted. A camera photographs the whole flow cell after every base.
HOW MANYOne vs. millionsSanger: one piece per reaction, about 1,000 bases. Illumina: millions of pieces on one flow cell, read at the same time. That's why whole genomes use Illumina.
SAME IN BOTHWhat they shareBoth use DNA polymerase building the new strand 5′ → 3′ from a primer, colored nucleotides, and modified nucleotides that stop the chain. Neither needs helicase.

CLICKER QUESTIONWhich of the following is a difference between dideoxy sequencing and Illumina sequencing?
  1. Separation of DNA synthesis products based on size to determine DNA sequence
  2. Use of nucleotides with different colors
  3. Inclusion of chain terminating nucleotides in the DNA sequencing reaction
  4. Use of DNA polymerase to synthesize products
  5. Requirement for DNA helicase
Show the answer
A. Separating products by size. Only Sanger sorts strands by length. Both use colored nucleotides, both use nucleotides that stop the chain (permanent in Sanger, removable in Illumina), both use DNA polymerase, and neither uses helicase.
CLICKER QUESTIONOne similarity between Sanger sequencing and Illumina sequencing is:
  1. DNA synthesis in both processes proceeds from the 3′ to 5′ direction
  2. dNTPs are modified at the 5′ position to terminate synthesis
  3. dNTPs are not needed in either process
  4. Modified nucleotides can terminate DNA synthesis during the sequencing reaction
Show the answer
D. Modified nucleotides can stop synthesis. Sanger's ddNTP and Illumina's blocked nucleotide are both modified at the 3′ end (not the 5′), and both stop the chain. Synthesis runs 5′ → 3′ in both, and both need nucleotides.
COMMON MIX-UP
Both methods use a nucleotide that stops the chain. Don't pick "chain terminators" as the difference. The difference is that a ddNTP stops it forever, while Illumina's block is removable.
LOCK IT IN · PART 1
1. Dideoxynucleotides are chain terminators. 2. DNA sequencing finds the order of nucleotides in a stretch of DNA. 3. Entire genomes can be sequenced by shotgun sequencing: cut, sequence, assemble. 4. Illumina sequencing reads millions of DNA fragments at the same time. 5. Those short reads must be assembled to see the genome's continuous sequence.

MODULE 3.2 · PART 2

What a genome is, and how it's packed

THE QUESTIONWhat is a genome, and how does so much DNA fit inside a cell?

How genomes are packed (prokaryotes vs. eukaryotes), why gene number and genome size don't match complexity (polyploidy, noncoding DNA, jumping genes, repeats), how genes are found (annotation, reading frames, ORFs), and what makes us different from chimps.

GENES AND GENOMES · GENOME ORGANIZATION

Genome organization: too much DNA, too little space

Sequencing gave us whole genomes. So what is a genome, and how does that much DNA fit in a cell?

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 3
BACTERIA1,500 µm of DNA in a 4 µm cellAn E. coli genome is one circle of double-stranded DNA, 4.6 million base pairs. Stretched out, it would be 1.5 mm (1,500 µm) long, about 375 times the length of the cell.
HUMAN CELL2 m of DNA in a 10 µm nucleusThe human genome is about 3 billion base pairs, on several linear chromosomes, each one double-stranded DNA molecule. The DNA in one cell, stretched out, is about 2 m. The nucleus it fits in is about 10 µm across.
TWO WAYSSo it has to be packedProkaryotes (bacteria and archaea) pack their DNA as a nucleoid; eukaryotes pack theirs as chromatin. The next two sections build each one up, step by step.
KEY TERMS · GENE AND GENOME
A gene is a stretch of DNA that is transcribed into an RNA; most genes code for proteins, and some RNAs do their job without being translated (see Module 2.4). A genome is all the genetic material of an organism: every gene, plus all the DNA between them.
GENOME ORGANIZATION · PROKARYOTES

Prokaryotes: the nucleoid (supercoiled loops)

Prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) have no nucleus. The slides and the book use the bacterium E. coli as the example: it packs one circle of DNA by twisting it and pinning the twists into loops.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
ONE CIRCLEBacteria have one circular chromosomeThe E. coli genome is one circular, double-stranded DNA molecule, 4.6 million base pairs.
SUPERCOILTwist it until it coilsAn enzyme, topoisomerase II, cuts the double helix, unwinds it a little, and seals it again. That strain makes the DNA coil on itself: a supercoil. Twist a rubber band and it does the same thing.
NUCLEOIDLoops on a protein coreProteins hold the supercoiled DNA in loops around a core. The whole structure is the nucleoid. E. coli has about 100 loops, each about 50,000 base pairs.
IN THE CELLNo nucleus neededBacteria have no nucleus. The nucleoid sits right in the cytoplasm, packed small enough to fit in a cell about 4 µm long.
PROKARYOTES = BACTERIA + ARCHAEA
Both groups have no nucleus and keep their DNA in a nucleoid, usually one circular chromosome. The example here is a bacterium. Going deeper (beyond the course): many archaea wrap their DNA on proteins similar to histones, so archaea sit partway between the two pictures.
COMMON MIX-UP
A nucleoid is not a nucleus. There's no membrane around it: it's just the bacterial DNA, folded into loops, sitting in the cytoplasm.
GENOME ORGANIZATION · EUKARYOTES

Eukaryotes: chromatin, DNA wound on histones

Now the switch: eukaryotes (plants, animals, fungi, protists) keep their DNA inside a nucleus. Eukaryotes pack each long, linear DNA molecule with proteins. DNA plus those proteins is chromatin. Follow one stretch of DNA from the double helix up.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 6
DNAThe double helix: 2 nm wideEach eukaryotic chromosome is one long, linear DNA molecule. Its sugar-phosphate backbone is negative, because of the phosphates.
HISTONESEight positive proteinsHistones are small proteins, rich in the positive amino acids lysine and arginine. Eight of them, two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, form a spool. Opposite charges attract, so histones bind any DNA, whatever its sequence. They are nearly the same in every eukaryote (highly conserved).
NUCLEOSOMEDNA wraps twiceAbout 150 base pairs of DNA wrap twice around the eight histones. That bead is a nucleosome.
10 NMBeads on a stringNucleosomes linked by short stretches of DNA look like beads on a string: the 10-nm fiber. This is the loosest packing, and genes here can be read (transcribed).
30 NMThe beads coil upThe string of nucleosomes coils into a thicker fiber: the 30-nm fiber.
DIVIDINGFully condensed, only for divisionWhen a cell gets ready to divide, the 30-nm fiber keeps coiling: loops on a protein scaffold, coils of coils, and finally a condensed chromosome about 1400 nm wide. That's the chromosome you see in mitosis (Ecology & Evolution Module 3.1).

KEY TERM · NUCLEOSOME
Eight histones (two each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4) with about 150 base pairs of DNA wrapped twice around them. Draw it as a pink spool with DNA wrapped twice around it.
COMMON MIX-UP
Chromatin is the material: DNA plus its proteins. A chromosome is one DNA molecule with its proteins. Every chromosome is made of chromatin; it only looks like the classic X when it's fully condensed for division.
THINK ABOUT IT
The book says loosely packed chromatin (the 10-nm fiber) is where genes can be switched on and read. So how tightly DNA is packed is one way a cell can control its genes: that's where gene regulation starts.
GENES AND GENOMES · TWO PARADOXES

G-value vs. C-value: two different surprises

Now that prokaryote and eukaryote genomes are side by side, compare genomes by two numbers. Both fail to predict complexity, for different reasons.1,2

COMMON MIX-UP
G-value = the number of genes. C-value = the total amount of DNA (genome size; the book defines it as the DNA in a reproductive cell). A genome can have few genes but lots of DNA, so the two numbers don't move together.
TWO PARADOXES · G-VALUE

The G-value paradox: gene number vs. complexity

Count the protein-coding genes in each organism. A small mustard plant beats you; a worm comes close.1,2,3

SLIDE VS. BOOK
These counts are the slide's. The book's newer table gives the nematode worm about 22,000 genes and the fruit fly about 17,000. The point is the same either way: humans don't top the list.
THINK ABOUT IT
A worm has 959 cells, 302 of them nerve cells; you have about 100 trillion. Yet your gene counts are close (book 12.5). Complexity comes from how genes are used, not how many there are.
TWO PARADOXES · C-VALUE

The C-value paradox: genome size vs. complexity

Now measure all the DNA instead of counting genes. The same organisms line up very differently.1,2,3

THINK ABOUT IT
Each tick on that scale is 1,000 times the last. The lungfish's genome is more than 45 times yours; an amoeba's is over 200 times (book Table 12.3). Two reasons, next: polyploidy, then noncoding DNA.
C-VALUE · REASON 1

Polyploidy: extra sets of chromosomes

One reason some genomes are huge: more than two sets of chromosomes. More DNA, but mostly copies of the same genes.1,2

KEY TERM · POLYPLOIDY
Having more than two sets of chromosomes. Especially common in plants: 30% to 80% of flowering plant species have polyploidy in their history (book 12.5). Many crops are polyploid: wheat, potatoes, olives, bananas, sugarcane and coffee.
C-VALUE · REASON 2

Genes vs. genomes: most DNA codes for no protein

The main reason for huge genomes: lots of DNA that doesn't code for protein. Compare what a human and a prokaryotic genome are made of.1,2,3

COMMON MIX-UP
"Noncoding" doesn't mean useless. It includes introns, promoters and other control sequences, and repeats. The 2.5% is only the part that spells out proteins.
C-VALUE · WHERE ALL THAT DNA COMES FROM

Transposable elements: jumping genes

More than half of the human genome is repeats, and most of those are transposable elements that copy themselves around the genome.1,2,3

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 7
TWO KINDSRepetitive sequences in eukaryotesTwo main kinds: transposable elements ("jumping" or "hopping" genes), about 45% of the human genome, and short tandem repeats, about 3%.
THE LINEA gene that copies itselfA LINE is the most common kind of transposable element: a stretch of DNA in a chromosome that carries the instructions for copying itself. Watch where each step happens: nucleus, cytoplasm, then nucleus again.
1 TRANSCRIBEIn the nucleus: DNA → RNAThe LINE is transcribed into RNA, exactly like any gene (see Module 2.4).
2 TRANSLATEIn the cytoplasm: RNA → proteinsThe RNA leaves the nucleus through a pore. A ribosome translates it into two enzymes: reverse transcriptase (copies RNA into DNA) and integrase (cuts DNA and pastes a piece in).
3 BACK INBack to the nucleusThe two enzymes hold on to the LINE's own RNA and carry it back into the nucleus, where the DNA is.
4 COPY BACKIn the nucleus: RNA → DNAReverse transcription: reverse transcriptase uses the RNA as a template to build DNA, the backwards direction (the same enzyme used to make cDNA in Module 3.1). The result is a double-stranded DNA copy of the LINE.
5 PASTE INIn the nucleus: a new spotIntegrase cuts the DNA at a new spot and inserts the copy. The original LINE never left, so now there are two: copy and paste. Over millions of years, copies pile up, which is a big part of why some genomes are so large.
COMMON MIX-UP
Transposable elements are also called transposons, jumping genes or hopping genes. Most don't code for anything the cell needs; they're sometimes called "selfish" DNA because they mostly just copy themselves.
GOING DEEPER · BEYOND THE COURSE
The slides' version (reverse transcriptase + integrase) is what to know for the exam. In real LINEs, the cutting job is done by a part of the same protein as the reverse transcriptase, and the RNA is copied into DNA right at the new spot as it's inserted. "Integrase" is the name for the enzyme that does the pasting in retroviruses like HIV.
TRANSPOSABLE ELEMENTS · THE FAMOUS EXPERIMENT

McClintock's corn: how jumping genes were found

The first jumping genes were spotted, literally, on ears of corn.2,4

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
PURPLEA pigment geneIn corn, a working pigment gene makes a kernel purple.
JUMP INA jumping gene breaks itIf a transposable element jumps into the pigment gene, the gene is broken, and the kernel stays pale.
JUMP OUTSpotsAs the kernel grows, in some cells the element hops back out. The gene works again in that cell, and in all the cells that come from it: a purple spot. A pale kernel covered in purple spots is a record of genes jumping.
McCLINTOCKThe discoveryBarbara McClintock worked this out in the 1940s and 50s by tracking kernel colors across generations, long before DNA could be sequenced. Few believed her at first. She won the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for her discovery of mobile genetic elements."
BEYOND THE COURSE
Your slides give the Nobel Prize; the corn details here come from McClintock's own work4. The element in her kernels moved by cutting and pasting itself, a different route from the LINE copy-and-paste above, but both are transposable elements.
REPETITIVE DNA · SHORT TANDEM REPEATS

Short tandem repeats and DNA fingerprinting

The second kind of repeat is small, and it's what makes your DNA identifiable.1,2

MICRO VS MINITwo sizes of repeat unitMicrosatellites (simple sequence repeats): a unit of 1 to 5 bases, repeated. Minisatellites (variable number tandem repeats, VNTRs): a unit of 6 to 500 bases, repeated.
EVERYONE DIFFERSHypervariableThe number of repeats at each spot varies from person to person, so each person has a unique set of repeat lengths.
FINGERPRINTINGDNA fingerprintingCopy the repeat regions (by PCR) and compare their lengths on a gel, as in Module 3.1. Matching bands mean the DNA could come from the same person: used at crime scenes and in paternity tests.

A sequenced genome is only letters. Annotation finds what each stretch is. Compare the same letters before and after.1,2

KEY TERMS
Genome annotation: identifying sequence features (genes, promoters and more) and where they are in the genome. Bioinformatics: the computational, mathematical and statistical methods it takes. Sequence motif: a telltale pattern, like a promoter or an open reading frame.

READING THE GENOME · FINDING GENES

Reading frames and open reading frames (ORFs)

How does a computer find a protein-coding gene in billions of letters? It looks for open reading frames. Builds on codons from Module 2.4.1,2

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
3 PER STRANDWhere do you start counting?Codons are read in threes, but nothing marks where a group of three starts. Start at letter 1, 2 or 3 and you get three different sets of codons. Starting at letter 4 gives the same codons as letter 1, just one codon later, so a strand has exactly 3 reading frames.
6 IN ALLThe other strand counts tooEach strand is read 5′ → 3′, and the two strands run in opposite directions. So the bottom strand, read right to left, has its own 3 frames. 3 + 3 = 6 reading frames in every stretch of double-stranded DNA.
THE SLIDE'S DNAMost frames hit stops fastThe slide's 64-base stretch in all six frames. Stop codons (red) show up quickly in five of them. Only frame 3 runs a long way: from ATG (Met) through 16 codons to one stop.
THE ORFOpen reading frameAn open reading frame (ORF) = a start codon, then codon after codon, then one stop codon, with no stop in between. Real ones run from a few dozen to several hundred codons. Computer programs scan genomes for ORFs to find possible protein-coding genes.
KEY TERMS
Reading frame: one way of grouping DNA letters into codons; every double-stranded stretch has 6. Open reading frame (ORF): start codon (ATG), codons, one stop, no stop in between.
CLICKER QUESTIONWhich of the following statements is true about open reading frames?
  1. Any stretch of DNA will have six possible open reading frames, three on each DNA strand.
  2. An open reading frame will end in a stop codon, but it will have no additional stop codons in frame.
  3. An open reading frame codes for either proteins or miRNAs or siRNAs.
  4. An open reading frame will contain two, three, or four nucleotides for each amino acid coded.
  5. Open reading frames are hard to find in bacteria due to the presence of introns.
Show the answer
B. An ORF ends in one stop codon, with none in between. DNA has six reading frames, but most aren't open; codons are always three bases; ORFs code for proteins; bacteria have no introns, so their ORFs are easy to find.
COMMON MIX-UP
"Reading frame" and "open reading frame" aren't the same thing. Every stretch of DNA has 6 reading frames. Only a frame that runs from a start to a stop without hitting a stop early is open.
GENOME ANNOTATION · PROKARYOTES VS EUKARYOTES

Finding genes: prokaryotes vs. eukaryotes

An ORF is easy to find when it's in one piece. In eukaryotes it usually isn't.1,2

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 2
PROKARYOTESA promoter and one ORFBacteria and archaea have no introns. A gene is a promoter, with a highly conserved sequence, followed by one unbroken ORF from ATG to stop. Spot the promoter and the ORF, and you've found the gene.
EUKARYOTESIntrons break the ORF upEukaryotic genes are split into exons by introns, so the ORF isn't in one piece in the genome. One fix: make cDNA from the cell's mRNA (with reverse transcriptase, as in Module 3.1) and line it up with the genome. The parts that match are exons; the gaps are introns. The ends of the mRNA that aren't translated are the untranslated regions (UTRs).
THINK ABOUT IT
Introns were first discovered exactly this way: by comparing the mRNA for β-globin (part of hemoglobin) with its gene's DNA (book 12.4).
GENES AND GENOMES · ONE LAST SURPRISE

Humans and chimpanzees: 98.8% the same

If gene number, genome size and even most of the sequence don't explain how different organisms are, what does?2,5

LOCK IT IN · PART 2
1. Open reading frames code for proteins. 2. Comparing DNA with mRNA (cDNA) reveals the intron-exon structure of eukaryotic genes. 3. More complex organisms don't necessarily have more genes or more DNA. 4. The human genome contains many repetitive elements. 5. DNA is packaged to fit the tight space of the cell or nucleus. 6. Expression of genes needs to be regulated.

QUESTION 1 OF 5 · WARM-UPDuring DNA synthesis, DNA polymerase attaches the incoming nucleotide to the:

Correct. Not quite. The incoming nucleotide's phosphate bonds to the 3′ OH at the end of the strand. That's why a ddNTP, with no 3′ OH, stops the chain. Review it in the flow chart.

QUESTION 2 OF 5 · SANGER SEQUENCINGTo sequence a strand of DNA, all four dNTPs, ddGTP (blue), DNA polymerase and primers are added to a tube. The template, with its primer, is 3′ TGACTCTAGCCTAGGACTATATCG 5′, primer 5′ ACTGA. How many BLUE fragments of different lengths do you get?

Correct. Not quite. A blue (G) end goes in across from every C in the template after the primer: CTAGCCTAGGACTATATCG. Five Cs, five blue lengths. The G inside the primer doesn't count: the polymerase didn't add it. Review it in the flow chart.

QUESTION 3 OF 5 · ILLUMINA SEQUENCINGWhich of the following is a difference between dideoxy sequencing and Illumina sequencing?

Correct. Not quite. Only Sanger sorts strands by size. Both use colored nucleotides, both use nucleotides that stop the chain (permanent in Sanger, removable in Illumina), both use DNA polymerase, and neither uses helicase. Review it in the flow chart.

QUESTION 4 OF 5 · SEQUENCE ASSEMBLYYou discover a new bacterium and sequence its genome by shotgun sequencing. You get these four fragments: 1. TAGCTTACGGT  2. ATACGTCCGTT  3. ATGCATTAG  4. CGGTACGTATA. What is their order in the genome?

Correct. Not quite. Match the end of one fragment to the start of another: 3 ends in TAG, which starts 1; 1 ends in CGGT, which starts 4; 4 ends in ATA, which starts 2. Joined: ATGCATTAGCTTACGGTACGTATACGTCCGTT. Review it in the flow chart.

QUESTION 5 OF 5 · GENOMESWhich of the following statements is true about open reading frames?

Correct. Not quite. An ORF runs from a start codon (AUG) through codon after codon to one stop codon, with no stop in between. DNA has six possible reading frames, but not every one is open; codons are always three bases; bacteria have no introns, so their ORFs are easy to spot. Review it in the flow chart.
