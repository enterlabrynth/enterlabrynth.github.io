MODULE 3.3 · PART 1

Mendel's experiments

THE QUESTION Breeders had crossed plants for centuries without finding rules. What did Mendel do differently, and what did he see?

In this part: Mendel's three choices and his seven traits, then the parental, F1 and F2 generations.

START HERE

Why Mendel saw what others missed

Gregor Mendel, a monk in Brno, crossed garden peas from 1856 to 1864. Plant breeders had done hybridization experiments before him, but none found rules. Mendel did, because of three choices.1,2

THE SEVEN TRAITS

Seven pairs of contrasting traits

CROSSING PEASPea flowers usually fertilize themselves. To cross two plants, Mendel opened a flower early and cut off its immature anthers (the male parts), dusted its stigma with pollen from the chosen father plant, and tied a cloth bag over it to keep stray pollen out. Each fertilized ovule became one pea in the pod.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Hybridization

Interbreeding between two different varieties (strains) or species. Mendel's crosses between true-breeding strains are hybridizations, and their offspring are hybrids.

True-breeding

A strain whose offspring look the same as the parents, generation after generation, when bred among themselves: homozygous for the trait.

THE FIRST CROSSES

P, F1, F2: one trait disappears and comes back

Mendel crossed true-breeding strains that differed in one trait. The result in the first generation was always the same: only one of the two traits showed.1,3

Check yourself: Why do we often say recessive traits "skip a generation"?

In the parental and F1 crosses, the recessive trait (green) disappears in the F1, because every F1 is Aa and one A is enough to make the seed yellow. The F1 still carry a, so when they are crossed, a quarter of the F2 are aa and green: the trait "skipped" the F1 and reappeared in the F2.

Check yourself: When two true-breeding parents are crossed, how can you tell which trait is dominant?

Look at the F1. The trait that shows in all the F1 is dominant; the one that disappears is recessive. With two genes, do it for each trait separately: yellow × green gave yellow F1, so yellow is dominant; wrinkled × round gave round F1, so round is dominant.

WHY YELLOW IS DOMINANTYellow peas make an enzyme that breaks down green chlorophyll. The green allele is a mutation that inactivates the enzyme. In an Aa seed, one working copy makes enough enzyme to clear the chlorophyll, so the seed is yellow.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Parental (P) generation

The first generation in a series of crosses: here the true-breeding parents (written P or P₁).

Parental cross

The cross between the two parents of the P generation, for example true-breeding yellow AA × true-breeding green aa. It produces the F1.

F1 generation

The first filial generation: the offspring of the parental cross. When the parents are true-breeding for different traits, all F1 are heterozygous hybrids and show the dominant trait.

F1 cross

A cross between F1 individuals (or an F1 self-fertilized), for example Aa × Aa. It produces the F2.

F2 generation

The second filial generation: the offspring of the F1 cross. The recessive trait reappears in the F2.

F2 cross

A cross between F2 individuals (or an F2 self-fertilized). Mendel self-fertilized F2 plants to tell the true-breeding AA from the Aa.

Dominant

The trait (or allele) that shows in the F1 when two true-breeding strains are crossed; one copy of the dominant allele is enough to give the trait. Written with a capital letter (A).

Recessive

The trait (or allele) that does not show in the F1, and shows only in homozygotes (aa). Written with a lowercase letter (a).

Lock it in · Part 1

  1. Mendel succeeded because he used true-breeding strains, followed one trait at a time, and counted the offspring.
  2. Parental cross (true-breeding AA × aa) → F1, all Aa, all showing the dominant trait.
  3. F1 cross (Aa × Aa) → F2, with the recessive trait back in a 3 : 1 ratio: it "skips a generation".
  4. The trait that shows in the F1 of two true-breeding parents is the dominant one.

MODULE 3.3 · PART 2

One gene: the principle of segregation

THE QUESTION Why does a trait that vanishes in the F1 come back in exactly a quarter of the F2?

In this part: the law of segregation and where it happens in meiosis, Punnett squares, the AND and OR rules, and the testcross.

MENDEL'S FIRST LAW

The principle of segregation

Mendel explained 3 : 1 with a few simple ideas, written here in today's words. The last one is the Law of Segregation, and meiosis is the reason it works.1,2

1Two alleles per geneEach body cell has two alleles of every gene, at the same locus on two homologs. True-breeding plants are homozygous (AA or aa); the F1 are heterozygous (Aa).
2One allele per gameteEach gamete carries only one allele of each gene: A or a, never both.
3They segregate equallyThe two alleles segregate equally into gametes: an Aa plant makes 1/2 A gametes and 1/2 a gametes.
4Random unionA zygote forms from the random union of one gamete from each parent.

Check yourself: Where in meiosis does segregation occur? How does the law of segregation help us predict the alleles in gametes?

In anaphase I, when homologous chromosomes separate. Because the two alleles always go to different gametes in equal numbers, we can predict gametes from a genotype: AA → all A; aa → all a; Aa → 1/2 A and 1/2 a.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Allele

One version of a gene: a variation of the gene's DNA sequence at a particular place on a chromosome. Mendel's "hereditary factors".

Locus

The place on a chromosome where a gene sits. The two alleles of a gene in a diploid cell are at the same locus on the two homologs.

Genotype

The combination of alleles an individual has for a gene (or genes): AA, Aa or aa.

Phenotype

The observable trait: yellow or green seeds. AA and Aa can have the same phenotype.

Homozygous

Having two identical alleles of a gene: AA or aa.

Heterozygous

Having two different alleles of a gene: Aa.

Gamete

A haploid reproductive cell (egg, sperm, or the ovule's egg and the pollen's sperm in plants) carrying one allele of each gene.

Zygote

The fertilized egg, formed by the random union of two gametes, one from each parent. It has two alleles of each gene.

Segregate

To separate. The two alleles of a gene segregate into different gametes, because homologous chromosomes separate in anaphase I.

Mendel's Law of Segregation

The two alleles of a gene separate (segregate) equally into gametes, so half the gametes carry one allele and half carry the other.

Check yourself: What do the letters on the outside and the inside of a Punnett square represent?

Outside: the alleles in each parent's gametes (one parent across the top, the other down the side), with their frequencies. Inside: the genotypes of the offspring, each made by one gamete from each parent.

Check yourself: What is the difference between a genotype ratio and a phenotype ratio?

A genotype ratio counts allele combinations (1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa). A phenotype ratio counts how the offspring look (3 yellow : 1 green). With dominance, different genotypes can share a phenotype, so the ratios differ.

Check yourself: True-breeding yellow × true-breeding green gives all-yellow F1. If the F1 self-cross, what fraction of the F2 is yellow AND heterozygous?

1/2. Aa is 2 of the 4 boxes, and every Aa seed is yellow.

HE TESTED ITThe square predicts that among yellow F2 seeds, 1/3 are AA (true-breeding) and 2/3 are Aa. Mendel self-fertilized yellow F2 plants (an F2 cross): 166 bred true and 353 gave some green, close to 1 : 2.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Punnett square

A grid for predicting a cross: one parent's gametes and their frequencies go across the top, the other's down the side, and each box is a possible offspring genotype.

Monohybrid cross

A cross in which both parents are heterozygous for the one gene being traced: Aa × Aa. (This course's definition.)

THE MATH

The AND and OR rules

A Punnett square is a picture of probabilities. Every probability is between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain), and two rules combine them.1,2

Check yourself: How do the 'both/and' and 'either/or' rules work?

Both/and: for independent events that happen together, multiply (A from mom AND a from dad: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4). Either/or: for outcomes that can't happen at the same time, add (AA or Aa: 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4).

Check yourself: p(Aa) from Aa × Aa, with the rules alone?

A from mom AND a from dad = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4; OR a from mom AND A from dad = 1/4. Add the two ways: 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2.

Check yourself: Xeroderma pigmentosum is a recessive skin condition. A woman homozygous for the dominant allele and a homozygous man with the condition have a child. Chance the child has it?

0%. Call the alleles P (dominant) and p (the condition). The woman is PP and gives only P; the man is pp and gives only p. Every child is Pp: a carrier who doesn't have the condition.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Carrier

A heterozygous individual (Aa) who carries one copy of a recessive allele without showing the recessive trait, and can pass it on.

Check yourself: What is a testcross and what does it allow you to figure out?

A cross of an individual with an unknown genotype to a homozygous recessive individual. It reveals the unknown's genotype: all dominant offspring → homozygous dominant (AA); about half recessive → heterozygous (Aa). It also directly shows segregation (a 1 : 1 ratio).

COMMON MIX-UPCross the unknown with the homozygous recessive, not the dominant. AA × AA and Aa × AA both give all-dominant offspring, so a dominant tester can't tell them apart.
KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Testcross

A cross of an individual with an unknown genotype to a homozygous recessive individual. The offspring's phenotypes reveal the unknown parent's gametes, and so its genotype.

Lock it in · Part 2

  1. Segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate equally into gametes (1/2 A, 1/2 a). It happens in anaphase I, when homologs separate.
  2. Punnett square: gametes outside, offspring inside. Aa × Aa (a monohybrid cross) gives genotypes 1 : 2 : 1 and phenotypes 3 : 1.
  3. AND → multiply (independent events). OR → add (mutually exclusive outcomes).
  4. A testcross (unknown × homozygous recessive) reveals the unknown genotype: all dominant → AA; half recessive → Aa.

Two or more genes: independent assortment

Dihybrid cross

A cross tracing two genes in which both parents are heterozygous for both: AaBb × AaBb. (This course's definition.) It gives a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotype ratio.

MENDEL'S SECOND LAW

Independent assortment comes from meiosis

The Law of Independent Assortment: the alleles of one gene segregate into gametes independently of the alleles of another. For genes on different chromosomes, the reason is metaphase I: each homologous pair lines up independently of the others.1,3

Check yourself: Where in meiosis does independent assortment occur? How does it help predict the alleles in gametes?

In metaphase I (with the separation in anaphase I): each homologous pair lines up independently, so which allele of gene A goes to a pole doesn't affect which allele of gene B goes with it. So an AaBb individual makes AB, Ab, aB and ab gametes in equal proportions, 1/4 each.

Check yourself: 2n = 4, heterozygous for two genes on separate chromosomes. How many kinds of gametes can one germ cell make? The whole organism?

One germ cell: 2 kinds (it lines up one way only: AB + ab, or Ab + aB). The whole organism: 4 kinds (AB, Ab, aB, ab), because different germ cells line up different ways.

Check yourself: Do chromosomes assort independently during mitosis?

No. Homologs never pair in mitosis; sister chromatids separate, and both daughter cells get every chromosome. Independent assortment happens only in meiosis I.

ONE CELL, PAIRS SEPARATINGSegregationA cell in anaphase I with A pulled from a and B from b shows the law of segregation. (One cell shows only one orientation.)
TWO CELLS, DIFFERENT WAYSBoth lawsTwo cells lined up different ways, each separating its homologs, show both segregation and independent assortment.
NONDISJUNCTION IN MEIOSIS IBreaks segregationIf the A/a pair fails to separate in meiosis I, A and a go to the same gamete: the law of segregation is violated.
Check yourself: Why is nondisjunction during meiosis II not a violation of independent assortment?

Independent assortment happens in meiosis I, when the homologous pairs line up and separate. By meiosis II the combination of maternal and paternal chromosomes in each cell has already been chosen at random. Nondisjunction in meiosis II only fails to separate sister chromatids, copies of the same allele, so it changes how many copies a gamete gets, not which combination of different genes' alleles travel together.

NOT EVERY GENEGenes close together on the same chromosome usually travel together and do not assort independently: they are linked. That comes in a later module.

Check yourself: When might you use two Punnett squares together instead of one big one?

Whenever the genes assort independently (different chromosomes). It is faster and less error-prone, especially when you want one particular genotype or phenotype, or when there are three or more genes (a big square would have 64 or more boxes).

Vocab · 26 terms
Allele One version of a gene: a variation of the gene's DNA sequence at a particular place on a chromosome. Mendel's "hereditary factors".
Carrier A heterozygous individual (Aa) who carries one copy of a recessive allele without showing the recessive trait, and can pass it on.
Dominant The trait (or allele) that shows in the F1 when two true-breeding strains are crossed; one copy of the dominant allele is enough to give the trait. Written with a capital letter (A).
Dihybrid cross A cross tracing two genes in which both parents are heterozygous for both: AaBb × AaBb. (This course's definition.) It gives a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotype ratio.
F1 cross A cross between F1 individuals (or an F1 self-fertilized), for example Aa × Aa. It produces the F2.
F1 generation The first filial generation: the offspring of the parental cross. When the parents are true-breeding for different traits, all F1 are heterozygous hybrids and show the dominant trait.
F2 cross A cross between F2 individuals (or an F2 self-fertilized). Mendel self-fertilized F2 plants to tell the true-breeding AA from the Aa.
F2 generation The second filial generation: the offspring of the F1 cross. The recessive trait reappears in the F2.
Gamete A haploid reproductive cell (egg, sperm, or the ovule's egg and the pollen's sperm in plants) carrying one allele of each gene.
Genotype The combination of alleles an individual has for a gene (or genes): AA, Aa or aa.
Heterozygous Having two different alleles of a gene: Aa.
Homozygous Having two identical alleles of a gene: AA or aa.
Hybridization Interbreeding between two different varieties (strains) or species. Mendel's crosses between true-breeding strains are hybridizations, and their offspring are hybrids.
Locus The place on a chromosome where a gene sits. The two alleles of a gene in a diploid cell are at the same locus on the two homologs.
Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment The segregation of one gene's alleles is independent of the segregation of another gene's alleles (for genes on different chromosomes), so all combinations of alleles appear in gametes in equal proportions.
Mendel's Law of Segregation The two alleles of a gene separate (segregate) equally into gametes, so half the gametes carry one allele and half carry the other.
Monohybrid cross A cross in which both parents are heterozygous for the one gene being traced: Aa × Aa. (This course's definition.)
Parental (P) generation The first generation in a series of crosses: here the true-breeding parents (written P or P₁).
Parental cross The cross between the two parents of the P generation, for example true-breeding yellow AA × true-breeding green aa. It produces the F1.
Phenotype The observable trait: yellow or green seeds. AA and Aa can have the same phenotype.
Punnett square A grid for predicting a cross: one parent's gametes and their frequencies go across the top, the other's down the side, and each box is a possible offspring genotype.
Recessive The trait (or allele) that does not show in the F1, and shows only in homozygotes (aa). Written with a lowercase letter (a).
Segregate To separate. The two alleles of a gene segregate into different gametes, because homologous chromosomes separate in anaphase I.
Testcross A cross of an individual with an unknown genotype to a homozygous recessive individual. The offspring's phenotypes reveal the unknown parent's gametes, and so its genotype.
True-breeding A strain whose offspring look the same as the parents, generation after generation, when bred among themselves: homozygous for the trait.
Zygote The fertilized egg, formed by the random union of two gametes, one from each parent. It has two alleles of each gene.

THE BIG IDEA

Each gene has two alleles that segregate equally into gametes, because homologs separate in anaphase I. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, because pairs line up independently in metaphase I. With a Punnett square or the AND and OR rules, any cross can be predicted.

  • Counted the offspring, looking for ratios; others only noted presence or absence.
  • Hybridization = crossing different varieties. Mendel controlled crosses by removing anthers, dusting pollen and bagging flowers.

Genotypes, gametes and offspring for one gene

  • Dominant = shows in the F1 of two true-breeding parents; recessive = hidden in the F1, back in a quarter of the F2.
  • Genotype (AA, Aa, aa) vs. phenotype (yellow, green). Homozygous AA or aa; heterozygous Aa; a heterozygote for a recessive condition is a carrier.

SECTION 3OBJECTIVE 4

Segregation and meiosis

  • Law of Segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate equally into gametes; each gamete gets one.
  • It happens because the alleles are on homologous chromosomes, which separate in anaphase I.
  • An F1 Aa germ cell: after S, A A and a a; anaphase I sends A one way and a the other; after meiosis II, 2 A gametes and 2 a gametes.
SECTION 4OBJECTIVE 5

Independent assortment and meiosis

  • Law of Independent Assortment: the alleles of one gene segregate independently of the alleles of another (genes on different chromosomes).
  • It happens because homologous pairs line up independently in metaphase I: AaBb → AB, Ab, aB, ab, 1/4 each.
  • One germ cell makes only 2 kinds of gamete (AB + ab, or Ab + aB); the whole organism makes all 4.
  • No independent assortment in mitosis. Genes close together on one chromosome are linked and do not assort independently (a later module).
  • Nondisjunction in meiosis I breaks segregation; nondisjunction in meiosis II doesn't break independent assortment (the combination was set in meiosis I).
SECTION 5OBJECTIVES 6, 7 AND 8

Two genes: genotypes, gametes, offspring

  • Write genotypes gene by gene: AaBb. A phenotype like "yellow round" covers several genotypes: A_ B_.
  • Parents → gametes: AAbb → Ab only; aaBB → aB only; AaBb → AB, Ab, aB, ab.
  • AaBb × AaBb: a 4 × 4 square, 16 boxes, 9 genotypes, 4 phenotypes: 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb.
  • Shortcut: solve each gene separately and multiply. p(AaBb) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 4/16; p(yellow round) = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.
  • Rows and columns = the number of gamete types of each parent.
  • Three or more genes: gamete types = 2ⁿ, n = number of heterozygous genes (BbLlRr → 8; BBLlRr → 4). For one offspring genotype, multiply gene by gene (AaBBCcDd × AaBbCcdd → aaBBccdd = 1/4 × 1/2 × 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/64).
SECTION 6OBJECTIVES 9 AND 10

Testcrosses and working backwards

  • Testcross: unknown (dominant phenotype) × homozygous recessive. All dominant offspring → homozygous; about half recessive → heterozygous.
  • Two genes: test with aabb and read each gene on its own. Brown long × bbll → all brown, half short → BB Ll.
  • Working backwards: 3 : 1 → both parents heterozygous; 1 : 1 → one heterozygous, one homozygous recessive; all dominant → at least one parent homozygous dominant; 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 → both parents dihybrid.
  • TtRr × ttrr → TtRr, Ttrr, ttRr, ttrr, 1/4 each; never TtRR.

Guiding questions · Crosses, Punnett squares and probability

These are the questions this module is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q1  What happens during a parental cross, an F1 cross, a monohybrid cross, and a dihybrid cross?

Parental cross: two true-breeding parents with different traits are crossed (AA × aa); all offspring, the F1, are heterozygous (Aa) and show the dominant trait.

F1 cross: two F1 individuals are crossed (or an F1 self-fertilizes), Aa × Aa, producing the F2, where the recessive trait reappears (3 : 1).

Monohybrid cross: both parents are heterozygous for the one gene traced (Aa × Aa): genotypes 1 : 2 : 1, phenotypes 3 : 1.

Dihybrid cross: both parents are heterozygous for two genes (AaBb × AaBb): phenotypes 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

Q2  What do the letters on the outside and the inside of a Punnett square represent?

Outside: the alleles carried by each parent's gametes, one parent across the top and the other down the side, with their frequencies (Aa → A 1/2, a 1/2).

Inside: the offspring genotypes (zygotes), each formed by the gamete in that row joining the gamete in that column; each box's probability is the row's times the column's.

Q3  Given the parental and F1 crosses shown at the start of the lecture, why do we often say that recessive traits “skip a generation”?

In the parental cross (AA × aa) every F1 offspring is Aa and shows the dominant trait, so the recessive trait disappears in the F1. But the F1 still carry the recessive allele, so in the F1 cross (Aa × Aa) a quarter of the F2 are aa and show it again: it seems to "skip" the F1 generation.

Q4  What is the difference between a genotype ratio and a phenotype ratio?

A genotype ratio compares allele combinations (Aa × Aa: 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa). A phenotype ratio compares observable traits (3 yellow : 1 green). With dominance, AA and Aa look the same, so the two ratios differ.

Q5  Where in meiosis does segregation occur? How does the law of segregation help us make predictions about the alleles that are found in gametes?

In anaphase I of meiosis, when the homologous chromosomes carrying the two alleles separate to opposite poles.

It tells us each gamete gets exactly one allele of each gene, and that a heterozygote's two alleles go into gametes equally: AA → all A; aa → all a; Aa → 1/2 A and 1/2 a. Those gamete frequencies go on the outside of a Punnett square.

Q6  How do the ‘both/and’ and ‘either/or’ rules work?

Both/and (multiplication rule): the probability that two independent events both happen is the product of their probabilities. p(A from mom AND a from dad) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.

Either/or (addition rule): the probability that one or another of mutually exclusive outcomes happens is the sum. p(AA OR Aa) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4.

Q7  Where in meiosis does independent assortment occur? How does the law of independent assortment help us make predictions about the alleles that are found in gametes?

In metaphase I (with the separation in anaphase I): each homologous pair lines up independently of every other pair, so which allele of one gene goes to a pole doesn't affect which allele of another gene goes with it.

So for genes on different chromosomes, every combination of alleles is equally likely in gametes: AaBb → AB, Ab, aB, ab, 1/4 each (1/2 × 1/2).

Q8  What cross gives you a 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio?

A dihybrid cross: AaBb × AaBb, where the two genes assort independently and each has a dominant and a recessive allele affecting a different trait. 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb.

Q9  Assume we are tracing the inheritance of two genes each located on separate chromosomes in an organism with 2n = 4. How many different kinds of gametes can one germ cell, heterozygous for both genes, make? How many different kinds of gametes can that whole organism make (when considering the whole population of germ cells)?

One germ cell: 2 kinds of gamete. It lines up one way in metaphase I, giving either AB and ab, or Ab and aB.

The whole organism: 4 kinds (AB, Ab, aB, ab), because half its germ cells line up one way and half the other.

Q10  Why is nondisjunction during meiosis II not a violation of independent assortment?

Independent assortment happens in meiosis I, when homologous pairs line up independently and separate. By meiosis II, the combination of chromosomes in each cell is already set. Nondisjunction in meiosis II only fails to separate sister chromatids, copies of the same allele, so it changes the number of copies in a gamete, not which alleles of different genes go together.

Q11  How many rows and columns do you need in a Punnett square? When might you use two Punnett Squares together instead of one big Punnett square? Explain your answers.

One row for each kind of gamete one parent can make and one column for each kind the other can make (Aa × Aa: 2 × 2; AaBb × AaBb: 4 × 4; AaBb × aabb: 4 × 1).

Use two (or more) small squares, one per gene, when the genes assort independently: solve each gene separately and multiply the probabilities (AND rule). It is faster and less error-prone than a 16- or 64-box square, especially when you need one specific genotype or phenotype.

Q12  How can you determine which trait is dominant, when two true-breeding parents are crossed? Consider a situation where we are tracing inheritance of one gene and the situation where we are tracing inheritance of two genes.

One gene: the trait that appears in the F1 of two true-breeding parents is dominant; the one that disappears is recessive (yellow × green → all yellow, so yellow is dominant).

Two genes: look at each trait separately in the F1. Yellow wrinkled × green round → all yellow round, so yellow is dominant to green and round is dominant to wrinkled.

Q13  What is a test cross and what does it allow you to figure out?

A testcross crosses an individual of unknown genotype (with the dominant phenotype) to a homozygous recessive individual. Because the tester only gives recessive alleles, the offspring's phenotypes show which alleles the unknown parent's gametes carried.

It tells you the unknown genotype: all offspring dominant → homozygous (AA); about half recessive → heterozygous (Aa). With two genes, read each gene separately (all brown, half short-tailed → BB Ll).

Q14  How can you figure out the number of unique gamete genotypes for an organism that is heterozygous for 3 or more genes? What about if an organism has a genotype with a mixture of heterozygous and homozygous genes?

Use 2ⁿ, where n is the number of genes for which the organism is heterozygous (assuming the genes assort independently): AaBbCc → 2³ = 8 kinds of gamete.

Homozygous genes give only one allele, so they don't add variety: BBLlRr → n = 2, 2² = 4 kinds (BLR, BLr, BlR, Blr). A branching diagram lists them all.
