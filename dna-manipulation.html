MODULE 3.1 · PART 1

Copying a gene in a tube

THE QUESTIONHow do you get enough of one gene to study it, when a cell has only two copies?

A replication warm-up, why amplify DNA, PCR step by step, the two keys (primers and Taq), PCR vs. replication in a cell, and reverse transcriptase for RNA.

STEP 1 · WARM-UP · WHAT PCR BORROWS

First, a replication refresher

Several lab techniques are built on how DNA is structured and copied. Three questions from Module 2.3 before we start: everything PCR does, a cell already does when it replicates.

CLICKER QUESTIONBefore DNA replication, the two strands must be separated. This job is done by:
  1. Topoisomerase
  2. Telomerase
  3. Primase
  4. Helicase
  5. DNA polymerase
Show the answer
D. Helicase. It unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between the bases. (In PCR, heat will do this job.)
CLICKER QUESTIONDNA polymerase can't start a brand-new strand. Synthesis of a new chain begins with the action of:
  1. Topoisomerase
  2. Telomerase
  3. Primase
  4. Helicase
  5. DNA polymerase
Show the answer
C. Primase. It lays down a short RNA primer, and DNA polymerase extends it. (In PCR, the primers are short DNA pieces made in a lab.)
CLICKER QUESTIONDuring DNA replication in a cell, DNA synthesis takes place:
  1. 3′→5′ on the lagging strand, 5′→3′ on the leading strand
  2. 3′→5′ on the leading strand, 5′→3′ on the lagging strand
  3. Always 3′→5′
  4. Always 5′→3′
Show the answer
D. Always 5′→3′. DNA polymerase only adds nucleotides to a 3′ end, on both strands. The lagging strand is just made in short pieces.

STEP 2 · THE BIG PICTURE · TWO METHODS

Two methods, one goal: lots of copies of one gene

This lecture covers two different techniques. Both make many copies of one specific gene, but in very different ways. Part 1 is all Method 1; Part 2 is all Method 2.

METHOD 1 · PART 1 OF THIS PAGEPCR: copy the gene in a tube
WHEREIn a tube. No cells at all.
YOU NEEDA DNA template, two primers, dNTPs and a heat-stable polymerase (Taq).
HOWHeat, cool, warm, 25 to 40 times; the copies double every cycle.
YOU GETMillions of copies of one DNA segment, in a few hours.
MAKES THE PROTEIN?No. PCR only copies DNA.
METHOD 2 · PART 2 OF THIS PAGEMolecular cloning: copy the gene in bacteria
WHEREInside living bacteria (E. coli).
YOU NEEDA plasmid vector, restriction enzymes, DNA ligase, bacteria and an antibiotic.
HOWPaste the gene into a plasmid, put the plasmid into bacteria, and let them grow.
YOU GETBacteria full of the plasmid: every time a cell divides, the gene is copied.
MAKES THE PROTEIN?It can: with an expression vector, the bacteria make the protein the gene codes for.
WHERE THE TWO METHODS MEET
They're separate techniques, but they're often used together. At the very end of this page, to get bacteria to make a human hormone, you first use reverse transcription and PCR (Method 1) to make the gene's intronless cDNA, then clone it (Method 2).
STEP 3 · THE BIG PICTURE · CASE FILES

Why copy DNA? Where these techniques are used

A cell has only two copies of most of its DNA, far too little to see, measure or work with. That's the problem these techniques solve, in labs, hospitals and courtrooms. Exam questions often hide the technique inside a story. In each case, ask: is the problem too little DNA (PCR), a starting material that is RNA (reverse transcription first), comparing sizes (a gel), or making a protein (cloning)?

STEP 4 · METHOD 1 · PCR · HOW IT WORKS

PCR, step by step: denature, anneal, extend

PCR is DNA replication in a tube, run over and over. Watch one tube go through one cycle; the only thing that changes between steps is the temperature.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
SET UP · ONE TUBEEverything goes in at the startInto one small tube: the template DNA containing the region to copy, two primers, dNTPs (dATP, dGTP, dCTP, dTTP: the free nucleotides that become the new strands) and a heat-stable DNA polymerase, Taq.Nothing else is added later. A machine just changes the temperature, over and over. The primers are short single strands of DNA made in a lab, called oligonucleotides ("oligo" = few), chosen to match the two ends of the target.
STEP 1 · DENATURATIONHeat pulls the helix apartThe tube is heated almost to boiling. That breaks the hydrogen bonds between the paired bases, and the double helix separates into two single strands. Heat does helicase's job.
STEP 2 · ANNEALINGCool, and the primers stickThe tube is cooled. Now hydrogen bonds can form again, and each primer base-pairs with its complementary sequence on one template strand: A with T, G with C. There are so many primers that they win the race against the long template strands pairing back up.Each primer pairs at the 3′ end of its template strand, so its own 3′ end points into the target.
STEP 3 · EXTENSIONTaq builds from the 3′ endThe tube is warmed to Taq's best working temperature. Taq adds dNTPs one at a time to each primer's 3′ end, matching each base to the template, so the new strands grow 5′ → 3′, just as in a cell. The two new strands grow in opposite directions, because the two templates run in opposite directions.
REPEAT · THE CHAIN REACTIONEach cycle doubles the copiesHeat, cool, warm: then again, 25 to 40 times. Each round doubles the number of molecules with the template's sequence: 2 after cycle 1, 4 after cycle 2, 2ⁿ after n cycles.After a few rounds, almost every molecule is exactly the stretch between the two primers.
COMMON MIX-UP
Primers do not stick because of DNA's negative charge. DNA and primers are both negatively charged (their phosphate backbones), so charge pushes them apart. Primers stick by complementary base pairing: hydrogen bonds between A and T, and G and C. Cooling lets those bonds form.
COMMON MIX-UP
The primers in PCR are DNA, made in a lab. In a cell, primase makes RNA primers. Both give DNA polymerase a 3′ end to extend.
THINK ABOUT IT
Thirty cycles from one molecule: 2³⁰ is about a billion copies. That's why PCR can work from a single hair, a fingerprint or one embryonic cell.
STEP 5 · METHOD 1 · PCR · DO THE MATH

How many copies after n cycles?

Each cycle copies every double-stranded DNA molecule in the tube, so the number doubles: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16... That's why it's called a chain reaction.

STEP 6 · METHOD 1 · PCR · TWO KEYS

What makes PCR work: primers you can design, and a polymerase that survives the heat

Two things had to be solved before PCR could work.

KEY 1 · SEQUENCE INFORMATION
To design the two primers, you need to know the sequence at both ends of the region you want to copy. The primers decide which segment of the template gets amplified: only the stretch between them.
KEY 2 · A HEAT-STABLE DNA POLYMERASE
Most DNA polymerases, including ours, are destroyed by the heat that separates the strands, so early PCR meant adding fresh enzyme every cycle. Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium that lives in near-boiling hot springs (such as those in Yellowstone). It survives the heat, so the tube can cycle between heating and copying again and again.
CLICKER QUESTIONWhat would happen if in a PCR reaction you used a DNA polymerase isolated not from Thermus aquaticus, but from an organism that grows at lower temperature?
  1. Only one of the two template strands would be replicated
  2. There would be very little or no DNA produced
  3. PCR would proceed normally
  4. As long as helicase is added, PCR would proceed normally
Show the answer
B. Very little or no DNA. The first heating step destroys a normal polymerase, so copying stops. Helicase doesn't help: the heat is still needed. (Question set.)
CLICKER QUESTIONIf you performed a PCR reaction but accidentally added only one of the two primers, what would the product be?
  1. There would be no product
  2. Only one DNA strand would be synthesized
  3. Both DNA strands would be synthesized
  4. The missing primer would be made in the first cycle
Show the answer
B. Only one strand. Each primer copies one strand. With one primer, only that strand is copied each cycle, so the copies add up slowly instead of doubling. Nothing in the tube can make a primer. (Question set.)
CLICKER QUESTIONThe diagram shows a PCR reaction with primers annealed to the templates. In the next round, nucleotides will be added at which positions?
  1. III and IV
  2. I and V
  3. II and IV
  4. II and V
Show the answer
C. II and IV. Polymerase only adds to a primer's 3′ end. The top template runs 3′ → 5′ left to right, so the primer under it runs 5′ → 3′ left to right: its 3′ end is II. The bottom primer runs the other way, so its 3′ end is IV. (Question set.)

Why the cDNA has no introns

THE PROBLEMDNA polymerase can only copy DNAWhat if the thing you want to copy is RNA, such as a virus's RNA genome or a cell's mRNA? DNA polymerase can only use DNA as a template, so PCR can't start from RNA.In eukaryotes, a gene's mRNA is also shorter than the gene: splicing has removed the introns.

WHY THIS IS SUPER IMPORTANT: NO INTRONS
To get bacteria to make a human protein (Part 2), the gene you give them must have no introns. Bacteria have no spliceosome, so they would read an intron straight into the protein and make garbage. So you don't use the gene from human DNA. You take the protein's mRNA, already spliced, from human cells that are making that protein (for growth hormone, pituitary cells), and reverse transcribe it into cDNA. That cDNA is what goes into the plasmid.

CLICKER QUESTIONWhich of the following enzymes can perform a reverse transcription reaction (make a DNA copy of an RNA molecule)?
  1. DNA polymerase
  2. RNA polymerase
  3. Telomerase

KEY TAKEAWAYS · PART 1
1. PCR amplifies specific DNA segments in a tube. The primers decide which segment of the template is amplified, and a heat-stable polymerase must be used. 2. RNA must be reverse transcribed into cDNA before it can be amplified by PCR.

IN NATURESmall extra circles of DNAPlasmids are small, circular molecules of DNA, separate from the bacterial chromosome. Bacteria copy them and pass them to their daughter cells when they divide.
IN THE LAB · A VECTORThree parts every plasmid vector needsA vector is a carrier: a DNA molecule that carries a piece of foreign DNA into a cell and gets it copied there, the way a delivery truck carries cargo. A plasmid vector is a plasmid built for that job. It is circular double-stranded DNA with three essential parts:1. An origin of replication (Ori), so the bacterium copies the plasmid. 2. An antibiotic resistance gene, also called the selectable marker, such as ampicillin resistance (AmpR), so you can tell which bacteria have it. 3. A cloning site (multiple cloning site, MCS) where your DNA of interest goes in.Plasmids let you make more copies of a gene of interest.
WHAT'S A VECTOR?
A vector is a carrier: a DNA molecule used to carry a piece of foreign DNA into a cell, where the cell copies it (and sometimes reads it). Think of a delivery truck: your gene is the cargo, the vector gets it into the bacterium, and the vector's own parts (an origin of replication, an antibiotic resistance gene, a cloning site) make the delivery work. Plasmids are the most common vectors.
STEP 2 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · STEP 1A · CUT

Restriction enzymes cut DNA at palindromes

To put a piece of DNA into a plasmid, you first cut both with the same molecular scissors. Those scissors look for palindromes.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
FIND THE SITEScissors that read the sequenceRestriction enzymes are endonucleases that cut DNA at specific sequences. There are hundreds, each cutting at a different sequence. EcoRI slides along the DNA and cuts only where it finds GAATTC, a 6 bp recognition site.How to spot where it will cut: scan the sequence for the enzyme's site. Every place you find GAATTC, EcoRI cuts; no GAATTC, no cut.
READ BOTH STRANDSDirection mattersThe two strands of DNA run in opposite directions (antiparallel): one runs 5′ → 3′ left to right, its partner 5′ → 3′ right to left.Read each strand in its own 5′ → 3′ direction and both say GAATTC. That's what a DNA palindrome is, and it means the enzyme sees the same site from either strand.
THE CUTSame spot on each strand, offsetEcoRI always cuts between the G and the A: G | AATTC, reading 5′ → 3′. It does that on both strands.Because the strands point opposite ways, "right after the G" is near the left end of the site on the top strand and near the right end on the bottom strand. So it isn't one clean cut through the middle: the two cuts are 4 bases apart, a staggered cut.
STICKY ENDSShort single-stranded tailsThe pieces fall apart, each ending in a 4-base single-stranded tail, AATT. The tails are complementary, so any two pieces cut by the same enzyme can base-pair again: sticky ends. That's what makes cut-and-paste possible.
BLUNT ENDSSome enzymes cut straight acrossEcoRV recognizes GATATC and cuts both strands at the same point, in the exact middle: GAT | ATC. That leaves blunt ends with no tail.Heads-up: the lecture slide prints EcoRV's site as GGATCC. That sequence is actually BamHI's site, which BamHI cuts G | GATCC, giving sticky ends. EcoRV's site is GATATC.

WHAT'S A PALINDROME (IN DNA)?
A word palindrome (racecar) reads the same backwards. A DNA palindrome is different: it reads the same on both strands, each read in its own 5′ → 3′ direction. You compare a strand with its partner, not with itself. Test: write the complementary strand underneath, read it right to left (that's its 5′ → 3′), and see if you get the same letters.
COMMON MIX-UP
The trap: comparing the two ends of one strand. ACGGCA reads the same backwards (A·C·G | G·C·A), so it looks like a palindrome, but its partner strand reads TGCCGT: not a DNA palindrome. Meanwhile GAATTC backwards is CTTAAG, not the same at all, yet it is a DNA palindrome. Shortcut: in a DNA palindrome, the first and last letters are complementary (G…C), then the next pair in (A…T), and so on. Identical ends (A…A) mean it's not.
How many times does EcoRI cut 5′-AGAATTCTTAGGAATTCCA-3′, and how many pieces do you get?

2 cuts, 3 pieces. Scan for GAATTC: it appears twice (AGAATTC… and …GGAATTCCA). A straight piece of DNA cut in two places falls into 3 pieces. (A circular plasmid cut twice gives 2 pieces.)

STEP 3 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · STEP 1B · SORT

Gel electrophoresis separates DNA pieces by size

After cutting, you have a mix of pieces. Gel electrophoresis sorts them by size, so you can find and isolate the fragment you want.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 3
LOADThree lanes, three samplesDNA samples go into small wells near one end of a gel, a slab like firm jelly made of a tangle of polymers. Each sample runs down its own lane:Ladder: a mix of DNA pieces of known sizes, like the marks on a ruler. You don't learn anything from the ladder itself; you compare your bands with it to read their sizes. Cut DNA: DNA cut by a restriction enzyme, so several pieces of different sizes. PCR product: the copies from a PCR, which should all be the same size.
RUNCharge pulls, size decides speedAn electric current runs through the gel. DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate backbone), so every piece moves toward the positive pole.The tangle of the gel slows big pieces down more: small pieces travel farthest, big pieces stay near the wells. Speed depends on size only, not sequence, so each size makes one band.
READCompare with the ladderA dye that glows under UV light shows the bands. Comparing them with the ladder gives their sizes.That's how you check PCR (one band of the expected size?), and how you pull out the one restriction fragment you want to clone: cut its band out of the gel.
STEP 4 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · STEP 1B · PRACTICE

Reading a restriction map with a gel

A favorite question type: a plasmid map with restriction sites, a gel, and "which lane?" Here's how to do it every time.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 6
STEP 1What the numbers meanX, Y and Z are restriction sites. The numbers are the sizes (in kb) of the DNA between neighboring sites, not positions. Add them up to get the whole plasmid: 5 + 2 + 4 + 1 = 12 kb.Notice Y appears twice: enzyme Y cuts in two places.
STEP 2One cut doesn't make two piecesA plasmid is a circle. Cutting a circle once just opens it into one straight piece, the full 12 kb. That's lane K.Rule: a circle cut in n places gives n pieces. (A straight piece of DNA cut in n places gives n + 1.)
STEP 3 · THE QUESTIONX and Y together, not ZCut at X and at both Y's: 3 cuts, so 3 pieces. Walk around the circle from cut to cut, skipping Z because enzyme Z isn't in the tube:X → Y = 5. Y → Y = 2. Y → (past Z) → X = 4 + 1 = 5. Two pieces are both 5 kb, so they run together as one band. Bands at 5 and 2: lane M.
CHECK · X + ZThe other question in your setTwo cuts, two pieces: Z → X = 1, and X → Y → Y → Z = 5 + 2 + 4 = 11. That's lane Q.
CHECK · Y + ZThree cuts, three piecesY → Y = 2, Y → Z = 4, Z → X → Y = 1 + 5 = 6. That's lane L.
CHECK · ALL THREEEvery site cutsFour cuts, four pieces: 5, 2, 4 and 1. That's lane H. Every lane is now explained, which is a good way to check your answer.
THE METHOD
1. Add up the sizes to get the whole plasmid. 2. Mark only the sites of the enzymes in the tube. 3. Walk around the circle from cut to cut, adding up the sizes between them. 4. Pieces of the same size make one band. 5. Find the lane with exactly those bands.

  1. Q
  2. L
  3. M
  4. H
  5. K
Show the answer
C. Lane M. Pieces of 5, 2 and 5 kb; the two 5 kb pieces make a single band. (Question set.)
CLICKER QUESTIONUsing the same map: which lane represents cutting with both enzymes X and Z together, but not with enzyme Y?
  1. Q
  2. H
  3. K
  4. M
  5. L
Show the answer

CLICKER QUESTIONPeople with sickle-cell disease have one amino acid change (Glu to Val) at position 6, from a single base change. If you PCR amplify the gene with primers that anneal outside the gene and run the products on a gel, what would you expect?
  1. The normal gene's product would migrate faster
  2. The products from the mutant and normal genes would migrate the same
  3. The mutant gene's product would migrate faster
  4. The primers couldn't amplify the mutant gene
Show the answer
B. They would migrate the same. A gel separates DNA by size. Changing one base doesn't change the length, so both products run to the same place. (Question set.)
STEP 5 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · STEP 1C · PASTE

Ligation: making recombinant DNA

Step 1 of cloning: cut the gene and the plasmid with the same enzyme, then paste the gene into the plasmid's multiple cloning site.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
THE INSERTThe DNA you want to cloneHere, the insert is the gene's cDNA from Part 1. It needs a restriction site at each end; a common trick is to build one into each PCR primer, so every PCR copy ends in GAATTC.
CUT THE INSERTSticky ends on the cDNAEcoRI cuts at both ends, so the cDNA ends in sticky AATT tails.
STEP 1 · CUT THE PLASMID TOOSame enzyme, matching endsThe plasmid is cut at its multiple cloning site with the same restriction enzyme, so its sticky ends match the insert's. The insert slides into the opening and the tails base-pair.
LIGATIONDNA ligase seals the backboneDNA ligase joins the pieces for good by sealing the sugar-phosphate backbone, the same job it does for Okazaki fragments in replication.The result is a recombinant DNA molecule: DNA from two different sources joined into one. It works because the DNA of every organism is chemically the same; only the sequence differs.
STEP 6 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · STEP 1C · PRACTICE

Choosing enzymes: directional vs. non-directional cloning

A question type from your set that trips up a lot of people. It's just three rules.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 5
THE SETUPPick enzymes to cut out the gene and open the plasmidTo clone the GH gene, you cut the gene out and open the plasmid with the same enzyme(s), so the sticky ends match. Which enzymes work?
RULE 1Don't cut the geneAn enzyme that cuts inside the gene chops it in pieces. Nope cuts in the middle of the GH gene, so any answer with Nope is out.
RULE 2Cut the plasmid once, in the MCSThe enzyme should cut the plasmid only in the multiple cloning site. Ja also cuts inside AmpR, the antibiotic resistance gene; that would destroy the selectable marker. Ja is out.
NON-DIRECTIONALOne enzyme, both sides of the geneWith a single enzyme that cuts on both sides of the gene, the gene's two ends are the same, so it can go into the plasmid either way round. Na works: one site on each side of the gene, and only one site in the plasmid, in the MCS.
DIRECTIONALTwo different enzymesUse two different enzymes, one on each side of the gene. Now the two ends are different, and each can only pair with its own match in the plasmid, so the gene goes in only one way, facing the promoter the right way.Nit and Yep: Nit cuts left of the gene and Yep cuts right of it, and both cut the plasmid only in the MCS. Nit also cuts once more, past Yep, but that only trims off DNA outside the gene. (Yeah and Yep would also work.)
CLICKER QUESTIONUsing the plasmid and gene above: which enzymes would you use to clone the GH gene directionally?
  1. Nit and Yep
  2. Nope and Yep
  3. Ja and Na
  4. Na and Ok
Show the answer
A. Nit and Yep. Nope cuts inside the gene; Ja cuts AmpR; Na and Ok leaves Na ends on both sides of the gene, which isn't directional. (Question set.)
CLICKER QUESTIONUsing the same plasmid and gene: which enzyme would you use to clone the GH gene non-directionally?
  1. Na
  2. Nay
  3. Nope
  4. Ja
Show the answer
A. Na. Na cuts on both sides of the gene and once in the plasmid's MCS. Nay cuts only on one side; Nope cuts the gene; Ja cuts AmpR. (Question set.)
STEP 7 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · STEPS 2 AND 3 · TRANSFORM AND GROW

Transformation and selection: bacteria copy the plasmid for you

Steps 2 and 3 of cloning: get the recombinant plasmid into bacteria, then get rid of every bacterium that didn't take it up.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
STEP 2 · TRANSFORMATIONPlasmids meet bacteriaTransformation is a genetic change in a cell from taking up and expressing foreign genetic material. Here: the recombinant plasmid gets into E. coli.The plasmids are simply mixed with the bacteria. But a plasmid can't just walk through a healthy cell membrane.
THE SHOCKOpen the door, brieflySo you make the membrane more permeable with a chemical treatment or an electric shock. For a moment, DNA can get in.Even so, only a very small fraction of the cells take up a plasmid. Most of the cells in the tube still have none, and you can't tell which is which by looking.
STEP 3 · SELECTIONLet the antibiotic do the sortingNow grow the bacteria on agar containing ampicillin. It kills every cell without a plasmid.The cells that took up a plasmid also took up its ampicillin resistance gene (AmpR), so they survive. That's why the vector carries an antibiotic resistance gene: it lets you select the transformed cells.
GROW MORE PLASMIDThe survivors divideEach surviving cell divides again and again into a colony of its descendants, and the plasmid's origin of replication lets it be copied every time.Each colony contains many bacterial cells, and each cell has many copies of the plasmid: lots of your DNA of interest.
CLICKER QUESTIONA student cloned a piece of DNA into a plasmid, transformed bacteria and plated them on agar with antibiotics. The next day the bacteria covered the whole plate; there were no separate colonies. What could explain this?
  1. The wrong restriction enzymes were used.
  2. The student forgot to add the antibiotic to the plates.
  3. The student forgot to use ligase.
  4. The plasmid had no origin of replication.
  5. The plasmid had no antibiotic resistance marker.
Show the answer
B. No antibiotic on the plates. Without ampicillin nothing is killed, so every cell grows, with or without a plasmid, into a solid lawn instead of separate colonies. (Without a resistance marker, or an Ori, you'd get no colonies at all.)
WHAT'S A SELECTABLE MARKER?
The plasmid's antibiotic resistance gene (here AmpR, ampicillin resistance) is its selectable marker: a gene that lets you select the cells that have the plasmid. On a plate with the antibiotic, only cells carrying the marker survive, so every colony that grows has the plasmid. Without it, you'd have no way to find the few transformed cells among millions.
WHAT IF...WHAT GROWS ON THE PLATE
Everything worksSeparate colonies, every one with the plasmid
The antibiotic is missing or expiredEvery cell grows, plasmid or not: a solid lawn
The plasmid has no resistance markerNothing: even cells with the plasmid are killed
The plasmid has no origin of replicationNothing: the plasmid isn't copied, so it's lost as cells divide
CLICKER QUESTIONA student performs a cloning experiment and plates the transformed cells on antibiotic plates, not knowing the antibiotic had expired and no longer works. What will be the result?
  1. No colonies, since the bacteria can't grow on antibiotic plates
  2. Cells will grow, but their plasmid won't contain the inserted DNA
  3. The expected number of colonies, each with the plasmid and the proper insert
  4. Cells will be able to grow whether or not they contain the plasmid
Show the answer
D. Cells grow whether or not they have the plasmid. A dead antibiotic kills nothing, so nothing is selected. (Question set.)
CLICKER QUESTIONA student performs the same cloning experiment, but the plasmid used had no antibiotic resistance marker. What will be the result?
  1. No colonies, since the bacteria can't grow on the antibiotic plates
  2. Cells will grow, but their plasmid won't contain the inserted DNA
  3. The plate will be completely covered with bacteria
  4. Cells will grow, but they won't contain the plasmid
Show the answer
A. No colonies. Without the resistance gene, even the cells that took up the plasmid are killed by the antibiotic. (Question set.)
CLICKER QUESTIONWhen transforming plasmids into bacteria:
  1. Researchers need to increase the permeability of the bacterial cell so the plasmid can be taken in
  2. Restriction enzymes must first cut the plasmid into smaller pieces
  3. Researchers inject the plasmid with a narrow needle
  4. The bacterial chromosome must first be destroyed

STEP 8 · METHOD 2 · CLONING · THE APPLICATION

Bacteria that make a human hormone

Everything in this module adds up to one of the great breakthroughs of modern medicine: getting bacteria to make a human protein that patients need.

Tap the buttons, or Next step, to click through all 4
THE PROBLEMPituitary dwarfismIn pituitary dwarfism, the pituitary gland makes too little growth hormone, the protein encoded by the human gene GH1, so children grow far less than they should.The treatment is the hormone itself. But it has to be human growth hormone, and there was no good way to make enough of it.
THE IDEATurn bacteria into a factoryMolecular cloning cracked it: engineer E. coli to make human growth hormone. The gene goes into an expression vector, a special type of plasmid vector: an ordinary plasmid vector only gets a gene copied; an expression vector also gets it transcribed and translated, so the bacteria make the protein.Around the gene, it needs bacterial signals that E. coli's machinery can read: a bacterial promoter (where transcription starts), a ribosome binding site (where translation starts) and a transcription terminator. The human gene is cloned into the multiple cloning site between them, with the usual restriction enzymes and ligase.
THE CATCHNo introns allowedThe human gene has introns, and bacteria can't remove them.So we take the hormone's mRNA from human pituitary cells. It is already spliced. We perform reverse transcription on it to get cDNA, then put that in the vector.
THE PAYOFFA human hormone, made by bacteriaTransformed E. coli grow by the billions, and every cell makes human growth hormone from the plasmid. The hormone is purified and used to treat patients.Human insulin for diabetes is made the same way.
COMMON MIX-UP
The cDNA doesn't go into the patient. It goes into the bacteria; the bacteria make the protein; the protein is purified, and the protein is what the patient gets (as injections). The patient's own genes are never changed. (Putting DNA into a patient's own cells is a different technique, gene therapy.)
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
Before bacteria made it, growth hormone for patients was extracted from the pituitary glands of human cadavers, so it was scarce, and in 1985 it was withdrawn in the US after some patients developed a fatal brain disease (Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease) from contaminated batches. Growth hormone made by engineered bacteria was approved the same year. Human insulin made by E. coli (1982) was the first medicine made with recombinant DNA; before it, people with diabetes used insulin from cows and pigs.
CLICKER QUESTIONTo express human insulin (for treating patients) in bacteria, we need to:
  1. Add a human promoter to the plasmid
  2. Use reverse transcriptase to generate an insulin cDNA
  3. Add human ribosome binding site sequences to the plasmid
  4. Add human transcription terminator sequences to the plasmid
Show the answer
B. Make an insulin cDNA with reverse transcriptase. Bacteria can't splice introns, so they need the intronless cDNA. The promoter, ribosome binding site and terminator must be bacterial ones, since bacterial machinery has to read them.
KEY TAKEAWAYS · PART 2
1. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sites; pieces with compatible ends can be ligated back together. 2. Plasmids need an origin of replication, an antibiotic resistance gene and a multiple cloning site. 3. Transformed bacteria can make many copies of a plasmid. 4. An expression vector can get bacteria to make foreign proteins, as long as the gene is intronless cDNA.
