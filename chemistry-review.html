STEP 1 · WHERE ENERGY COMES FROM

Phototrophs and chemotrophs

Every living thing needs energy, and there are only two places to get it: light, or chemicals. Either way, it ends up stored in the chemical bonds of sugar.

VOCABPhototrophAn organism that captures energy from sunlight.photo = light · troph = feeder. Plants, algae and some bacteria.
VOCABChemotrophAn organism that gets its energy from chemical compounds, like the glucose in food.chemo = chemical · troph = feeder. Animals, fungi and many bacteria.

STEP 2 · REDOX

Redox reactions are coupled electron transfers

Moving an electron always takes two molecules: one gives it, one takes it. Watch the electrons leave A one at a time and pile up on B, and watch each charge change.

TRACK THE CHARGE · THE EASY WAY TO TELLReduced means its charge was reduced.
1Electrons are negative. So follow the charge on each molecule as they move.
2B's charge goes 0 → −2: its charge is literally reduced, so B is reduced.
3Something reduced B: A, by handing over its electrons. So A is the reducing agent.
4A lost those electrons: its charge goes 0 → +2, raised. A is oxidized, and B, the one that took them, is automatically the oxidizing agent.
One charge goes down, the other goes up, every time: that is why redox reactions are always coupled. Salt works the same way: Na → Na⁺ (charge up: oxidized, the reducing agent) and Cl → Cl⁻ (charge down: reduced, the oxidizing agent).
COMMON MIX-UP
Reduced doesn't mean "has less". A reduced molecule's charge went down because it now holds more electrons, and it usually holds more potential energy. Glucose is reduced; CO₂ is oxidized.
STEP 3 · CHEMICAL POTENTIAL ENERGY

Where the energy actually lives

So what does moving electrons have to do with energy? An electron's potential energy depends on where it sits.

Electrons far from a positive nucleus, or shared evenly between two atoms, are like a stretched rubber band: loaded. Electrons pulled in close, toward an atom like oxygen, are slack: little energy left.

So the energy in food is in its nonpolar bonds (C–H and C–C). Moving those electrons into polar bonds with oxygen releases it.

"HIGH-ENERGY" AND "LOW-ENERGY" MOLECULESHigh-energy usually means reduced. Low-energy usually means oxidized.
HIGH ENERGY · reducedElectrons held loosely, shared evenly in C–H and C–C bonds, far from a strong pull.

Respiration runs from the high-energy side to the low-energy side: glucose and O₂ in, CO₂ and H₂O out, and the difference is caught. The one exception is ATP: it is "high-energy" because of its three crowded, negatively charged phosphates, not because of redox. ADP is its low-energy form.

STEP 4 · LOOK CLOSER · ENERGY LIVES IN ELECTRONS

Where an electron sits sets its energy

Before we can see why glucose is fuel, we need one piece of physics: the tension between a positive nucleus and a negative electron.

FARFar out: lots of potential energyThe nucleus is full of protons (+). The electron is negative, so the nucleus pulls on it. Holding the electron far from that pull is like holding a rubber band stretched.That stretch is stored energy. The farther out the electron sits, the more it has.
SNAPLet it move closer: energy comes outIf the electron gets the chance to move closer to a positive nucleus, the band snaps back. The energy it held is released.This is all chemical energy is: electrons moving to spots where they are held more tightly.
CLOSEPulled in close: little leftAn electron already sitting close to a strong pull has a slack band. There is little energy left to release.Electrons on outer levels have more potential energy than ones close to the nucleus. Electrons close to other electrons (negative charges) also have more, because like charges push apart.
THE IDEA TO HOLD
The energy is not in the atom. It is in where the electron sits compared with positive and negative charges. Far from a nucleus = stretched band = high potential energy. Close = slack band = low.
STABLE MEANS LOW ENERGYA ball at the bottom of the stairs is stable: it has nowhere lower to fall. An electron held tightly is the same. Atoms are also most stable when their outer shell is full: 2 electrons for hydrogen, 8 for carbon, nitrogen and oxygen. That is why carbon makes 4 bonds, nitrogen 3, oxygen 2 and hydrogen 1.
STEP 5 · LOOK CLOSER · GETTING INTO THE WEEDS OF ELECTRONEGATIVITY

Why some atoms pull harder

Most definitions stop at "electronegativity is an atom's tendency to attract electrons." That says what, not why. Here is the why: count the protons, and see what stands between them and the electrons.

HYDROGEN1 proton, but nothing in the wayHydrogen's nucleus is a single proton, so its pull is small. But its electron sits in the first shell, very close, with no other electrons in between.Small pull, very close. It ends up about level with carbon.
CARBON6 protons, 2 of them hiddenCarbon has 6 protons. Its 2 inner electrons sit between the nucleus and the outer shell and cancel out about 2 of them. The outer electrons, the ones that form bonds, feel a pull of about +4.Carbon and hydrogen pull about equally. Remember this: it is why C–H bonds share electrons evenly.
NITROGENOne more proton, same shellNitrogen has 7 protons. The outer electrons are in the same shell as carbon's, but now they feel about +5.A stronger pull at the same distance draws the shell in tighter. That is why atoms get smaller across a row: the protons make the radius small.
OXYGENThe strongest pull in biologyOxygen has 8 protons. Its outer electrons feel about +6, still in the second shell, close to the nucleus.So oxygen is the most electronegative atom in biological molecules. In any bond with carbon or hydrogen, the shared electrons are dragged toward oxygen.
SULFURMore protons, but weaker. Why?Sulfur has 16 protons, twice oxygen's. But 10 inner electrons in two shells cancel out most of them, and its outer shell is one level farther out.About the same net pull, from farther away, is a weaker grip. So sulfur is less electronegative than oxygen even with more protons.
SO, IS IT PROTONS OR RADIUS?
You are right that it starts with the protons. Electronegativity is how hard a nucleus pulls on shared electrons, and the protons do the pulling. The full answer has two parts:
1 · How much charge the electrons feel: protons minus the inner electrons that cancel some of them.
2 · How far away they are: a pull gets weaker with distance.
Across a row, the protons win and also shrink the atom. Down a column, a new shell farther out wins, so the pull drops.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
The textbook explains the row: more protons hold electrons more tightly. The idea that inner electrons cancel part of the nucleus (called shielding, or effective nuclear charge) is from general chemistry, not the slides. The +4, +5 and +6 are a simple count (protons minus inner electrons), not measured values.
Rank the atoms of life by electronegativity, the way the slides do.

O > N, S, P > C ≈ H. Oxygen is the most electronegative; carbon and hydrogen are about equal.

STEP 6 · LOOK CLOSER · TENSION IN A BOND

Nonpolar vs. polar: where the shared electrons sit

In a covalent bond two atoms share a pair of electrons. Who pulls harder decides where that pair sits, and so how much energy the bond holds.

NONPOLAR COVALENTC–H: a tug of war nobody winsCarbon and hydrogen pull about equally, so the shared electrons sit in the middle, far from both nuclei.Both bands stay stretched. The electrons are held loosely, so the bond holds lots of potential energy. C–C bonds are the same.
POLAR COVALENTO–H: oxygen wins the tug of warOxygen pulls much harder, so the shared electrons sit close to the oxygen nucleus. Oxygen becomes slightly negative (δ−) and hydrogen slightly positive (δ+).Oxygen's band is slack. The electrons are already held tightly, so the bond holds little potential energy. It is stable. C=O bonds are the same.
THE TENSION, IN ONE LINE
Even sharing keeps electrons far from a strong pull: high tension, high energy. Uneven sharing lets them sit close to oxygen: tension released, low energy.
WHERE OUR ENERGY COMES FROMWe cannot turn sunlight into chemical energy the way plants do. So we eat molecules full of nonpolar bonds and turn them into molecules with polar bonds, like CO₂ and H₂O. Changing bonds from nonpolar to polar releases energy.
THINK ABOUT IT
A stable molecule is one with nothing left to give. CO₂ and water are the exhaust: every electron is already pulled toward oxygen.

Where is the fuel in glucose?

We know what makes a bond loaded or spent. Now apply it to the real fuel: label every bond in glucose.

THE FUELLet's look at a molecule of glucoseHere is glucose as a straight chain: six carbons, each with H and OH groups, and a C=O at the top. (In water most glucose closes into a ring, but the bonds are the same.)Two questions from the slide: what types of bonds do you see, and are they polar or nonpolar?
NThe loaded bonds: C–C and C–HEvery carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen bond is nonpolar. Carbon and hydrogen pull about equally, so these are the stretched bands that store glucose's energy.
PThe spent bonds: anything touching oxygenEvery bond to oxygen, C–O, C=O and the O–H inside each OH, is polar. Oxygen already holds those electrons close, so there is little energy left in them.
THE ANSWERAre electrons equally shared?Yes for nonpolar bonds, no for polar bonds. The dots show it: in the middle of every N bond, pulled toward O in every P bond.So glucose is mostly loaded, with some carbon already partly oxidized. The slides call it a sugar with "some reduced carbon". Respiration's job is to turn every N bond into a bond to oxygen.
WHY THIS MATTERS
The more C–H and C–C bonds a molecule has, the more fuel it is. CO₂ has none left. Glucose is somewhere in between: loaded, but not as loaded as methane, because it already has some oxygen.
Which bonds in glucose hold the most potential energy?

The nonpolar ones: C–H and C–C. Their shared electrons sit in the middle, far from both nuclei.

METHANEFour bonds, all stretchedMethane is a carbon with four hydrogens. Carbon and hydrogen pull about equally, so every shared pair sits dead in the middle of its bond, far from both nuclei. Of the molecules on the slide (CH₄, H₂O, NaCl, NH₃, CO₂), it holds the most potential energy.The humming is our picture of all that stored tension: four bands pulled back, waiting to snap.
HOW TO READ THE DOTS
Each dot is one outer electron. Two dots on a bond line are the pair the two atoms share. Two dots beside a letter are a lone pair, kept by that atom. Watch where on the line the shared pair sits: in the middle means an even tug of war, next to O means oxygen is winning.
WHY WE BURN IT: NATURAL GAS
Methane is the main gas in natural gas. On a gas stove, CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Every shared pair moves from the middle of a C–H bond to the oxygen end of a bond, the bands snap, and the energy leaves as heat and light. Glucose is full of the same C–H and C–C bonds.
COMMON MIX-UP
Lewis dots show where electrons are, not the shape. Water is really bent, not a straight line. And the shaking is a picture: the stored energy is in where the electrons sit, not in the motion.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
Natural gas and the stove equation are general chemistry, not from the slides.
NH₃ and NaCl were also on the slide. Why are they lower than CH₄?

N–H bonds are polar: nitrogen pulls the shared pair toward itself. In NaCl, chlorine has taken sodium's electron completely. In both, electrons already sit close to a strong pull.

STEP 9 · USING ENERGY

Nonpolar in, polar out: two fires

Here is redox doing work. Two fires we use, from gentle to explosive. Same chemistry in both.

Example 1 · a gas stove. Natural gas is mostly methane. Before, every shared pair sits in the middle of its bond. After, every pair has moved toward an oxygen. The energy comes from electrons moving out of nonpolar bonds into polar ones.

Example 2 · a rocket. Strip fuel down to hydrogen and oxygen. Same move, nonpolar to polar, but all at once: the energy explodes out in every direction, and the exhaust is just water. This is what fuels a rocket.

WHO WAS OXIDIZED?
In both fires the fuel gives its electrons up to oxygen: the fuel is oxidized (its carbon goes from −4 to +4). Oxygen pulls them in: oxygen is reduced. Burning is one big redox.
GLUCOSE IS FUEL TOO
Glucose is full of the same C–H and C–C bonds as methane. Burn sugar in a flame and you get the same CO₂, water, heat and light.
ADDED FROM OUTSIDE THE COURSE MATERIALS
The stove and rocket examples are general knowledge, not from the slides: the Space Shuttle's main engines burned liquid hydrogen with liquid oxygen; some newer rockets burn methane with oxygen.

But… a cell can't blow up to get its energy.

It has to take fuel apart a little at a time, and catch the energy in small packets as it goes: energy-carrying molecules. That's where the biology begins.
