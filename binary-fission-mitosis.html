DNA, chromosomes and binary fission

THE QUESTION Every cell comes from a cell that already existed. How does one cell become two, each with a complete copy of the DNA?

Five steps: why cells divide, the two jobs of every division, how prokaryotes organize their DNA, binary fission, and how eukaryotes organize theirs.

Why do cells divide?

Every cell comes from a cell that already existed. Cell division is how one cell becomes two, and there are several reasons a living thing needs it.

STEP 2 · TWO JOBS

Copy, then share

Whatever the reason, every cell division has the same two parts. First the cell copies its DNA. Then it hands one copy to each of the two daughter cells.

INTERACTIVE · ONE CELL BECOMES TWO

PART 1 · REPLICATIONCopy the DNAThe cell replicates its DNA: it makes an exact copy of every DNA molecule. The two copies stay joined for now.
PART 2 · SEGREGATIONSend one copy to each daughter cellThe copies are segregated, pulled apart into two daughter cells. Each daughter gets one full copy.
TWO KINDS OF DIVISIONEvery division copies and shares, but the result depends on which kind. Mitosis: 2 cells with the parent's DNA. Meiosis: 4 gametes with different DNA. Compare them just below.
COMMON MIX-UPReplication and segregation are not the same thing. Replication makes the copies. Segregation separates the copies into different cells. A cell that replicated but did not segregate would just have twice the DNA.
Check yourself: What results from mitosis, and how could a mutation change that?

Two daughter cells with the same DNA as the parent. A mutation, a change that happens by chance when the DNA is copied, makes the copies differ slightly, so a daughter cell can carry a new mutation the parent cell did not have.

STEP 4 · PROKARYOTES DIVIDE

Binary fission

Bacteria and archaea divide by binary fission. It has the same two jobs, replication and segregation, and it is the simplest way to do them.

INTERACTIVE · FOUR STAGES

One bacterium becomes two

Tap the buttons, or Next, to click through all 4
1 · BEFOREDNA attached to the membraneThe circular chromosome is held to the inside of the cell membrane by a protein.
2 · REPLICATIONCopying the circleReplication starts at one spot on the circle and runs both ways around it. The result is two circles, each attached to the membrane.
3 · SEGREGATION BY GROWTHThe cell lengthensAs the cell grows longer, the two attachment points move apart, carrying the two circles to opposite ends. Growth itself does the separating.
4 · DIVISIONTwo daughter cellsThe cell pinches in at the middle and new membrane and wall form. Each daughter cell has the same DNA as the parent cell.
WHAT SETS THE SPEED?Binary fission is regulated by nutrient availability. With plenty of nutrients, a bacterium copies its DNA and divides quickly. When nutrients run short, division slows down or stops.
NOT ONLY BACTERIAMitochondria and chloroplasts also divide by binary fission inside your cells, the way the bacteria they came from did (Module 2.2).
Check yourself: Why can a bacterium separate its DNA just by growing, when a eukaryotic cell cannot?

Because a bacterium's DNA is attached to the cell membrane. As the cell grows, the membrane carries the two copies apart. A eukaryotic cell's DNA is inside a nucleus, not on the membrane, so it needs another way to pull the copies apart: mitosis.

STEP 5 · EUKARYOTES

How DNA is organized in eukaryotes

Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular, and their cells have compartments. Their DNA is spread across more than one compartment and more than one chromosome.

Check yourself: Why do mitochondria and chloroplasts contain circular DNA?

Because they were once free-living bacteria. An ancestral cell engulfed them (endosymbiosis), and they kept the circular DNA bacteria have. They still divide by binary fission.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Chromosome

One double-stranded DNA molecule packaged with proteins. It carries many genes. After S phase it is two sister chromatids, but it still counts as one chromosome.

Nuclear DNA

The DNA in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell, organized into linear chromosomes. In eukaryotes, "genome" usually means the nuclear DNA.

Lock it in · Part 1

  1. Cells divide to reproduce (one-celled organisms, asexual reproduction), to grow from one fertilized egg, and to repair and replace tissues.
  2. Every division has two jobs: replicate the DNA, then segregate the copies into two daughter cells.
  3. Mitosis: 2 daughter cells with the same DNA as the parent, except new mutations. Meiosis: 4 gametes with different DNA.
  4. Prokaryotes: no nucleus, one circular chromosome, often plasmids. They divide by binary fission, regulated by nutrient availability.
  5. Eukaryotes: usually several linear chromosomes in the nucleus (the genome). Mitochondria and chloroplasts have circular DNA because they came from bacteria.
  6. Each chromosome is one DNA molecule carrying many genes.

The eukaryotic cell cycle

Check yourself: A cell is in G2. What did each chromosome look like in G1, and what will happen to it next?

In G1 each chromosome was one DNA molecule. S phase copied it, so in G2 it is two sister chromatids. Next comes M phase, where the sisters are pulled apart so each daughter cell gets one.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Interphase

The part of the cell cycle between one M phase and the next: G1, S and G2. The cell grows, copies its DNA and gets ready to divide.

S-phase

The phase of interphase in which DNA is synthesized: every chromosome is replicated into two sister chromatids.

M-phase

The part of the cell cycle in which the cell divides: mitosis followed by cytokinesis.

Cytokinesis

Division of the cytoplasm, which splits one cell into two daughter cells. It follows mitosis.

Lock it in · Part 2

  1. The cell cycle: G1 → S → G2 → M (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase, cytokinesis). Interphase = G1 + S + G2.
  2. G1: grow. S: replicate every chromosome. G2: get ready. M: mitosis, then cytokinesis.
  3. In interphase chromosomes are unspooled and not visible. They condense at the start of mitosis.
  4. S phase: one chromosome stays one chromosome but goes from 1 to 2 DNA molecules: two sister chromatids joined at the centromere.
  5. Count centromeres for chromosomes, chromatids for DNA molecules.
  6. Mutations happen by chance, mostly as copying errors in S phase.

Check yourself: If no mutations have occurred, do two sister chromatids carry the same allele or different alleles of each gene?

The same allele. They were copied from one DNA molecule.

Check yourself: Can you have two different alleles of the same gene in your cells?

Yes. You have one homologous chromosome from each parent, and the two can carry different alleles, such as A from your mother and a from your father.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Homologous chromosome

One of a pair of chromosomes that carry the same set of genes, one inherited from the mother and one from the father. Their DNA is nearly identical, so their alleles can differ.

Allele

One version of a gene. Different alleles have slightly different DNA sequences. A diploid cell has two alleles of every gene, one on each homologous chromosome.

STEP 3 · HAPLOID VS. DIPLOID

One set or two?

A haploid cell (n) has one complete set of chromosomes. A diploid cell (2n) has two complete sets, so its chromosomes come in homologous pairs. To tell them apart, look for pairs.

Check yourself: Can a haploid cell go through mitosis? Can a diploid cell?

Yes, both. Mitosis copies whatever chromosomes a cell has and gives each daughter the same set. A haploid cell makes haploid daughters, and a diploid cell makes diploid daughters.

Check yourself: Are your somatic cells haploid or diploid?

Diploid. Your somatic cells have 23 pairs of chromosomes. Only your gametes, eggs or sperm, are haploid.

KEY TERMS · TAP ONE TO OPEN IT
Haploid

A cell with one complete set of chromosomes (n). Every chromosome carries a different set of genes, so there are no homologous pairs.

Diploid

A cell with two complete sets of chromosomes (2n), so its chromosomes come in homologous pairs. Most human cells are diploid.

THE BIG IDEA

Every cell division copies the DNA, then shares it out. Bacteria do it by binary fission. Eukaryotes copy every chromosome in S phase and split the sister chromatids in mitosis, so each daughter cell gets the same DNA as the parent. Diploid cells carry chromosomes in homologous pairs, one from each parent.

SECTION 1OBJECTIVES 1 AND 2

The eukaryotic cell cycle

  • G1 (gap 1): the cell grows and replicates its organelles. Each chromosome is one DNA molecule.
  • S (DNA synthesis): every chromosome is replicated into two sister chromatids joined at the centromere.
  • G2 (gap 2): the cell keeps growing and prepares to divide. Each chromosome is two sister chromatids.
  • M: mitosis (the nucleus divides), then cytokinesis (the cytoplasm divides). Each daughter starts again in G1.
  • Interphase = G1 + S + G2. Checkpoints make sure the cell is ready before it moves on.
  • In interphase, chromosomes are unspooled so genes can be read, and they are not visible under a microscope. They condense at the start of mitosis.

SECTION 2OBJECTIVE 3

Haploid and diploid

  • Haploid (n): one complete set of chromosomes. Every chromosome carries a different set of genes; there are no pairs.
  • Diploid (2n): two complete sets, so chromosomes come in homologous pairs, one from each parent.
  • How to tell: look for pairs of matching chromosomes (same size, same genes). Replicated sister chromatids are copies, not a pair.
  • A cell with only 2 chromosomes: you can't tell from the number alone. Homologous → diploid (2n = 2). Different genes → haploid (n = 2).
  • Both haploid and diploid cells can go through mitosis. Your somatic cells are diploid; gametes are haploid.
SECTION 3OBJECTIVE 4

Mitosis, drawn out

  • Start (G1): each chromosome unreplicated. After S: each chromosome is two sister chromatids with the same allele.
  • Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible; the mitotic spindle forms from two poles; the nuclear envelope breaks down (the textbook calls that part prometaphase).
  • Metaphase: each replicated chromosome lines up separately in the middle, attached to spindle fibers from both poles.
  • Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles. Each is now a chromosome.
  • Telophase: a nuclear envelope forms around each set. Cytokinesis: the cytoplasm splits into two daughter cells.
  • Result: two daughter cells with the same chromosomes and alleles as the parent cell.

SECTION 4OBJECTIVE 5

Genes and alleles

  • A gene is a stretch of DNA carrying the information for one product. Each chromosome carries many genes.
  • An allele is one version of a gene. Alleles of the same gene have slightly different DNA sequences.
  • Each chromosome has one allele per gene. A diploid cell has 2 alleles of every gene, one on each homolog. They can be the same or different.
  • After S phase, a diploid cell has 4 copies of each gene but still at most 2 alleles, because sister chromatids carry the same allele.
  • Exception: the X and Y sex chromosomes form a pair but carry different sets of genes.
DIFFERENT ALLELESThe long pair in the example cell: A from the mother, a from the father. Same gene, different versions.
THE SAME ALLELEThe short pair: B from the mother and B from the father. Homologs can match. Sister chromatids always match, because one was copied from the other.
SECTION 5OBJECTIVE 6

What mitosis and binary fission are for

  • Why cells divide: reproduction (one-celled organisms, and asexual reproduction in some species), development and growth from a single fertilized egg, and repair and regeneration of tissues.
  • Every division has two parts: replication of the DNA, then segregation of the copies into two daughter cells.
  • Binary fission (bacteria, archaea, and mitochondria and chloroplasts): the circular DNA is attached to the membrane; it is copied, the cell grows so the copies move apart, and the cell pinches in two. It is regulated by nutrient availability.
  • Mitosis (eukaryotes): needed because the DNA is in a nucleus on several linear chromosomes, so the copies must be pulled apart by the spindle.

SECTION 6OBJECTIVE 7

Reading a karyotype

  • A karyotype is a picture of all of a cell's chromosomes, sorted into pairs. It is made during mitosis, when they are condensed and visible.
  • Human somatic cells: 46 chromosomes = 23 pairs: 22 numbered pairs, longest (1) to shortest (22), plus the sex chromosomes, XX or XY.
  • Two copies of each numbered chromosome means human somatic cells are diploid.
  • Before S phase each chromosome (1) has many genes, (2) has one allele per gene, (3) is a single double-stranded DNA molecule.
SECTION 7OBJECTIVE 8

Large numbers of chromosomes

  • The logic doesn't change with the number. A horse cell has 64 chromosomes: 64 DNA molecules in G1, 128 after S, and each daughter cell gets 64 after mitosis.
  • A human cell: 46 chromosomes in G1 (46 DNA molecules), 46 chromosomes but 92 DNA molecules in G2, 46 in each daughter.
  • Mitosis never changes the chromosome number: a cell with 5 replicated chromosomes in G2 gives daughters with 5 each.
SECTION 8OBJECTIVE 9

Cell division in a life cycle

  • Humans: a diploid zygote (fertilized egg) divides by mitosis many times to build every somatic cell.
  • Meiosis in germ cells makes haploid gametes (eggs and sperm). At fertilization two gametes fuse into a new diploid zygote: sexual reproduction.
  • Every somatic cell came from the same zygote, so skin, muscle, heart and liver cells all have the same DNA, except new mutations. They differ in which genes they use.

Lock it in · Part 1

  1. Cells divide to reproduce (one-celled organisms, asexual reproduction), to grow from one fertilized egg, and to repair and replace tissues.
  2. Every division has two jobs: replicate the DNA, then segregate the copies into two daughter cells.
  3. Mitosis: 2 daughter cells with the same DNA as the parent, except new mutations. Meiosis: 4 gametes with different DNA.

Lock it in · Part 3

  1. Mitosis: prophase (condense, spindle forms), metaphase (line up separately in the middle), anaphase (sister chromatids separate), telophase (two nuclei), then cytokinesis.

Guiding questions · Why and how cells divide

These are the questions this module is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q1  Why do cells divide? Give at least 3 reasons.

Reproduction: for one-celled organisms, dividing is making offspring, and some multicellular species reproduce asexually this way.

Development and growth: a single fertilized egg becomes a whole body.

Repair and regeneration: healing wounds and replacing worn-out cells such as skin and blood cells.

Q2  What is the difference between replication of DNA and segregation of DNA into daughter cells?

Replication makes a copy of the DNA. Segregation separates the copies into the two daughter cells. Replication comes first; segregation shares out what was copied.

Q3  What results from mitosis? How does mutation affect the results of mitosis?

Two daughter cells with the same DNA as the parent cell.

A mutation, a change that happens by chance mostly when DNA is copied, can make a daughter cell's DNA differ slightly from the parent's.

Q4  Compare and contrast how DNA is organized in prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic cells. How does the arrangement of DNA in these different cell types affect cell division?

Prokaryotes: no nucleus, one circular chromosome, often plasmids. The DNA is attached to the cell membrane, so the copies separate as the cell grows: binary fission.

Eukaryotes: usually several linear chromosomes inside a nucleus, plus circular DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Because the DNA is in a nucleus, the copies must be pulled apart by the spindle: mitosis.

Q5  What happens during binary fission, and what regulates the speed at which binary fission occurs?

The circular DNA, attached to the membrane by a protein, is replicated. The cell grows longer so the two copies move apart, then it pinches in two, giving two cells with the same DNA.

Its speed is regulated by nutrient availability: fast when nutrients are plentiful, slow when they are scarce.

Q6  Why do mitochondria and chloroplasts contain circular DNA?

Because they descend from bacteria that were engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell (endosymbiosis). They kept bacterial-style circular DNA, and they still divide by binary fission.

Q7  What does the term “genome” usually refer to in eukaryotes?

The nuclear DNA: the full set of chromosomes in the nucleus.

Q10  What events occur in each part of mitosis? What key parts of mitosis do you need to know so you can trace the movement of genetic information between parent and daughter cells?

Prophase: chromosomes condense, the spindle forms, the nuclear envelope breaks down. Metaphase: each replicated chromosome lines up separately in the middle. Anaphase: sister chromatids separate to opposite poles. Telophase: two nuclei form. Then cytokinesis divides the cell.

To trace the genetic information you need three moments: after S (each chromosome is two sister chromatids), metaphase (they line up), and anaphase (the sisters separate, one copy to each daughter).

Q11  What is the difference between a chromosome and a chromatid? How does counting centromeres help you? How many sister chromatids are there in each replicated chromosome?

A chromosome is one DNA molecule with proteins, or, after S phase, two identical ones joined at a centromere. A chromatid is one of those two copies.

Counting centromeres counts chromosomes, whether replicated or not.

Each replicated chromosome has 2 sister chromatids.

Q12  After S-phase but before M phase, how many copies are there of each gene in a cell? How many alleles are there of each gene? What is the difference between a gene and an allele?

In a diploid cell: 4 copies of each gene (two homologs × two sister chromatids), but at most 2 alleles, since sister chromatids carry the same allele.

A gene is a stretch of DNA carrying the information for one product. An allele is one version of that gene.

Q13  Can a haploid cell go through mitosis? Can a diploid cell go through mitosis?

Yes and yes. Mitosis copies whatever set of chromosomes a cell has, so a haploid cell makes haploid daughters and a diploid cell makes diploid daughters.

Q14  How can you tell a haploid cell from a diploid cell?

Look for pairs. A diploid cell has homologous pairs (matching chromosomes carrying the same genes). In a haploid cell every chromosome is different. Sister chromatids are copies and do not count as a pair.

Q15  What is a homologous pair of chromosomes? Where does each chromosome in a pair of homologous chromosomes come from?

Two chromosomes carrying the same set of genes. One was inherited from your mother and the other from your father. Their DNA is nearly, not exactly, identical.

Example: a haploid egg brings a long chromosome with A and a short one with B; a haploid sperm brings a long one with a and a short one with B. The zygote has two homologous pairs: long (A and a) and short (B and B).

Q16  Is it possible for you, in your cells, to have two different alleles of the same gene? If so, how?

Yes. Your two homologous chromosomes came from different parents, so they can carry different alleles of the same gene, for example A from your mother and a from your father.

They can also match: in the same example the short pair is B and B, the same allele. Sister chromatids, unlike homologs, always carry the same allele.

Q17  Are your somatic cells haploid or diploid?

Diploid. 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs.

Q18  Is a cell that only has 2 chromosomes in it haploid or diploid? Or is it impossible to tell?

Impossible to tell from the number alone. If the two are homologs carrying the same genes, it is diploid (2n = 2). If they carry different genes, it is haploid (n = 2).

Q19  How does mitotic cell division in a multicellular organism affect the DNA that is present in different types of somatic cells (e.g. skin cells, muscle cells, heart cells, liver cells, etc.)? Do all cells have the same DNA, or do different cells have different DNA?

Every somatic cell came from the same zygote by mitosis, so they all have the same DNA, except for any new mutations. Skin, muscle, heart and liver cells differ in which genes they use, not in which DNA they have.
