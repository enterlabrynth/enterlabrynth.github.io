PART 1

Endosymbiosis

THE QUESTION The mitochondria powering your cells were once free-living bacteria. How do we know?

The short version: a big cell swallowed a small one, kept it, and the two became one. The seven steps below follow that story in order: who was involved, what happened, what it led to, and how we can be sure.

STEP 1 · THE STARTING LINE

Meet the three cells

Before any of this happened there were no mitochondria and no chloroplasts. There was one large host cell, and there were free-living bacteria. Three cells matter for this story.

TWO KINDS OF CELL

Eukaryotic vs. bacterial

Hold on to circular DNA and binary fissionⓘThe way bacterial cells divide: one cell splits into two. Mitochondria and chloroplasts divide this way too.. They come back as evidence in Step 6.

Those are the three cells. So how did they end up as one?
STEP 2 · THE IDEA

One cell living inside another

Symbiosis is a close interaction between species that has evolved over a long time. Endosymbiosis is a symbiosis in which one species lives inside the other.

THE THEORY OF ENDOSYMBIOSIS Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria. They were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells and became a permanent part of them.

Lynn Margulis championed this theory. Endosymbiosis has been fundamentally important to the origin of eukaryotes.

That is the idea. Here is what actually happened, in order.
STEP 3 · WHAT HAPPENED

Two engulfing events, in order

The host did not start out with mitochondria or chloroplasts. It gained them one at a time, by engulfing a bacterium and keeping it.

THE TIMELINE
ABOUT 2 BILLION YEARS AGO · EVENT 1Host + alpha-proteobacteriumThe alpha-proteobacterium becomes the mitochondrion.
LATER · EVENT 2Host with mitochondria + cyanobacteriumThe cyanobacterium becomes the chloroplast.
TODAYThe descendantsAll eukaryotes descend from the cell that gained mitochondria. Plants and some algae carry chloroplasts from Event 2 as well.
Host cell membrane Nucleus Alpha-proteobacterium, then mitochondrion Cyanobacterium, then chloroplast

The frames are a simplified model. Nobody watched these events happen, which is why Step 6 is about evidence.

EVENT 1 · ABOUT 2 BILLION YEARS AGO

The alpha-proteobacterium moves in

1The host cell, with no mitochondria, meets a free-living alpha-proteobacterium.
2The host engulfs it.
3It is not digested. It survives inside the host.
4Over a very long time it becomes the mitochondrion.
RESULT OF EVENT 1

A eukaryotic cell with mitochondria

The two became obligate mutualists: neither can live without the other. Every eukaryote alive today descends from this cell.

EVENT 2 · LATER

The cyanobacterium moves in

1A descendant of that cell, which already has mitochondria, meets a free-living cyanobacterium.
2The host engulfs it.
3It survives inside the host too.
4Over a very long time it becomes the chloroplast.
RESULT OF EVENT 2

A eukaryotic cell with mitochondria and chloroplasts

This cell can photosynthesize, because its chloroplasts can. Plants and some algae descend from it.

WHY IT LASTED

It was a mutualism

Host cell
protection + resourcesenergy the host can use
Engulfed bacterium

Both sides gained, so the association persisted and became more and more integrated, until it was an obligate mutualismⓘA mutualism at least one species cannot live without..

THE ORDER

Mitochondria came first

Mitochondriathen chloroplasts

How we know: the cyanobacteriumⓘPhotosynthetic bacteria. The group that chloroplasts descended from. was engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria. That is why all eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only photosynthetic ones have chloroplasts as well.

Two events, two new organelles. What did that lead to?
STEP 4 · WHAT IT BECAME

From those two events to you and to plants

Follow the host cell forward. One branch of its descendants stopped after Event 1. Another went through Event 2 as well.

THE STARTThe ancestral host cellA nucleus, but no mitochondria and no chloroplasts.
Event 1 · engulfs an alpha-proteobacterium
AFTER EVENT 1A eukaryotic cell with mitochondriaThe ancestor of every eukaryote alive today.
No second event
TODAY · MITOCHONDRIA ONLYAnimals and fungiYour own cells are here: a nucleus and mitochondria, no chloroplasts.
Event 2 · engulfs a cyanobacterium
TODAY · MITOCHONDRIA AND CHLOROPLASTSPlants and some algaeA plant cell has both: mitochondria from Event 1 and chloroplasts from Event 2.

So the cell with both organelles is the end of the story, not the start. Cyanobacteria themselves are still around too: they are photosynthetic, but they are bacteria, not plants. Other groups of algae got their chloroplasts in a more roundabout way, which is beyond this module.

So what do those two organelles look like today?
STEP 5 · THE TWO ORGANELLES TODAY

What to notice in each one

Both still carry clear signs of where they came from: their own DNA and more than one membrane.

The colours match Step 3. Blue is membrane like the host cell's, on the outside. Pink and green are the inner membrane, the one the engulfed bacterium brought with it. That is the pattern an engulfing event predicts.

That is the story. Nobody watched it happen, so how do we know it is true?
STEP 6 · HOW DO WE KNOW?

Testing the theory: five predictions

Nobody watched any of this happen. So the theory is tested the way any claim about the past is tested: if it is true, what should we still be able to see today?

THE LOGIC OF THIS STEP
1The claim
Mitochondria and chloroplasts used to be free-living bacteria.
2The prediction
Then they should still look, behave and test like bacteria, even though they sit inside a eukaryotic cell.
3The check
Five things to look at. Each one below is a prediction, then what we actually find.
PREDICTION 1 OF 5
1

Size

alpha-proteobacteriummitochondrion≈cyanobacteriumchloroplast≈
IF THEY WERE ONCE BACTERIA, WE WOULD EXPECTAn organelle that used to be a bacterium should be about the size of one.
WHAT WE FINDMitochondria are about the size of alpha-proteobacteria. Chloroplasts are about the size of cyanobacteria.
SOIt fits. On its own this is the weakest clue, since plenty of things are small.
PREDICTION 2 OF 5
2

Their own circular DNA

bacteriummitochondrionchloroplastnucleusCIRCULAR DNAa closed loop, no endsLINEAR DNAstrands with ends
IF THEY WERE ONCE BACTERIA, WE WOULD EXPECTA former bacterium should have brought its own DNA along, packaged the way bacteria package it: as a circle.
WHAT WE FINDEach organelle carries its own genome, separate from the DNA in the nucleus, and it is a circular molecule. The cell's own chromosomes, in the nucleus, are not loops.
SOIt fits. There is bacterial-style DNA sitting inside a eukaryotic cell, and only inside these two organelles.
PREDICTION 3 OF 5
3

Binary fission

a bacteriuma mitochondriona chloroplastonecopies its DNApinches in twotwo
IF THEY WERE ONCE BACTERIA, WE WOULD EXPECTA former bacterium should still reproduce the way bacteria do. Bacteria divide by binary fission. Eukaryotic cells do not: they divide by mitosis and meiosis.
WHAT WE FINDMitochondria and chloroplasts multiply by binary fission. Their DNA is copied, then the organelle pinches in two.
SOIt fits. The cell around them divides one way, and these two parts keep dividing the other way, the bacterial way. They still reproduce like the bacteria they once were.
PREDICTION 4 OF 5
4

Two or more membranes

1The bacterium has its own membrane. It sits against the host's membrane.
2The host's membrane folds inward around it.
3The fold pinches off. The bacterium is inside, wrapped in a bubble of host membrane. That makes two.
Inner membrane: the bacterium's own Outer membrane: from the host
IF THEY WERE ONCE BACTERIA, WE WOULD EXPECTA cell that was engulfed should be wrapped in more than one membrane: its own, plus one from the host that folded around it.
WHAT WE FINDMitochondria and chloroplasts are wrapped in two membranes, or more.
SOIt fits. That is consistent with an engulfing event. The three frames are a simplified model of how it would happen.
PREDICTION 5 OF 5 · THE MOST CONVINCING
5

DNA sequences

an alpha-proteobacteriumMITOCHONDRIAL DNAan alpha-proteobacteriuma cyanobacteriumCHLOROPLAST DNAa cyanobacterium
IF THEY WERE ONCE BACTERIA, WE WOULD EXPECTThis is the sharpest test. If mitochondria descended from alpha-proteobacteria and chloroplasts from cyanobacteria, their DNA sequences should group with those bacteria on a family tree.
WHAT WE FINDThey do. Mitochondrial DNA nests inside the alpha-proteobacteria. Chloroplast DNA nests inside the cyanobacteria.
SOThe first four show a resemblance to bacteria. This one shows descent, and it names the exact groups. That is why it is the most convincing, and why it settled the question.
PUT TOGETHER

Five for five

Size, DNA, division and membranes: four signs that these organelles look and act like bacteria living inside a cell. DNA sequences: which bacteria they came from. Every prediction the theory makes checks out.

One last thing that living together for so long explains.
STEP 7 · AFTER A VERY LONG TIME TOGETHER

Where did the genes go?

Mitochondria and chloroplasts have far fewer genes than the free-living bacteria they came from.

GRAPH · NUMBER OF GENES, NOT TO SCALE

Thousands became dozens

Free-living alpha-proteobacteria and cyanobacteria
Thousands of genes.
Mitochondria and chloroplasts today
Dozens of genes.
SOME MOVEDThey migrated to the nucleus of the eukaryotic host.
OTHERS WERE LOSTThey were not needed in the new environment inside a cell.
TWO THINGS TO KNOW

Loose ends

EUKARYOTES WITHOUT MITOCHONDRIAA few exist. They most likely lost their mitochondria along the way, and they still carry mitochondrial genes in the nuclear genome. So mitochondria still count as a universal feature of eukaryotes.
WHERE THE NUCLEUS CAME FROMNot resolved. How and when the nucleus first originated is still an active area of research.

Lock it in

  1. Mitochondria descended from alpha-proteobacteria. Chloroplasts descended from cyanobacteria.
  2. Mitochondria came first. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria.
  3. Mitochondria do cellular respiration. Chloroplasts do photosynthesis.
  4. Five lines of evidence: size, circular DNA, binary fission, two or more membranes, and DNA sequences.
  5. The most convincing is the DNA sequences: the organelles' DNA nests inside those bacterial groups.
  6. The organelles kept only dozens of genes. The rest moved to the nucleus or were lost.
PART 2

Food webs

THE QUESTION Some cells can make their own food and the rest have to eat. So who eats whom, and what happens to everyone else when one species disappears?

Eight steps, picking up exactly where Part 1 stopped: who makes food, how to read a chain and a web, how effects travel, and why a few species matter far more than the rest.

STEP 1 · FROM PART 1 TO HERE

Two ways to get food

Part 1 ended with two kinds of eukaryotic cell: one with chloroplasts and one without. That one difference decides how an organism gets its food.

HAS CHLOROPLASTSIt can make its own foodPlants and algae photosynthesize. So do cyanobacteria, the bacteria chloroplasts came from. These are autotrophs.
NO CHLOROPLASTSIt has to eatAnimals and fungi get their food from other organisms. These are heterotrophs.
FOUR PREFIXES THAT DECODE THE VOCABULARY
auto-MEANS “self”Makes its own food.
hetero-MEANS “other”Gets its food from other organisms.
photo-MEANS “light”Gets its energy from light.
chemo-MEANS “chemical”Gets its energy from chemicals.
PUT THEM TOGETHER
PHOTO + AUTO + TROPHA photoautotroph uses light to make its own food: a plant, an alga, a cyanobacterium.
CHEMO + HETERO + TROPHA chemoheterotroph gets both its energy and its food by eating: you, an elk, a wolf, a fungus.

Module 2.3 goes further into the different kinds of producer. Here you only need the prefixes.

So some organisms make food and the rest eat. Put them in order.

One chain is tidy. Real communities are not.
STEP 3 · THE REAL THING

A food web: many chains at once

No community is one neat chain. Most organisms eat more than one thing and are eaten by more than one thing. Draw all of it and you have a food web.

DIAGRAM · A SIMPLIFIED YELLOWSTONE FOOD WEB

Read it from the bottom up

BerriesWillowsGrassesAspenMooseElkDeerBeaverInsectsRabbitsSongbirdsWolves
Primary producers Primary consumers Feed on more than one level One food chain inside the web

The highlighted path, willows → beaver → wolves, is one food chain inside the web. You can trace many others.

HOW TO LABEL ANY ORGANISM

Follow its arrows

PRIMARY PRODUCERMakes its own food. No arrow points into it.
PRIMARY CONSUMEREvery arrow into it comes from a producer.
SECONDARY CONSUMEREats primary consumers. A tertiary consumer eats secondary consumers.
TROPHIC OMNIVOREFeeds on two or more trophic levels.
WATCH THIS DEFINITION

Omnivore does not mean “eats plants and animals” here

In everyday use an omnivore eats both plants and animals. In ecology a trophic omnivore is anything that feeds on two or more trophic levels.

Wolves eat elk and beaver (primary consumers) and berries (producers), so wolves are trophic omnivores. Songbirds feed on grasses and on insects, so they are too.

A consumer that eats both primary and secondary consumers is a trophic omnivore even if it never touches a plant. And anything that feeds on a trophic omnivore is classed as one as well.

WHO IS MISSING?

Decomposers

Bacteria, archaea and fungi feed on the dead remains of every level. They are often left out of food web drawings, but they are always there.

Everything in that web is connected. So what happens when one part changes?
STEP 4 · EFFECTS THAT TRAVEL

Direct and indirect effects

Because everything in a web is connected, a change in one species reaches others it never touches.

DIAGRAM · EFFECTS, NOT ENERGY

Foxes help grass without ever touching it

Arctic foxesGeeseLemmingsGrasses−−−−+
Direct effect Indirect effect

These arrows show who affects whom, so they are not food web arrows. Foxes eat geese and lemmings. Geese and lemmings eat grass. More foxes means fewer grazers, which means more grass.

THE TWO KINDS

How to tell them apart

DIRECT EFFECTBetween two species that interact with each other. A wolf eats an elk.
INDIRECT EFFECTBetween two species that do not interact directly. It travels through one or more species in between.
TRY IT ON THE YELLOWSTONE WEB

What affects moose?

WILLOWS AND ASPENDirect. They are what moose eat.
WOLVESDirect. Wolves eat moose.
ELK AND BEAVERIndirect. They eat the same willows and aspen, leaving less for moose.

More than one trophic level shapes the moose population.

Now start that change at the very top.
STEP 5 · FROM THE TOP DOWN

Trophic cascades

When a top predator influences the density of species at every trophic level below it, that is a trophic cascade. Each level down flips direction.

YELLOWSTONE WITHOUT WOLVES
✕Wolves
The top predator is gone.
↑Elk
More elk, grazing wherever they like.
↓Willows and aspen
Eaten down.
YELLOWSTONE WITH WOLVES BACK
↑Wolves
The top predator returns.
↓Elk
Fewer elk, and warier ones.
↑Willows and aspen
They recover.
NOT ONLY NUMBERS

Behaviour counts too

Wolves do not just lower the number of elk. Elk also change how they behave: with wolves around they spend less time feeding in places where they are easy to catch. Less browsing there lets the willows and aspen grow back.

TWO DIAGRAMS, TWO JOBS

A cascade diagram is not a food web

FOOD WEBShows who eats whom. Arrows follow the energy.
CASCADE DIAGRAMShows how abundance changes level by level, starting from the top predator.

If there are trophic omnivores in the web, a cascade is hard to predict. You would need data on how strong the direct and indirect effects are.

Wolves are not the only species with that kind of reach.
STEP 6 · NOT EVERY SPECIES MATTERS EQUALLY

Keystone, dominant and engineer

Three labels for species that shape their whole community. They are easy to mix up, so here they are side by side.

GRAPH · IMPACT VS. HOW MUCH OF IT THERE IS

The difference in one picture

↑ TOTAL IMPACT OF THE SPECIES
KEYSTONEsmall biomass, big impactDOMINANTbig biomass, big impactmost species
PROPORTIONAL BIOMASS OF THE SPECIES →

A dominant species matters because there is so much of it. A keystone species matters far more than its amount would suggest.

KEYSTONE SPECIES

Small in amount, huge in effect

Its effect on the community is out of proportion to its abundance or biomass, largely through indirect effects. Example: the seastar Pisaster in Step 7.

DOMINANT SPECIES

Big effect because it is everywhere

Very common, and it shapes the community as a result. Example: maple trees in many eastern North American forests. Maples are dominant, not keystone.

ECOSYSTEM ENGINEER

It rebuilds the habitat

It creates, maintains, destroys or otherwise substantially alters a habitat. Example: beavers, whose dams turn streams into ponds.

Wolves reshape Yellowstone through who they eat, not by physically changing the habitat, so wolves are not ecosystem engineers.

Here is the experiment that showed what a keystone species is.
STEP 7 · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

The seastar that held a shoreline together

The classic keystone experiment. One predator, removed from one stretch of rocky shore, for more than ten years.

1

The setup

On the rocky intertidal of the Pacific coast, mussels dominate. The seastar Pisaster eats a range of invertebrates, especially mussels, and sets the lower limit of where mussels can live.

MUSSELSThe dominant species. Left alone, they take over the rock.
PISASTERA predator. Far less of it than there is of mussels.
2

Seastars were removed from an experimental site and kept out. A matching site was left alone as the control.

The seastar never touched most of those lost species. By eating mussels it kept space open for them. That is an indirect effect.

WHAT THIS EXAMPLE TEACHES

A keystone species

Pisaster is a small part of the community by biomass, yet removing it changed which species could live there. Its impact is out of proportion to its abundance.

A predator is not the only thing that can remove a species.
STEP 8 · ONE MORE PLAYER

Parasites shape food webs too

Parasites rarely appear in food web drawings, but they can change a community as much as a predator can.

HOW A PARASITE REACHES A WHOLE COMMUNITY

Through its host

Parasitehost does worseeverything the host affects changes

A parasite lowers the fitness of its host. If that host is a keystone species or a top predator, the effects spread through the web by the same indirect routes you saw in Steps 4 to 7.

TRY THIS ONE

A disease is killing seastars along the coast. Based on Step 7, what should happen to the rest of the community?

Check your reasoning
Losing seastars to disease is like the removal experiment, run by a parasite. With fewer seastars, mussels should spread, crowd the rock, and the number of species should fall.
ONE THAT LOOKS LIKE FEEDING BUT IS NOT

The tree shrew and the pitcher plant

A tree shrew feeds on nectar made by a pitcher plant and leaves its droppings in the pitcher. The shrew gets food. The plant gets nutrients. Both benefit, so this is a mutualism, the + / + interaction from Module 2.1.

Quiz

12 questions on endosymbiosis. Pick an answer to see whether it is right and why.

QUESTION 1 OF 12Mitochondria descended from…

Correct. Not quite. Mitochondria were once free-living alpha-proteobacteria.

QUESTION 2 OF 12Chloroplasts descended from…

Correct. Not quite. Chloroplasts were once free-living cyanobacteria, which are photosynthetic bacteria.

QUESTION 3 OF 12Which came first?

Correct. Not quite. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a eukaryotic cell that already had mitochondria. All eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only some have chloroplasts.

QUESTION 4 OF 12Which line of evidence is the most convincing?

Correct. Not quite. Molecular phylogenies place mitochondrial DNA within the alpha-proteobacteria and chloroplast DNA within the cyanobacteria.

QUESTION 5 OF 12How do mitochondria and chloroplasts divide?

Correct. Not quite. Binary fission is how bacteria divide. Eukaryotic cells use mitosis and meiosis.

QUESTION 6 OF 12A mitochondrion has its own DNA, and so does a chloroplast. In both of them, that DNA is organized as…

Correct. Not quite. Both are circular. A mitochondrion and a chloroplast each carry their own genome as a circular DNA molecule, just as alpha-proteobacteria and cyanobacteria do.

QUESTION 7 OF 12Having two or more membranes is consistent with…

Correct. Not quite. An engulfed cell keeps its own membrane and is also wrapped in membrane from the host.

QUESTION 8 OF 12Free-living bacteria have thousands of genes. Why do mitochondria and chloroplasts have only dozens?

Correct. Not quite. Inside a host cell, many genes were no longer needed, and some migrated to the nucleus.

QUESTION 9 OF 12What is the main job of a mitochondrion?

Correct. Not quite. Mitochondria make most of the ATP that powers cellular functions. Chloroplasts do photosynthesis. A mitochondrion does carry a small genome of its own, but that is a clue to its origin, not its job.

QUESTION 10 OF 12A few eukaryotes have no mitochondria. Why are mitochondria still called a universal feature of eukaryotes?

Correct. Not quite. Their ancestors had mitochondria. The leftover genes in the nuclear genome show it.

QUESTION 11 OF 12Endosymbiosis means…

Correct. Not quite. Endo means inside. Symbiosis is a close, long-evolved interaction.

QUESTION 12 OF 12Over time, the engulfed bacterium and its host became…

Correct. Not quite. Both benefited, and eventually neither could do without the other.
