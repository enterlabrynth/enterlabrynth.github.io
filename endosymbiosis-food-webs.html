PART 1

Endosymbiosis

THE QUESTION The mitochondria powering your cells were once free-living bacteria. How do we know?

Six steps: the two kinds of cells, the theory, the two engulfing events, the two organelles, the evidence, and what happened to the genes.

STEP 1 · BACKGROUND YOU NEED

Two kinds of cells

The evidence for endosymbiosis only makes sense once you know how a bacterial cell differs from a eukaryotic cell.

A BACTERIAL CELL

Small and simple

one circular chromosome, no nucleus
INSIDENo nucleus. No mitochondria. No chloroplasts.
DNAMost of it is one circular chromosome.
HOW IT DIVIDESBinary fission, which is unique to bacterial cells.
A EUKARYOTIC CELL

Larger, with compartments

nucleusmitochondrionchloroplast
INSIDEA nucleus and mitochondria. Photosynthetic eukaryotes also have chloroplasts.
DNAChromosomes kept in the nucleus.
HOW IT DIVIDESMitosis and meiosis, not binary fission.
STEP 2 · THE IDEA

One cell living inside another

Symbiosis is a close interaction between species that has evolved over a long time. Endosymbiosis is a symbiosis in which one species lives inside the other.

THE THEORY OF ENDOSYMBIOSIS Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria. They were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells and became a permanent part of them.

The theory is Lynn Margulis's. Endosymbiosis has been fundamentally important to the origin of eukaryotes.

STEP 3 · WHAT HAPPENED

Two engulfing events, in order

It happened twice, with two different kinds of bacteria. The order matters.

Host cell membrane Nucleus Alpha-proteobacterium, then mitochondrion Cyanobacterium, then chloroplast
EVENT 1 · ABOUT 2 BILLION YEARS AGO

The origin of mitochondria

1An ancestral eukaryotic cell meets a free-living alpha-proteobacterium.
2The cell engulfs it.
3It is not digested. It survives inside the host.
4Over time it becomes the mitochondrion.
EVENT 2 · LATER

The origin of chloroplasts

1A eukaryotic cell that already has mitochondria meets a free-living cyanobacterium.
2The cell engulfs it.
3It survives inside the host too.
4Over time it becomes the chloroplast.
WHY IT LASTED

It was a mutualism

Host cell
protection + resourcesenergy the host can use
Engulfed bacterium

Both sides gained, so the association persisted and became more and more integrated. Eventually it was an obligate mutualismⓘA mutualism at least one species cannot live without.: neither can live without the other.

THE ORDER

Mitochondria came first

Mitochondriathen chloroplasts

How we know: the cyanobacteriumⓘPhotosynthetic bacteria. The group that chloroplasts descended from. was engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria. That is why all eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only photosynthetic ones have chloroplasts as well.

STEP 4 · MEET THE TWO ORGANELLES

What to notice in each one

Both still carry clear signs of where they came from: their own DNA and more than one membrane.

MITOCHONDRION

The cell's power supply

outer membraneinner membrane, foldedits own circular DNA
WHAT IT DOESCellular respiration. It makes the ATP that powers cellular functions.
WHERE IT CAME FROMA free-living alpha-proteobacterium.
WHAT TO NOTICETwo membranes, its own circular DNA, and it divides by binary fission.
CHLOROPLAST

The cell's solar panel

outer membraneinner membraneits own circular DNAstacks that capture light
WHAT IT DOESPhotosynthesis. It captures light energy and converts it into chemical energy.
WHERE IT CAME FROMA free-living cyanobacterium.
WHAT TO NOTICETwo or more membranes, its own circular DNA, and it divides by binary fission.

The colours match Step 3. Blue is membrane like the host cell's, on the outside. Pink and green are the inner membrane, the one the engulfed bacterium brought with it. That is the pattern an engulfing event predicts.

STEP 5 · HOW DO WE KNOW?

Five lines of evidence

This is the part to be able to explain. Four of the five say the organelles look and behave like bacteria. The fifth shows they are related to bacteria.

EVIDENCE 1 OF 5
1

Size

alpha-proteobacteriummitochondrion≈cyanobacteriumchloroplast≈

Mitochondria are about the size of alpha-proteobacteriaⓘThe group of bacteria that mitochondria descended from.. Chloroplasts are about the size of cyanobacteria.

EVIDENCE 2 OF 5
2

Their own circular DNA

bacteriummitochondrionchloroplastcircular DNAlinear, in the nucleus

Each has its own genome, organized as a circular DNA molecule, just as alpha-proteobacteria and cyanobacteria do.

EVIDENCE 3 OF 5
3

Binary fission

onepinches in twotwo

Mitochondria and chloroplasts divide by binary fissionⓘThe way bacterial cells divide: one cell splits into two. Mitochondria and chloroplasts divide this way too., the way bacteria do. Eukaryotic cells divide by mitosis and meiosis.

EVIDENCE 4 OF 5
4

Two or more membranes

inner: the engulfed cell's own membraneouter: host membrane that wrapped around it

This is what an engulfing event predicts: the engulfed cell keeps its own membrane and gains a second one from the host that wrapped around it.

EVIDENCE 5 OF 5 · THE MOST CONVINCING
5

DNA sequences

an alpha-proteobacteriumMITOCHONDRIAL DNAan alpha-proteobacteriuma cyanobacteriumCHLOROPLAST DNAa cyanobacterium

When biologists sequenced the genomes of mitochondria and chloroplasts and built family trees from them, mitochondrial DNA nested inside the alpha-proteobacteria and chloroplast DNA nested inside the cyanobacteria. If the organelles descended from those bacteria, their DNA should show it, and it does. This was the conclusive evidence.

STEP 6 · AFTER A VERY LONG TIME TOGETHER

Where did the genes go?

Mitochondria and chloroplasts have far fewer genes than the free-living bacteria they came from.

GRAPH · NUMBER OF GENES, NOT TO SCALE

Thousands became dozens

Free-living alpha-proteobacteria and cyanobacteria
Thousands of genes.
Mitochondria and chloroplasts today
Dozens of genes.
SOME MOVEDThey migrated to the nucleus of the eukaryotic host.
OTHERS WERE LOSTThey were not needed in the new environment inside a cell.
TWO THINGS TO KNOW

Loose ends

EUKARYOTES WITHOUT MITOCHONDRIAA few exist. They most likely lost their mitochondria along the way, and they still carry mitochondrial genes in the nuclear genome. So mitochondria still count as a universal feature of eukaryotes.
WHERE THE NUCLEUS CAME FROMNot resolved. How and when the nucleus first originated is still an active area of research.

Lock it in

  1. Mitochondria descended from alpha-proteobacteria. Chloroplasts descended from cyanobacteria.
  2. Mitochondria came first. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria.
  3. Mitochondria do cellular respiration. Chloroplasts do photosynthesis.
  4. Five lines of evidence: size, circular DNA, binary fission, two or more membranes, and DNA sequences.
  5. The most convincing is the DNA sequences: the organelles' DNA nests inside those bacterial groups.
  6. The organelles kept only dozens of genes. The rest moved to the nucleus or were lost.

Quiz

12 questions on endosymbiosis. Pick an answer to see whether it is right and why.

QUESTION 1 OF 12Mitochondria descended from…

Correct. Not quite. Mitochondria were once free-living alpha-proteobacteria.

QUESTION 2 OF 12Chloroplasts descended from…

Correct. Not quite. Chloroplasts were once free-living cyanobacteria, which are photosynthetic bacteria.

QUESTION 3 OF 12Which came first?

Correct. Not quite. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a eukaryotic cell that already had mitochondria. All eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only some have chloroplasts.

QUESTION 4 OF 12Which line of evidence is the most convincing?

Correct. Not quite. Molecular phylogenies place mitochondrial DNA within the alpha-proteobacteria and chloroplast DNA within the cyanobacteria.

QUESTION 5 OF 12How do mitochondria and chloroplasts divide?

Correct. Not quite. Binary fission is how bacteria divide. Eukaryotic cells use mitosis and meiosis.

QUESTION 6 OF 12How is the DNA inside a mitochondrion or chloroplast organized?

Correct. Not quite. Each has its own genome, organized as a circular DNA molecule.

QUESTION 7 OF 12Having two or more membranes is consistent with…

Correct. Not quite. An engulfed cell keeps its own membrane and is also wrapped in membrane from the host.

QUESTION 8 OF 12Free-living bacteria have thousands of genes. Why do mitochondria and chloroplasts have only dozens?

Correct. Not quite. Inside a host cell, many genes were no longer needed, and some migrated to the nucleus.

QUESTION 9 OF 12What does a mitochondrion do?

Correct. Not quite. Mitochondria make the ATP that powers cellular functions. Chloroplasts do photosynthesis.

QUESTION 10 OF 12A few eukaryotes have no mitochondria. Why are mitochondria still called a universal feature of eukaryotes?

Correct. Not quite. Their ancestors had mitochondria. The leftover genes in the nuclear genome show it.

QUESTION 11 OF 12Endosymbiosis means…

Correct. Not quite. Endo means inside. Symbiosis is a close, long-evolved interaction.

QUESTION 12 OF 12Over time, the engulfed bacterium and its host became…

Correct. Not quite. Both benefited, and eventually neither could do without the other.

Guiding questions · endosymbiosis

These are the questions this part is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q1  Among the eukaryotes, which groups have been influenced by endosymbiosis?
All of them. Mitochondria are a universal feature of eukaryotes.Photosynthetic eukaryotes were shaped by a second endosymbiosis as well, the one that gave them chloroplasts.
Q2  What kinds of organisms are photosynthetic but are not plants?
Cyanobacteria, which are photosynthetic bacteria, and algae, which are photosynthetic eukaryotes that are not plants.
Q3  Which type of organism has DNA most similar to mitochondrial DNA? To chloroplast DNA?
Mitochondrial DNA is most similar to the DNA of alpha-proteobacteria.Chloroplast DNA is most similar to the DNA of cyanobacteria.
Q4  Which came first, the mitochondrion or the chloroplast? How do you know?
The mitochondrion.The cyanobacterium that became the chloroplast was engulfed by a eukaryotic cell that already had mitochondria. All eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only some also have chloroplasts.
Q5  What key pieces of evidence support the theory of endosymbiosis?
1. The organelles are the same size as the bacteria they came from.2. They have their own genome, organized as circular DNA.3. They divide by binary fission.4. They have two or more membranes, consistent with being engulfed.5. Most convincingly, their DNA sequences nest inside the alpha-proteobacteria (mitochondria) and the cyanobacteria (chloroplasts).
Q6  What cellular functions do mitochondria and chloroplasts carry out?
Mitochondria: cellular respiration, making the ATP that powers cellular functions.Chloroplasts: photosynthesis, capturing light energy and converting it into chemical energy.
