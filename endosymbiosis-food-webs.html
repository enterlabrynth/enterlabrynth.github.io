PART 1

Endosymbiosis

THE QUESTION The mitochondria powering your cells were once free-living bacteria. How do we know?

The short version: a big cell swallowed a small one, kept it, and the two became one. The seven steps below follow that story in order: who was involved, what happened, what it led to, and how we can be sure.

AN ANIMAL CELL Mitochondria make most of the ATP that powers the cell.
A PLANT CELL Chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis. Plant cells have mitochondria too.

Both organelles look oddly like bacteria living inside a bigger cell. That is what they once were.

STEP 2 · THE IDEA

One cell living inside another

Symbiosis is a close interaction between species that has evolved over a long time. Endosymbiosis is a symbiosis in which one species lives inside the other.

THE THEORY OF ENDOSYMBIOSIS Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living bacteria. They were engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells and became a permanent part of them.

Lynn Margulis championed this theory. Endosymbiosis has been fundamentally important to the origin of eukaryotes.

That is the story. Nobody watched it happen, so how do we know it is true?

PART 2

Food webs

THE QUESTION Some organisms make their own food and the rest get theirs from other organisms. So who eats whom, and what happens to everyone else when one species disappears?

Eight steps, picking up exactly where Part 1 stopped: who makes food, how to read a chain and a web, how effects travel, and why a few species matter far more than the rest.

STEP 1 · FROM PART 1 TO HERE

Two ways to get food

Part 1 ended with two kinds of eukaryotic cell: one with chloroplasts and one without. Among eukaryotes, that difference goes with two ways of getting food.

HAS CHLOROPLASTSIt can make its own foodPlants and algae photosynthesize with their chloroplasts. Cyanobacteria, the bacteria chloroplasts came from, were photosynthesizing long before and still do. All of these are autotrophs.
NO CHLOROPLASTSIt gets its food from other organismsAnimals and fungi cannot make their own. These are heterotrophs.
FOUR PREFIXES THAT DECODE THE VOCABULARY
auto-MEANS “self”Makes its own food.
hetero-MEANS “other”Gets its food from other organisms.
photo-MEANS “light”Gets its energy from light.
chemo-MEANS “chemical”Gets its energy from chemicals.
PUT THEM TOGETHER
PHOTO + AUTO + TROPHA photoautotroph uses light to make its own food: a plant, an alga, a cyanobacterium.
CHEMO + HETERO + TROPHA chemoheterotroph gets both its energy and its food from other organisms: you, an elk, a wolf, a fungus.

WATCH THIS DEFINITION

Omnivore does not mean “eats plants and animals” here

In everyday use an omnivore eats both plants and animals. In ecology a trophic omnivore is anything that feeds on two or more trophic levels.

A consumer that eats both primary and secondary consumers is a trophic omnivore even if it never touches a plant. And anything that feeds on a trophic omnivore is classed as one as well.

WHO IS MISSING?

Decomposers

Bacteria, archaea and fungi feed on the dead remains of every level. They are often left out of food web drawings, but they are always there.

STEP 4 · EFFECTS THAT TRAVEL

Direct and indirect effects

Because everything in a web is connected, a change in one species reaches others it never touches.

THE TWO KINDS

How to tell them apart

DIRECT EFFECTBetween two species that interact with each other. One eats the other, for example.
INDIRECT EFFECTBetween two species that do not interact directly. It travels through one or more species in between.

The test: is there a species in the middle? If yes, the effect is indirect.

STEP 5 · FROM THE TOP DOWN

Trophic cascades

When a top predator influences the density of species at every trophic level below it, that is a trophic cascade. It is a chain of indirect effects that starts at the top.

YELLOWSTONE WITHOUT WOLVES
✕Wolves
The top predator is gone.
↑Elk
More elk, grazing wherever they like.
↓Willows and aspen
Eaten down.
YELLOWSTONE WITH WOLVES BACK
↑Wolves
The top predator returns.
↓Elk
Fewer elk, and warier ones.
↑Willows and aspen
They recover.

This is the simplified version of the Yellowstone example. In the real park other things matter as well, such as other predators, water and climate, and how large the wolf effect is remains debated.

HOW DO YOU KNOW IT IS A TROPHIC CASCADE?

Three things to check

1It starts at the top
The change begins with a top predator: it arrives, disappears, or changes in number.
2It reaches every level below
Not only the predator's prey, but what the prey eats too.
3The direction usually flips at each level
In a simple chain: predator up, prey down, producers up. Or the reverse.

If the change starts with a producer or a primary consumer, it is not a trophic cascade, even if other species are affected.

Run the three checks

Sea otters disappear. Sea urchins multiply. Kelp forests are grazed away.

Trophic cascade. It starts with a top predator (otters), reaches the producers (kelp), and flips at each level: otters down, urchins up, kelp down.

Not a cascade. The change starts at the bottom, with a producer. Nothing here begins with a top predator.

Not a cascade. Willows do benefit indirectly, but the change starts with a primary consumer, not with the top predator.

Trophic cascade. Top predator, every level below, direction flipping each time.

NOT ONLY NUMBERS

Behaviour counts too

Wolves do not just lower the number of elk. Elk also change how they behave: with wolves around they spend less time feeding in places where they are easy to catch. Less browsing there lets the willows and aspen grow back.

TWO DIAGRAMS, TWO JOBS

THE BIG IDEA

Two engulfing events gave eukaryotes their mitochondria and chloroplasts. Chloroplasts are why plants and algae can capture light energy. A food web traces where that energy goes, and because everything in a web is connected, changing one species can change species it never touches.

SECTION 2

The theory of endosymbiosis

  • Symbiosis: a close interaction between species that has evolved over a long time. Endosymbiosis: a symbiosis in which one species lives inside the other.
  • Mitochondria were once free-living alpha-proteobacteria. They were engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell and became obligate mutualists.
  • Chloroplasts were once free-living cyanobacteria. They were engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell that already had mitochondria.
  • Lynn Margulis championed the theory. The DNA evidence was conclusive.
MITOCHONDRIONCellular respiration. Makes most of the ATP that powers the cell. From an alpha-proteobacterium.
CHLOROPLASTPhotosynthesis. Converts light energy into chemical energy. From a cyanobacterium.
WHICH CAME FIRST?Mitochondria. How you know: all eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only photosynthetic ones have chloroplasts as well. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria.
  • Who was shaped by endosymbiosis? Every eukaryote, through mitochondria. Plants and algae twice, through chloroplasts as well.
  • Photosynthetic but not plants: algae, and cyanobacteria. Cyanobacteria are bacteria, not plants.

SECTION 4

Where the genes went

  • Free-living cyanobacteria and alpha-proteobacteria have thousands of genes. Chloroplasts and mitochondria have dozens.
  • Some genes migrated to the nucleus of the host. Others were lost, because they were not needed inside a cell.
  • A few eukaryotes have no mitochondria. They most likely lost them, and they still carry mitochondrial genes in the nuclear genome. So mitochondria still count as a universal feature of eukaryotes.
  • How and when the nucleus first originated is not resolved. It is an active area of research.

SECTION 6

Direct and indirect effects

DIRECT EFFECTBetween two species that interact with each other. One eats the other, for example.
INDIRECT EFFECTBetween two species that do not interact directly. It passes through one or more species in between.
  • The test: is there a species in the middle? If yes, it is indirect.
  • Sign trick: two negatives in a row make a positive. Wolves lower elk, elk lower willows, so wolves help willows.
  • Moose in Yellowstone: willows and aspen affect them directly (food). Wolves affect them directly (predator). Elk and beaver affect them indirectly, by eating the same plants.
SECTION 7

Trophic cascades

A trophic cascade is when a top predator influences the density of species at every trophic level below it. It is a chain of indirect effects that starts at the top.

WOLVESELKWILLOWS AND ASPEN
Without wolvesGoneUpDown
With wolves backUpDownUp
  • Three checks: it starts at the top, it reaches every level below, and the direction flips at each level.
  • A change that starts with a producer or a primary consumer is not a trophic cascade.
  • Behaviour counts too. With wolves around, elk spend less time feeding where they are easy to catch. Less browsing there lets willows and aspen recover.
  • Cascade diagram vs. food web: a food web shows who eats whom. A cascade diagram shows how abundance changes level by level.
  • Omnivory complicates it. If the web has trophic omnivores, you cannot predict the effect of removing a consumer without data on how strong the direct and indirect effects are.

SECTION 9

Parasites in food webs

  • A parasite lowers the fitness of its host. That changes every species the host affects.
  • If the host is a keystone species or a top predator, the effect spreads through the whole web.
  • Example: sea star wasting syndrome removes seastars, like the removal experiment run by a disease. Expect mussels to spread and the number of species to fall.
  • Not everything that feeds is a predator or parasite. A tree shrew feeds on a pitcher plant's nectar and leaves droppings in the pitcher. The shrew gets food, the plant gets nutrients: a mutualism.
SECTION 10FULL VERSION IN MODULE 2.3

Trophic pyramids and trophic efficiency

  • A trophic pyramid shows the energy in each trophic level. Energy is highest at the base, in the primary producers.
  • On average only about 10% of the energy in one level is transferred to the next. That is what limits the number of trophic levels.
  • Energy can be given in units of mass (kg of carbon) or of energy (kilocalories), because the energy is stored in the organic matter organisms are made of.
EFFICIENCYTHE QUESTION IT ANSWERSTO KNOW
Consumption · CEHow much of the available food is actually eaten?On average 13% of primary production on land, 35% in water.
Assimilation · AEHow much of what is eaten is digested?The rest leaves as waste in feces.
Production · PEHow much of what is digested builds new biomass?Most goes to making ATP by cellular respiration instead.
trophic efficiency = CE × AE × PE
production at the next level = production at this level × trophic efficiency
TWO KINDS OF QUESTIONGiven CE, AE and PE: multiply them, then multiply by the lower level's production. Example: 0.50 × 0.50 × 0.40 = 0.10, so 1,000 units below gives 100 above. Not given them: use the rule of thumb, 10%.
REAL WORLDMore than 80% of land is used for livestock, yet that supplies just 18% of the calories in food. Livestock are primary consumers, so only about 10% of the energy in their feed becomes animal biomass.

  2. Mitochondria came first. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a cell that already had mitochondria.
  3. Mitochondria do cellular respiration. Chloroplasts do photosynthesis.

20 / 21 · PART 2 · LOCK IT IN

Lock it in

  1. Autotrophs make their own food. Heterotrophs get it from other organisms. Photo- means light, chemo- means chemical.
  2. Food web arrows point from the food to the eater, the direction the energy moves.
  3. Count the levels below an organism to name it. One that eats from two or more levels is a trophic omnivore.
  4. Direct effects are between species that interact. Indirect effects pass through a species in between.
  5. A trophic cascade starts with a top predator and reaches every level below, flipping direction each time.
  6. Keystone: impact out of proportion to its biomass. Dominant: big impact because there is so much of it. Engineer: it changes the habitat.

Quiz

32 questions: 12 on endosymbiosis, 16 on food webs, and 4 on the energy terms from the same reading. Pick an answer to see whether it is right and why.

QUESTION 1 OF 32Mitochondria descended from…

Correct. Not quite. Mitochondria were once free-living alpha-proteobacteria.

QUESTION 2 OF 32Chloroplasts descended from…

Correct. Not quite. Chloroplasts were once free-living cyanobacteria, which are photosynthetic bacteria.

QUESTION 3 OF 32Which came first?

Correct. Not quite. The cyanobacterium was engulfed by a eukaryotic cell that already had mitochondria. All eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only some have chloroplasts.

QUESTION 4 OF 32Which line of evidence is the most convincing?

Correct. Not quite. Molecular phylogenies place mitochondrial DNA within the alpha-proteobacteria and chloroplast DNA within the cyanobacteria.

QUESTION 5 OF 32How do mitochondria and chloroplasts divide?

Correct. Not quite. Binary fission is how bacteria divide. Eukaryotic cells use mitosis and meiosis.

QUESTION 6 OF 32A mitochondrion has its own DNA, and so does a chloroplast. In both of them, that DNA is organized as…

Correct. Not quite. Both are circular. A mitochondrion and a chloroplast each carry their own genome as a circular DNA molecule, just as alpha-proteobacteria and cyanobacteria do.

QUESTION 7 OF 32Having two or more membranes is consistent with…

Correct. Not quite. More than one membrane is what you would expect of a cell that was taken inside another cell.

QUESTION 8 OF 32Free-living bacteria have thousands of genes. Why do mitochondria and chloroplasts have only dozens?

Correct. Not quite. Inside a host cell, many genes were no longer needed, and some migrated to the nucleus.

QUESTION 9 OF 32What is the main job of a mitochondrion?

Correct. Not quite. Mitochondria carry out cellular respiration, which makes the ATP that powers cellular functions. Chloroplasts do photosynthesis. A mitochondrion does carry its own DNA, but that is a clue to its origin, not its job.

QUESTION 10 OF 32A few eukaryotes have no mitochondria. Why are mitochondria still called a universal feature of eukaryotes?

Correct. Not quite. Their ancestors had mitochondria. The leftover genes in the nuclear genome show it.

QUESTION 11 OF 32Endosymbiosis means…

Correct. Not quite. Endo means inside. Symbiosis is a close, long-evolved interaction.

QUESTION 12 OF 32Over time, the engulfed bacterium and its host became…

Correct. Not quite. Both benefited, and eventually neither could do without the other.

QUESTION 13 OF 32Auto- in autotroph means…

Correct. Not quite. Auto- means self and hetero- means other. Photo- means light and chemo- means chemical.

QUESTION 14 OF 32In a food web, an arrow runs from grass to elk. What does it show?

Correct. Not quite. Arrows go from the organism being eaten to the organism eating it, the direction the energy moves.

QUESTION 15 OF 32An organism eats only primary producers. It is a…

Correct. Not quite. There is exactly one trophic level below it.

QUESTION 16 OF 32In ecology, a trophic omnivore is an organism that…

Correct. Not quite. A consumer that eats both primary and secondary consumers is a trophic omnivore even if it never eats a plant.

QUESTION 17 OF 32In the Yellowstone web, wolves eat elk and beaver, and also berries. Wolves are best described as…

Correct. Not quite. Elk and beaver are primary consumers and berries are producers, so wolves feed on two trophic levels.

QUESTION 18 OF 32Which group is often left out of food web drawings?

Correct. Not quite. Decomposers feed on the dead remains of every level, yet they are often missing from the drawing.

QUESTION 19 OF 32Arctic foxes eat geese. Geese eat grass. The effect of foxes on grass is…

Correct. Not quite. Foxes never touch the grass. By lowering the geese, they leave more grass.

QUESTION 20 OF 32Elk and moose both eat willows, and neither eats the other. The effect of elk on moose is…

Correct. Not quite. It travels through the willows they both eat.

QUESTION 21 OF 32A trophic cascade starts with a change in…

Correct. Not quite. Then it reaches every trophic level below, flipping direction at each one.

QUESTION 22 OF 32Wolves return to Yellowstone and elk decline. What should happen to willows and aspen?

Correct. Not quite. Fewer elk, and warier elk, means less browsing.

QUESTION 23 OF 32A keystone species is one that…

Correct. Not quite. The seastar Pisaster is the classic example.

QUESTION 24 OF 32Maple trees are very common in many eastern North American forests and shape those forests as a result. Maples are…

Correct. Not quite. Their impact is large because there is so much of them.

QUESTION 25 OF 32Why are beavers ecosystem engineers when wolves are not?

Correct. Not quite. Wolves reshape the community through who they eat, not by rebuilding the habitat.

QUESTION 26 OF 32When seastars were removed from a stretch of rocky shore, the number of species…

Correct. Not quite. The seastar kept space open by eating mussels. Without it, mussels crowded out most other species.

QUESTION 27 OF 32Why does trophic omnivory make it hard to predict what happens when a consumer is removed from a food web?

Correct. Not quite. Omnivory is common, and it increases the number of indirect effects running through the web.

QUESTION 28 OF 32A disease kills most of the seastars on a rocky shore. Based on the removal experiment, what should happen?

Correct. Not quite. Losing the seastars to disease has the same effect as removing them by hand. Parasites and disease can reshape a food web through their hosts.

QUESTION 29 OF 32In a pyramid of energy, where is the amount of energy highest?

Correct. Not quite. The base is much larger than the level above it, and each level above is smaller again.

QUESTION 30 OF 32On average, about how much of the energy in one trophic level is transferred to the next?

Correct. Not quite. About 10%. That is the rule of thumb to use when you are not given the three efficiencies.

QUESTION 31 OF 32Consumption efficiency is 50%, assimilation efficiency is 50% and production efficiency is 40%. What is the trophic efficiency?

Correct. Not quite. Trophic efficiency is the product of the three: 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.10, which is 10%.

QUESTION 32 OF 32Primary producers hold 10,000 units of energy. You are given no efficiencies. About how much production should there be in the primary consumers?

Correct. Not quite. With no efficiencies given, use the rule of thumb: about 10% moves up. 10% of 10,000 is 1,000.

Guiding questions · endosymbiosis

These are the questions this part is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q1  Among the eukaryotes, which groups have been influenced by endosymbiosis?
All of them. Mitochondria are a universal feature of eukaryotes.Photosynthetic eukaryotes were shaped by a second endosymbiosis as well, the one that gave them chloroplasts.
Q2  What kinds of organisms are photosynthetic but are not plants?
Cyanobacteria, which are photosynthetic bacteria, and algae, which are photosynthetic eukaryotes that are not plants.
Q3  Which type of organism has DNA most similar to mitochondrial DNA? To chloroplast DNA?
Mitochondrial DNA is most similar to the DNA of alpha-proteobacteria.Chloroplast DNA is most similar to the DNA of cyanobacteria.
Q4  Which came first, the mitochondrion or the chloroplast? How do you know?
The mitochondrion.The cyanobacterium that became the chloroplast was engulfed by a eukaryotic cell that already had mitochondria. All eukaryotes have mitochondria, but only some also have chloroplasts.
Q5  What key pieces of evidence support the theory of endosymbiosis?
1. The organelles are about the same size as the bacteria they came from.2. They have their own genome, organized as circular DNA.3. They divide by binary fission.4. They have two or more membranes, consistent with being engulfed.5. Most convincingly, their DNA sequences nest inside the alpha-proteobacteria (mitochondria) and the cyanobacteria (chloroplasts).
Q6  What cellular functions do mitochondria and chloroplasts carry out?
Mitochondria: cellular respiration, making the ATP that powers cellular functions.Chloroplasts: photosynthesis, capturing light energy and converting it into chemical energy.

Guiding questions · food webs

These are the questions this part is built to answer. Try each one from memory, then tap it to check.

Q7  What does the direction of an arrow in a food web mean? Which way should arrows point?
Arrows show the flow of energy.They point from the organism being eaten to the organism eating it.
Q8  What do the prefixes photo- and chemo- mean? What do auto- and hetero- mean?
Photo-: light. Chemo-: chemical.Auto-: self. Hetero-: other.
Q9  Which groups of organisms are often missing from food web drawings?
Decomposers: bacteria, archaea and fungi.
Q10  Why are beavers classified as ecosystem engineers, when wolves are not?
An ecosystem engineer creates, maintains, destroys or substantially alters a habitat. Beavers do: their dams turn streams into ponds.Wolves change the community through predation, not by altering the habitat itself.
Q11  How do you assign an organism in a food web to primary producer, or to primary, secondary or tertiary consumer?
Primary producer: makes its own food.Primary consumer: eats producers. Secondary consumer: eats primary consumers. Tertiary consumer: eats secondary consumers.
Q12  How do trophic omnivores differ from primary, secondary and tertiary consumers?
A primary, secondary or tertiary consumer feeds on one trophic level. A trophic omnivore feeds on two or more.Anything that feeds on a trophic omnivore is classed as a trophic omnivore too.
Q13  What are direct and indirect effects, and how do you tell them apart?
A direct effect is between two species that interact with each other.An indirect effect is between two species that do not interact directly. It passes through one or more species in between.
Q14  Describe the effects at least three different organisms in the Yellowstone food web can have on moose.
Willows and aspen: direct. They are the moose's food.Wolves: direct. Wolves eat moose.Elk and beaver: indirect. They eat the same willows and aspen, leaving less for moose.
Q15  How is a trophic cascade different from a change at the producer or primary consumer level? How does a cascade diagram differ from a food web?
A trophic cascade starts with a top predator and influences the density of species at every level below it. A change that starts with a producer or a primary consumer is not a trophic cascade.A food web shows who eats whom. A cascade diagram shows how abundance changes at each level.
Q16  At Yellowstone, how did prey behaviour affect primary producers after wolves were reintroduced?
Elk changed how they behave: with wolves around they spent less time feeding where they were easy to catch.Less browsing in those places let willows and aspen recover. So predators affect prey by killing them and by changing their behaviour.
Q17  Why is the tree shrew and pitcher plant interaction classified as a mutualism?
Both benefit. The shrew gets food from the plant, and the plant gets nutrients from the shrew's droppings.
Q18  In a pyramid of energy, where is energy highest? How does it change from base to top, and what limits the number of trophic levels?
Highest at the base, in the primary producers.It shrinks at every step up: on average only about 10% of the energy in one level reaches the next.That shrinking is what limits the number of trophic levels. Module 2.3 covers this in full.
Q19  Why can energy be measured both in units of mass and in units of energy?
The energy is stored in the organic matter that organisms are made of. So an amount of biomass or carbon, such as kg of carbon, stands for an amount of energy, such as kilocalories.
Q20  How is trophic efficiency calculated? What are consumption, assimilation and production efficiency, and how does cellular respiration come in?
Trophic efficiency = energy at one trophic level ÷ energy at the level below. It equals CE × AE × PE.CE: not all the available food is eaten. On average only about 13% of primary production on land and 35% in water is consumed.AE: not everything eaten is digested. The rest leaves as waste.PE: of what is digested, only a small part builds new tissue.Most of it is used to make ATP by cellular respiration, so it never reaches the next level.
Q21  Why is more than 80% of land used for livestock, when that supplies just 18% of the calories in food?
Livestock are primary consumers. Only about 10% of the energy in the plants they eat ends up as animal biomass.So producing a calorie of meat takes far more land than producing a calorie of plants.
Q22  How do keystone species differ from dominant species? Give examples of each.
A keystone species has an effect out of proportion to its abundance or biomass. Example: the seastar Pisaster.A dominant species is very common and has a large effect for that reason. Examples: maple trees in eastern North American forests, and mussels on the rocky shore.
Q23  What was the design of the Pisaster experiment, and what did the results show?
Seastars were removed from an experimental site on the rocky shore and kept out for over a decade. A site with seastars was the control.Where seastars stayed, the number of species stayed high. Where they were removed, mussels took over and the number of species fell sharply. Pisaster is a keystone species.
Q24  What are some ways parasites can influence food webs?
A parasite lowers the fitness and numbers of its host, which changes every species that host affects.If the host is a keystone species or a top predator, the effect spreads through the web. Example: sea star wasting syndrome removing seastars.
